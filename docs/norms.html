Alberta Electoral Boundary Audit

What the Norms Ask of an Electoral Map

Plain-language companion · English only for now · Not a verdict on anyone

This episode has three lenses. The law asks what is legal. The statistics ask what is unusual. The third lens asks what is customary, and custom is the contested ground. "The government broke a sixty-year norm" is not a neutral measurement. It is the heart of the opposition's accusation. So this page does not just recite the norms. It cross-examines them: where they came from, what they are for, what the case for setting them aside actually is, and what remains once the rhetoric on both sides is cleared away.

The norm, stated plainly

Politicians do not draw their own districts. Canada spent roughly sixty years building that rule, not as a law but as a practice. Since the federal reform era of the 1960s, every province and the federal process have used arm's-length boundary commissions. The person who will run in a district does not hold the pen that draws it.

The norm exists because of what came before it. Governing parties once routinely drew their own ridings, and the conflict of interest is not subtle. A map-drawer who must win under the map has every reason to draw it in his own favour, and no one watching can tell an honest line from a self-serving one. The commission model does not make maps perfect. It makes the drawer's interest separable from the outcome. That is what lets everyone else trust the result.

What the norm bought, in this very cycle

The 2025–26 commission is its own best exhibit. It held hearings in sixteen communities. It received 1,345 written submissions. It published an interim proposal, took criticism, and reversed itself on the strength of eighty-plus submissions. That is the system visibly responding to public input. Whatever else the commission failed at, the process around it worked exactly as designed: in public, on the record, and answerable to what citizens said.

The departure

In April 2026, weeks after the split commission filed two incompatible maps, the Legislative Assembly set both aside. Government Motion 37, passed April 21, handed the work to a committee of six MLAs, four from the governing party, chaired by a government member. The committee is not required to hold public hearings. Per CBC's reporting, it will not hold hearings on the map it produces. No draft will be released for public challenge before the map is final.

In May, Duane Bratt, Professor of Political Science at Mount Royal University, described it to the author as without precedent in Canada. That claim is wrong. In 1992 Alberta set a deadlocked commission's work aside and gave the map to a committee of MLAs; the Opposition refused to take part, and in 1994 the Court of Appeal let the map stand while faulting the committee for being unable to explain several of its boundaries. The Act was amended in 1995. What is new in 2026 is narrower: a commission majority signed a report, which the Act treats as the commission's report, and it was set aside anyway. What it means is where the argument starts.

The case for setting the norm aside — taken seriously

An audit that only rehearses one side's outrage is not checking anything. So take the government's side at full strength. Build its strongest case, the steel-man, before testing it:

  • The commission failed at its one job. The commission model promises a single arm's-length answer. This commission delivered two incompatible maps and a chair publicly accusing colleagues of bad faith. When the independent route deadlocks, someone has to decide. The statute says who.
  • The statute gives the legislature the pen by design. Under Alberta's Act, a commission report has no legal force. Boundaries change only when the Assembly passes an Electoral Divisions Act. Government Motion 37 used a door the law built on purpose. Nothing was smuggled.
  • Elected members answer to voters; commissioners answer to no one. The oldest argument for legislative control is accountability. An MLA who draws a self-serving map can be voted out. A commissioner cannot.
  • The committee is not a purely partisan instrument. Two of its six members are opposition MLAs. The advisory panel that drafts the proposal includes two opposition nominees and is chaired by a retired Court of Appeal justice.
  • The government cited the chair's own recommendation. Its stated justification for the 91-seat mandate was Recommendation 5, written by the commission chair himself. And it told the House exactly that: “We are adopting the recommendations from the report.”

Where the steel-man bends

Each of those points is real. The documented record also pushes back on each:

  • The deadlock explains replacing the maps. It does not explain removing the public. A hearing requirement, a published draft, or a binding panel would have answered the deadlock too. The chosen vehicle forfeits each transparency feature the comparative literature treats as protective. The deadlock forced none of those forfeits.
  • "The statute allows it" is the law lens, and it is answered there: every step was lawful. The norms question is different. Some jurisdictions make a commission's map effective unless the legislature overrides it. Alberta's reversed default means the governing party always controls whether any commission map becomes law. Lawful and customary are different questions.
  • The accountability argument proves too much. It argues against boundary commissions everywhere. Yet every Canadian jurisdiction — and the UK, and Australia — moved the other way, precisely because election-time accountability is weak protection against a map designed to shape those same elections.
  • The panel advises; the MLAs' votes decide. Bipartisan advice with a government-controlled decision is a different institution from an independent commission. The difference is exactly the norm at issue.
  • Recommendation 5's author wrote it to dissuade the legislature from adopting the minority map, and his majority colleagues did not endorse it. It is also specific. It leaves Airdrie, Calgary and everything south of them exactly as the majority drew them, the very lines the commission split over, and nothing in the committee's mandate holds it to that. Citing a recommendation against its author's stated purpose is a documented tension, not a resolved one.
  • The other document read onto the floor — page 58 of the majority report — laments the two-seat cap. But the remedy attached to it is for the Legislature to give future commissions the power to set seat counts. The motion took that latitude now, for a committee of MLAs, while declining the report’s operative request: adopt the 89-seat map.

Whose accusation is it?

Here is the separation this page exists to make. The following are documented facts. The departure happened, for the first time since Alberta's own 1992 committee. The transparency features were forfeited. The commission chair called one minority boundary a "bad faith effort" in a public statutory document. The Canadian Civil Liberties Association publicly raised the politicization concern. The opposition voted against the motion. And the accusations run both ways. The minority report charges the majority with “an inclination to delete or mangle rural electoral divisions and, in effect, reduce effective rural representation” — rural representation being, in the minority's own words, “not merely a question of numbers; it is a question of access, voice, and practical governability” (p. 356). The audit tested that charge the day it surfaced it (exploratory; the hyphen-robust classifier and every classified name are published with the finding). The direction reverses. The minority map keeps fewer rural-anchored divisions than the majority (23 vs 28 against 2019's 31) and absorbs more of 2019's rural divisions into city-named districts (seven vs three). Both maps thin rural representation relative to 2019, so the charge's core observation is real. But on the measure the accusation names, it lands on its author. Its companion charge, that the majority declined to use the Act's ±25% flexibility for rural Alberta, was tested the same day and holds. The majority drew its rural divisions near population parity; the minority drew them light. One charge reversed, one supported, both published. The same one-sided-provenance discount applied to the chair's flags applies here too, in both directions.

The following is not a documented fact. It is a political judgment: that the departure was illegitimate. That judgment depends on how much deference the norm deserved in the face of a deadlocked commission, and reasonable people weigh that differently. The audit documents the departure and its costs. It does not carry the opposition's conclusion, and nothing in the audit's statistics can carry it either. A number can say a map is rare. It cannot say a process was wrong.

Norms are not law — and one place turned the norm into law

Constitutional conventions, the unwritten rules of political behaviour, bind politically, not judicially. The Supreme Court said so in the 1981 Patriation Reference, and courts have held that line since: a court may name a convention and will still decline to enforce it. That gap, between what is customary and what is compellable, is where this entire episode lives.

Quebec shows what the other choice looks like. Its boundary commission is permanent and independent, and on the map itself the commission ordinarily has the last word. When Quebec's government tried to freeze its commission by legislation, the courts did act, because a law was now in play. On April 22, 2026, the Supreme Court of Canada upheld, 7–2 and from the bench, the ruling that the freeze violated the Charter's democratic-representation guarantee. Its reasons followed on May 1 (Quebec (AG) v. Lalande, 2026 SCC 13). Where the norm was written into law, it became enforceable. Alberta's norm never was, and so it wasn't. The contrast cuts both ways, though. On June 12, 2026, Quebec's National Assembly passed its own 127-seat map.

The process scorecard: making the norms question measurable

Rhetoric about norms is cheap in both directions. The audit's answer is a third scorecard, alongside the structural tests and the statistics. It scores the process on nine protective features the sixty-year practice guaranteed. Each is a yes-or-no question answerable from public documents, and the same nine score every process this audit examines.

One more way to see this scorecard: it is the third axis. The audit's chart plots every map on two dimensions: structure and statistics. All three maps plotted so far were drawn by commission processes that score nine of nine here, so the third axis has had nothing to separate. The November map will be the first point in the audit's history with a different process score behind it.

Read the table honestly, in both directions. The commission scores nine of nine by construction. The criteria describe the institution the norm built, and the commission was that institution. The informative part looks forward. Four of the committee's No and Pending cells are features it can still add between now and November: hearings, a published draft, new submissions, and the responsiveness they make possible. If it adds them, the audit's process-departure finding is materially mitigated, and the audit has committed in writing to saying so.

Status matters here: this scorecard's criteria, sources, and predicted directions are registered on the OSF (u47bq, July 14, 2026) — frozen months before the committee reports, so nothing can be quietly reworded when the answer arrives. It was written mid-episode by an author whose earlier findings you have just read — a bias risk the registered document discloses rather than hides. Applied backward to the commission, it is description, not prediction. When the committee's map lands, the same locked map tests run unchanged — and this checklist scores the process that produced it. At that point the norms debate stops being a shouting match about tradition and becomes a scored comparison: did the committee behave like the institution it replaced?

The audit's standing rule

Same rule as everywhere else on this site. The audit documents what happened, how rare it is, and who claimed what. Whether the norm deserved to survive the deadlock — and what its setting-aside should cost at the ballot box, if anything — belongs to readers and voters. Not to a statistical audit.
