Overview

[1]            The petitioner Awad Hussein Mohamed applies pursuant to s. 58 of the Wills, Estates and Succession Act, S.B.C. 2009, c. 13 [WESA] for an order that a draft will prepared by Krista Maria Henriksen’s solicitor be fully effective, even though it does not otherwise comply with that Act.

[2]            Applications for such orders have been brought before. At least one succeeded even though the deceased died before being able to see the final version their solicitor prepared: Fleury Estate (Re), 2025 BCSC 22. But what makes this application unique — at least so far as I am aware and so far as counsel was able to determine — is that in this case the draft was made after the deceased died.

[3]            As in Fleury Estate, the deceased here was consulting with her solicitor when she died suddenly on July 4, 2025. The difference in this case is that because Ms. Henriksen and Mr. Mohamed asked for “mirror wills”, the solicitor provided drafts of the will in his name only, albeit with some annotations showing where her will would be different in some details. During Ms. Henriksen’s lifetime, no draft was ever produced using her name as the testator.

[4]            So while I am convinced — for reasons that I will set out at length — that the draft represents Ms. Henriksen’s fixed and final testamentary intentions, I must first address a legal question. Is it possible to make a s. 58 order for a post-death draft?

[5]            For the reasons that follow, I do not think that is possible. A wide variety of records or documents can be made effective under s. 58, but they must be “authentic”, which I take to mean they must have been created by the deceased or under their direction prior to death.

[6]            However, s. 59 of WESA permits what is called “rectification” of wills and I am persuaded that in this case it is appropriate to make the draft mirror will prepared during Ms. Henriksen’s life effective and then rectify it by substituting Ms. Henriksen’s name for Mr. Mohamed’s and vice versa throughout. In this case, this will accomplish the same result as the order Mr. Mohamed is asking for, but will do so without stretching the statutory framework of s. 58 beyond its breaking point.

Background

[7]            Ms. Henriksen has no children. Her next of kin would either be Mr. Mohamed (if he is legally characterized as a “spouse” within the meaning of WESA) or her siblings. The siblings have been served but have not appeared.

[8]            In light of how I have decided to resolve this application, it is not necessary for me to legally characterize the relationship of Mr. Mohamed and Ms. Henriksen at the time of her death, but that relationship plays a role in understanding the background to the document that is sought to be made effective and so I will set out what the evidence says about it.

[9]            Mr. Mohamed and Ms. Henriksen met in April 1991. They got married in August 1992. They divorced in 2003. However, according to Mr. Mohamed, and other evidence before me, they reconciled within months of their divorce and he deposes they returned to a marriage-like relationship, although they never remarried. They had separate homes, but that was true both before and after the divorce.

[10]         Towards the end of 2024, Ms. Henriksen suggested to Mr. Mohamed that they visit a lawyer to make wills and obtain appropriate decision-making authority for their old age.

[11]         On January 29, 2025, they met with Jessica Poon, an estate planning solicitor at Westcoast Wills and Estates to discuss the preparation of mirror wills, powers of attorney and representation agreements for both of them. At the meeting, Ms. Henriksen did most of the talking and in Ms. Poon’s words, Mr. Mohamed “took a back seat to the conversation”.

[12]         Ms. Poon has provided extensive notes of that conversation, which show that they considered each other to be spouses and that she considered them both to have capacity and not to be subject to undue influence. Her instructions were to draft mirror wills with each being the other’s first executor and with each to obtain the residue of the other’s estate, with contingent beneficiaries after that. A specific gift “for Kristie” was to provide her jewelry to Hanan, Mr. Mohamed’s niece. In addition, they each gave instructions for being buried in accordance with their religious tradition: Muslim in the case of Mr. Mohamed and Catholic in the case of Ms. Henriksen.

[13]         Ms. Poon followed up with an email that evening with a list of information she needed from Mr. Mohamed and Ms. Henriksen. Some email correspondence followed over the course of February.

[14]         On February 24, 2025, Ms. Poon emailed Ms. Henriksen and Mr. Mohamed with a draft Will and a draft Power of Attorney and Representation Agreement. The draft Will was in Mr. Mohamed’s name and the draft Power of Attorney and Representation Agreement was in Ms. Henriksen’s. The text of the email explained:

Because they will be mirror documents (except as detailed below and in the documents), I will finalize the draft for one person before I create the final version of the spouse’s document.

[15]         The text of the February 24 draft will is in Mr. Mohamed’s name but includes a clause 3(b) entitled “Jewelry” with a note “[Kristie’s Will only]” indicating it was clearly only intended for the final will in Ms. Henriksen’s name. The clause provides for delivery of “my” jewelry to Mr. Mohamed’s niece, Hanan, if alive on the date of “my death” and makes the costs of packing, freight and insurance a charge on the estate, which was clearly intended to be Ms. Henriksen’s estate.

[16]         There was some delay until mid-May of 2025 in reviewing the documents because of an unpaid invoice. Ms. Henriksen conducted the correspondence with Ms. Poon and her office. The invoice was paid and there was another meeting on June 4, 2025. On June 25, 2025, Ms. Poon provided Ms. Henriksen and Mr. Mohamed updated drafts based on the revisions discussed in that meeting. As with the February drafts, the draft Will was in Mr. Mohamed’s name and the draft Power of Attorney and Representation Agreement was in Ms. Henriksen’s. I will refer to this document as the “June Draft”.

[17]         In her email delivering the drafts, Ms. Poon asked her clients to schedule a final meeting where the signing was anticipated to take place.

[18]         Sadly, Ms. Henriksen died suddenly on July 4.

[19]         Following her passing, Mr. Mohamed arranged for her funeral. The funeral home asked for a copy of her will. Mr. Mohamed asked Ms. Poon to provide a draft will based on the June Draft but in a mirrored version with Ms. Henriksen identified as the testator and Mr. Mohamed as the designated executor and beneficiary of the residue of the estate.

[20]         Ms. Poon was on vacation when Ms. Henriksen died, so her office prepared that will and provided it to the funeral home. I will refer to this document as the “Post‑Death Draft”. It is this Post-Death Draft that Mr. Mohamed now seeks to be declared to represent Ms. Henriksen’s testamentary intentions and to be ordered to be fully effective as though it had been made in accordance with the other terms of WESA.

Can A Draft Will Created After Death Be Made Effective Under Section 58 of WESA?

[21]         Since this is the first case, so far as I or counsel were able to determine, in which this court has been asked to make an order under s. 58 of WESA for a document created after the death of the purported testator, I must engage in statutory interpretation in order to determine whether this is possible.

[22]         Section 58 states, in relevant part:

Court order curing deficiencies

58(1)    In this section, “record” includes data that

(a)        is recorded or stored electronically,

(b)        can be read by a person, and

(c)        is capable of reproduction in a visible form.

(2)        On application, the court may make an order under subsection (3) if the court determines that a record, document or writing or marking on a will or document represents

(a)        the testamentary intentions of a deceased person, […]

(3)        Even though the making, revocation, alteration or revival of a will does not comply with this Act, the court may, as the circumstances require, order that a record or document or writing or marking on a will or document be fully effective as though it had been made

(a)        as the will or part of the will of the deceased person, […]

[23]         I find that the Post-Death Draft does represent the testamentary intentions of Ms. Henriksen. The interpretative question is therefore whether a “record, document or writing” can include one made after death.

[24]         In any interpretative exercise, the court must look at the statute’s text, its context and its purpose. That all three are part of the interpretative enterprise is universally acknowledged and is sometimes referred to as the “modern approach” to statutory interpretation, although it was arguably well-known to Pufendorf, Coke and Blackstone. But while the importance of all three is undisputed, exactly how they relate has become something of a controversy recently: see Hunt v. Canada, 2026 FCA 88 at paras. 10–13.

[25]         As Justice Stratas argues, it is important to recognize that when judges are interpreting a statute — as opposed to developing the common law or considering the statute’s constitutionality — text must be the “anchor of the interpretative enterprise”: Quebec (Commission des droits de la personne et des droits de la jeunesse) v. Directrice de la protection de la jeunesse du CISSS A, 2024 SCC 43 at para. 24. That is because it is of the very nature of interpretation, as opposed to legislating, that it is about figuring out what the text means. Any discussion of what the law should be that is not anchored to the text is something other than interpretation.

[26]         That does not imply that courts should take a literal approach to statutes that purports not to refer to the context in which the words appear or the purposes for which it could plausibly be said to have been enacted. Realistically, when humans try to understand what another person has said or written, they always use the context in which the speech or writing occurs and they always do so making assumptions of what the speaker or writer wanted to accomplish. The modern principle’s injunction to always consider context and purpose just makes this process conscious and explicit.

[27]         Since a legislature is always trying to accomplish some public policy goal, it is therefore perfectly appropriate to consider what those policy goals might have been in trying to make sense of the words on the page. However, in doing this, the judge is not trying to figure out what they think would best promote those goals, but what the legislature did for that purpose. And it is important to bear in mind that any actual legislation is a compromise between competing purposes: Frank Easterbrook, “Statutes’ Domains” (1983) 50:2 U. Chicago. L. Rev. 533; Mark Mancini, “The Purpose Error in the Modern Approach to Statutory Interpretation” (2022), 59 Alta. L. Rev. 920 at p. 921.

[28]         In the absence of a constitutional challenge, it is not for the courts to replace the compromise the legislator actually struck with one they think works better. Policy considerations “cannot be permitted to distort the actual words of the statute, read harmoniously with the scheme of the statute, its object, and the intention of the Legislature, so as to make the provision say something it does not”: TELUS Communication Inc. v. Wellman, 2019 SCC 19 at para. 79.

[29]         In other words, statutes should not be interpreted literally, but they must be interpreted faithfully.

[30]         Applying these cautions to the question of whether s. 58 can apply to records, documents or writings created after death, it is fair to note that s. 58 does not explicitly limit them to those created during the testator’s lifetime.

[31]         Further, the scope of things that a court can determine to represent the “testamentary intentions of a deceased person” is very broad. In addition to “documents” and “writings”, it includes the defined term “record”, which is defined incredibly broadly.

[32]         An important part of the context of s. 58 of WESA was that it was “a significant reform” and “marked departure” from the pre-existing law, which emphasized formalism and made British Columbia a “strict compliance” jurisdiction: Hadley Estate (Re), 2017 BCCA 311 at paras. 1 and 33. Pre-existing law is useful context, especially where there is clearly a reform, and in this situation points away from a formalist point.

[33]         Moreover we know a lot about the purpose of WESA because it followed from a proposal of the British Columbia Law Institute: Wills, Estates and Succession: A Modern Legal Framework (BCLI Report No. 45, June 2006).

[34]         Chapter III of Part 1 of the BCLI Report discusses the policy goals of reform of British Columbia’s prior legislation, the Wills Act, R.S.B.C. 1996, c. 489 and recommended a broad dispensing power to avoid frustrating the intentions of a testator when the evidentiary functions of statutory formalities were dealt with in some other way.

[35]         Part 2 of the BCLI Report contained draft legislation, s. 46 of which is the model for what is now s. 58 of WESA. The comment under the draft s. 46 stated:

The argument in favour of a curative power is that rigid enforcement of the formal requirements of execution and attestation is often self-defeating. The formal requirements are designed to uphold testamentary intent by ensuring authenticity and guarding against fraud. When there is no question as to authenticity of the will and the formal requirements have been breached due to ignorance or inadvertence, however, the resulting invalidity of the will merely defeats the testator’s last wishes without serving a redeeming protective purpose.

[36]         This indicates a broad purpose of being more flexible in realizing testator intent, although it also includes the sometimes competing purposes of ensuring authenticity and guarding against fraud.

[37]         In Hadley Estate at para. 35, the Court of Appeal emphasized how wide the ability to relieve against formal problems to vindicate the actual intentions of the testator has become in British Columbia:

For an order to be granted under s. 58 of the WESA, the court must be satisfied that the document represents the testamentary intentions of the deceased person. However, unlike the curative provisions in some provinces, s. 58 does not require a minimum level of execution or other formality for a testamentary document to be found fully effective. Regardless of its form, if the court grants an order under s. 58(3), the document may be admitted to probate.

[38]         In para. 37 of Hadley Estate, the Court of Appeal even allowed the possibility that a document at the time of its creation that did not reflect testamentary intentions of the deceased could subsequently come to do so if the deceased by words or actions indicated a fixed and final intention that their property should be disposed of in accordance with the prior-created document.

[39]         So it would appear that text, context, purpose and case law all converge on the principle that what is of principal importance are testamentary intentions, not how they are recorded.

[40]         But notice that s. 58 does not permit a court simply to give effect to what it finds to be the fixed and final testamentary intentions of the deceased. Rather, the court must find a record, document, writing or marking that is to be given effect. If all that mattered was intention, then this would be unnecessary. The court could simply draft the will that best represents the best evidence of what the testator wanted. Section 58 does not allow that.

[41]         This reflects a theme that is also present in the BCLI Report, namely that the traditional formalities of will making fulfilled important functions, even though they sometimes created injustices. The purpose of the legislation was not just to throw away the vestiges of the past, but to do so in a way that maintained the benefits of the older system.

[42]         The most fundamental formality of all was — and remains — that a will must be in writing or at least recorded. This dates back to the very introduction of wills into the English legal system. Even if a court could be morally certain of what the deceased’s intentions were, if they were merely orally expressed, they could not be given effect by a court. That principle was not changed by s. 58, since it does not permit a court to author a will but just to give effect to a record, document or writing.

[43]         In my view, there is no real difference between a court creating a will after death from what it knows of the deceased’s testamentary intentions and a solicitor doing so and then asking for the court to bless the result. If a solicitor could simply, after death, draft a will based on instructions, then all that would matter would be the deceased’s intentions. As a result, in my view, a draft created after death out of the oral instructions of the deceased would not, in the relevant sense, be a record, document, writing or marking.

[44]         My conclusion is bolstered by the case law. In Hadley Estate, the Court of Appeal adopted from the Manitoba Court of Appeal a two-part test. The second part of the test is “whether the non-compliant document represents the deceased’s testamentary intentions”. But the threshold issue is whether the document is “authentic”: Estate of Young, 2015 BCSC 182 at para. 34, as adopted in Hadley Estate at para. 36.

[45]         If the “authenticity” part of the test is to have any independent weight, then it must be possible for a document to fully represent the deceased’s testamentary intentions, but not be “authentic” in the relevant sense. It seems to me that a lawyer’s draft after death would be the canonical example of such a document. An “authentic” document must date to the life of the deceased.

[46]         While policy is not decisive, the compromise the Legislature was trying to arrive at certainly is. If a solicitor could draft what becomes an effective will after death, this would in effect mean that purely oral intentions of the deceased could be the basis for testamentary disposition. This would be a radical break, not just with the formalism of the pre-WESA law in British Columbia, but with the writing requirement that goes back to medieval canon law. Wills were the very first documents that English law required to be in writing, long before the Statute of Frauds required that of land transactions. If the Legislature wanted to effectively reverse that, it would have done so in clear terms. In fact, it did the opposite.

[47]         While the statute does not explicitly say that a record, document or writing must originate before death, by not allowing a judge simply to create a will, it implicitly does so, since there is really no difference between that and giving effect to one drafted by a solicitor after death. As a result, tempting as it would be, I do not think I can give effect to the Post-Death Draft.

Can the Draft in Mr. Mohamed’s Name Be Rectified to Be Ms. Henriksen’s Will?

[48]         That does not leave Mr. Mohamed without a remedy.

[49]         In this case, we do have a draft that was written before Ms. Henriksen died, which she received, and which she apparently reviewed, namely the June Draft. The problem is that it names Mr. Mohamed as the testator and Ms. Henriksen as the executor and residual beneficiary, when obviously the intention for her will was the opposite. The reason for this is explained in Ms. Poon’s affidavit.

[50]         The question therefore arises whether the June Draft can be rectified, so that this is fixed. If it can, then it could be ordered effective without the legal obstacle I have identified for the Post-Death Draft.

[51]         Section 59(1) states as follows:

On application for rectification of a will, the court, sitting as a court of construction or as a court of probate, may order that the will be rectified if the court determines that the will fails to carry out the will-maker's intentions because of

(a)        an error arising from an accidental slip or omission,

(b)        a misunderstanding of the will-maker's instructions, or

(c)        a failure to carry out the will-maker's instructions.

[52]         “Failure to carry out the will-maker’s instructions” need not mean a culpable failure. In my view, this is a relatively easy case for rectification under s. 59(1)(c). The June Draft failed to carry out the will-maker’s intentions because of firm policy in the context of mirror wills still in development. This fits within the meaning of s. 59(1)(c).

[53]         With this rectification, I am persuaded that the June Draft is a document that represents the testamentary intentions of Ms. Henriksen.

Conclusion and Order

[54]         I have therefore declared that the June Draft, as modified in accordance with the detailed order on file, represents the testamentary intentions of Ms. Henriksen and should be made fully effective as though it had been made as her will.

[55]         Reasonable costs of the petitioner can be recovered on a full indemnity basis from the estate.
