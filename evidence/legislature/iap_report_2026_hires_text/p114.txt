    7. The factors noted above are to be used as guidelines rather than definite characteristics for each
    hybrid constituency. Communities of interest may supersede these factors. Similarly, the need to ensure
    effective representation for all Albertans may necessitate at times that a hybrid be used purely to satisfy
    the numerical challenge across the province that a Commission faces."

The independent advisory panel applied these principles to the proposals it made in west Calgary and
in Lethbridge. The panel felt comfortable in doing that because the Chairman of the 2025-2026 Electoral
Boundaries Commission, in his Addendum, indicated that he did not consider the hybridization of Bearspaw
or Springbank with Calgary to be unreasonable. Indeed, the Commission, itself hybridized the electoral
division of Calgary-West by putting a community outside Calgary into it. That community was Elbow Valley
and the area which the Commission incorporated into the electoral division stretched all the way from the
city limits to Highway 22 northeast of Bragg Creek.

The Chairman also indicated that he did not consider the hybridization of Lethbridge to be necessarily
unreasonable or procedurally unfair given the extensive submissions the Commission received about
that hybridization. The Chairman's complaint appeared to relate to other hybrids which this panel is not
recommending, not because they were necessarily unreasonable, but only because the panel did not have
enough time to thoroughly review their merits or otherwise.

The minority's criticism of the panel's proposed hybrids can be summarized in two sentences. The
Commission's hybrids are fine. The advisory panels are not.

The use of the word "hybrid" in the 2025-2026 Electoral Boundaries Commission Report and in this section
of the panel's report assumes the definition that the Commission gave the term; namely an electoral division
which contains all or a part of one of Alberta's 19 or 20 cities and a portion of an adjacent municipality
or country.

A better definition might eliminate a lot of needless argument that might cause an electoral boundaries
commission to focus on the potentially detrimental effect of hybrids, namely that of mixing populations with
different communities of interest. For example, the mixing of urban with rural agrarian populations.

When populations of differing interests are put in the same electoral division, it is argued that it becomes
difficult for the elected representative to represent both populations when their interests' conflict. And, as a
consequence, effective representation is not achieved. Indeed, it is hindered. There are those who say that
such fears are unfounded, including a member of the advisory panel, who was the elected representative of
one of the largest hybrid electoral divisions in Canada for many years.

The hybrids proposed by the advisory panel for Calgary were not hybrids at all according to the foregoing
definition. They were simply electoral divisions mixing suburban city folks with suburban country residential
folks, some of whom live closer to downtown Calgary then a lot of communities within the city. In the case of
all three of the proposed Calgary electoral divisions, the people outside the city have the same interests as
those within the city and they interact with each other on a daily basis.

Where the concern arises is when the hybrid mixes populations of demonstrably different interests. That
was a concern in proposing the Lethbridge hybrids which are true hybrids in that substantial urban and
agrarian populations are recommended to be put in the same electoral division. That is, people with different
interests are being mixed in a single electoral division with a single representative. However, based on the
experience of one of our panel who was the elected representative of such a hybrid electoral division for
about 15 years, we were assured that effective representation can be achieved in these situations if both the
urban and the rural population share the same interests.

But it was more than our panel member's electoral experience that persuaded those of us uncomfortable
with the hybridization to support the proposal. It was also the evidence of what might be called an identifiable,
shared southern Alberta community of interest driven by dependency on the agriculture and food processing
industries stretching from Medicine Hat to the upper reaches of the Waterton and St. Mary Rivers.
