The principle for solving it

The practical approach is to keep compact urban cores modestly above the provincial average and use the
statutory population band where growth demands flexibility. Calgary-Klein, Calgary-Cross and Calgary-
Confluence can remain coherent urban divisions bounded by recognizable features such as Deerfoot Trail,
16 Avenue, the Trans-Canada Highway and the Bow River. Calgary-East is the logical growth-management
division because it lies on the city's edge and can absorb developing communities without fragmenting
stable inner neighbourhoods. Historical boundaries and future growth scenarios should also guide the map
so each new Commission does not have to redraw the area from scratch.

Treating the four northeast Calgary divisions as a single system keeps major communities together, respects
the permitted population range and gives the map room to absorb near-term growth.

The proposed four-division solution

Calgary-Klein, about 62,300. This should be the airport-adjacent urban division. Deerfoot Trail and 16
Avenue provide clear boundaries, creating a stable seat that the next Commission can assess without
starting over.

Calgary-Cross, about 61,800. Whitehorn, Temple, Rundle, Pineridge and Monterey Park form a recognizable
community cluster. They should remain together rather than be split eastward simply to approach the
numerical average.

Calgary-East, about 58,300. This should be the growth-management division. Janet, Starfield, Applewood,
Huxley, Twinhills and Belvedere fit an urban-edge Calgary seat; they should not be joined to Chestermere or
used to disrupt Conrich or established inner northeast communities.

Calgary-Confluence, about 51,000. This division should remain a stabilizing part of the plan. The Bow
River provides a defensible organizing boundary, while the outer boundary helps neighbouring divisions stay
compact and accommodate expected growth.

Some historical context

Northeast Calgary's "Properties" communities--Whitehorn, Temple, Rundle and Pineridge--were developed
in the mid-to-late 1970s north of the Trans-Canada Highway and east of 36 Street NE. They share a
development history, urban form, roads, commercial areas, schools and services.

Together, these neighbourhoods form a coherent electoral community. Keeping them together supports
community-of-interest principles and avoids dividing a recognizable part of northeast Calgary without a
clear boundary rationale.

Monterey Park developed later but is a natural extension of the Properties because of its location and
housing pattern. For electoral purposes, it belongs within the same northeast Calgary community system.
