Irrigation districts in southern Alberta cover areas as large as an electoral division and tie their members
and operators together from Waterton to Medicine Hat. Agricultural research facilities, both federal and
provincial, create far-reaching communities of interest in improving productivity.

Those who suggest there is no unique, shared southern Alberta community of interest that is agriculturally
driven do not understand Lethbridge's role in the region. Those in Lethbridge who oppose hybrids may not
have had experience with them and are understandably apprehensive about what appears to be a radical
and unprecedented change. The proposed hybrids are neither to be feared, nor are they unprecedented
or radical.

The mapping of southern Alberta that the advisory panel proposes is not unprecedented. Alberta already
relies extensively on hybrid electoral divisions. All but two of Alberta's 19 incorporated cities have hybrid
electoral divisions associated with them. Eight of Alberta's 19 cities are parts of large rural-urban electoral
divisions. Nine of Alberta's largest cities, including Calgary and Edmonton, have electoral divisions that
extend beyond boundaries. Only two cities in the Province do not have electoral divisions which extend
beyond their boundaries. Those two cities are Lethbridge and Red Deer and even Red Deer was once
hybridized and will likely have to be hybridized again in the future.

Significantly, the 2025-2026 Electoral Boundaries Commission's Final Report did not oppose hybrids. In fact,
it increased the use of hybrids, including around major cities. The relevant question is therefore not whether
hybrids are permissible or desirable, but whether a proposed hybrid reflects a genuine community of interest
or is needed to achieve population-compliance. Around Lethbridge, the answer to both questions is yes.

Here is what Judge Ernest J. M. Walter, Chair of the 2009-2010 Electoral Boundaries Commission, had to say
about scarcity of population. In the "Issues for Future Consideration" section of the Commission's Report,
Chief Judge Walter said:

       "The Interim Report foreshadowed the type of major changes which future Commissions will
       need to consider and the reaction in central Alberta is indicative of the impassioned responses
       that can be expected.
       It seems likely that outlying electoral divisions may be fewer and larger in the future...
       The Legislative Assembly needs to seriously consider how the urban/rural perspectives will be
       addressed in the future. The Commission believes this is a province-wide issue that will have to
       be addressed."

Justice Myra Bielby, Chair of the 2016-2017 Electoral Boundaries Commission, stated
       "The majority accepts that the time has come to stop treating differences between rural and
       urban Albertans as a main driver in setting the boundaries of electoral divisions. All areas of the
       province are interdependent...."

Justice Bielby also correctly pointed out that the Electoral Boundaries Commission Act makes no reference
to urban interests versus rural interests as a consideration in designing constituency boundaries. She also
observed,

       "The majority has considered that many representers in the areas that currently have blended
       electoral divisions considered them to be working well. In particular, those from Fort McMurray
       and Medicine Hat were satisfied with their blended constituencies."

And, of course, the 2025-2026 Electoral Boundaries Commission devoted an entire Appendix to the issue
of hybrid electoral divisions. The Commission's definition of a "hybrid" electoral division has been of
assistance to the panel, although it fails to distinguish between electoral divisions that simply extend beyond
the boundaries of a city into suburban subdivisions and those in which a portion or all of a city's resident
population is proposed to be put in an electoral division with a significant rural or agrarian resident population.
