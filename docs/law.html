Alberta Electoral Boundary Audit

What the Law Asks of an Electoral Map

Plain-language companion · English only for now · Not legal advice

This audit measures maps. Courts judge them. The two jobs are related but not the same, and this page explains the difference: what Canadian law actually asks of an electoral boundary, which questions belong to a court rather than to a statistician, and where the audit's evidence fits. The audit itself takes no position on any legal conclusion — it reports what the data show and stops there.

Why this audit never rules "gerrymander"

"Gerrymandering" is used throughout the audit in its everyday political sense — manipulating electoral boundaries for partisan advantage. But in Canadian law the word has no legal definition. There is no statute to violate by "gerrymandering" and no court test with that name. A map cannot be found guilty of a category that does not exist, which is one reason the audit refuses to issue that verdict no matter what the numbers say.

The American contrast is worth a moment, because most of this audit's statistical tools — the efficiency gap, neutral map ensembles — were invented for American fights. In the US, partisan gerrymandering is at least a named claim. But the US Supreme Court shut the federal courthouse door in Rucho v. Common Cause, 588 U.S. 684 (2019), holding that federal courts lack "judicially discernible and manageable standards" to judge such claims. The same metrics remain live in some US state courts — most prominently League of Women Voters v. Commonwealth, 178 A.3d 737 (Pa. 2018).

Canada took a different path. Instead of a gerrymandering test, Canadian law asks two answerable questions. Does the map deliver effective representation under section 3 of the Charter? And did the commission comply with its governing statute? The audit's findings are evidence a court could weigh inside those two inquiries — inputs, not a verdict on either. And statistics cannot read minds: boundary geometry can show what a map does, never what its drafters intended.

The constitutional test: effective representation

Section 3 of the Charter of Rights and Freedoms guarantees citizens the right to vote. In Reference re Provincial Electoral Boundaries (Saskatchewan), [1991] 2 SCR 158 — the audit calls it the Saskatchewan Reference — the Supreme Court of Canada held that what s.3 protects in boundary-drawing is not one-person-one-vote arithmetic but effective representation.

Writing for the majority, McLachlin J. explained (at para. 33) that "relative parity of voting power" is the starting point. But it must be weighed against permissible deviations for geography, community history, community interests and minority representation. The Court deliberately set no bright-line tolerance. A deviation from strict population equality is constitutionally defensible if the non-numeric factors justify it.

Two consequences follow, and both cut against overreading this audit's numbers:

  • Partisan asymmetry alone does not make a map unconstitutional. Under the case law reviewed in the monograph, a statistical tilt must be paired with something more — population deviations not justified by geography or community of interest, or evidence the neutral factors were invoked to rationalize a partisan outcome rather than to serve their statutory purpose. The audit's structural tests (population spread, city splits, chair-flagged boundaries) exist precisely because the law asks about that pairing.
  • Courts defer to commissions. The standard is a deferential "reasonable commission" review, not a search for the best possible map. In Raîche v. Canada (Attorney General), 2004 FC 679 — the closest reviewed case to a boundary challenge on community-of-interest grounds — the Federal Court found no Charter s.3 violation. The violations it did find were statutory (the federal boundary statute's community-of-interest duty), and the remedy was referral back to the commission, not boundaries struck down. It is a trial-level decision on the federal statute — persuasive, not binding, for any Alberta challenge.

Where does the audit sit in that framework? Its measurements — how far each map's partisan metrics sit from a million neutral alternatives, how population variance is distributed, where boundaries depart from municipal lines — are inputs a court could weigh in the effective-representation analysis. No metric, and no combination of metrics, is a proxy for the test's outcome. When the audit reports that several indicators point the same way, that is a statement about an empirical pattern, not a legal conclusion.

The statute: Alberta's Electoral Boundaries Commission Act

Alberta's commissions are governed by the Electoral Boundaries Commission Act, RSA 2000, c. E-3 (the EBCA). Three of its rules matter most for reading this audit.

The population band. As a general rule, each proposed electoral division must stay within ±25% of the provincial average population. Both 2026 proposals comply: apart from the three divisions each map protects under the s.15(2) exception described next, every district sits inside the band, and the largest deviation on the high side (+24.06%, in the minority map) comes within a point of the cap without crossing it. Inside that generous band the commission has wide discretion — the audit's population findings (the minority's 48%-wider spread, the NE/central Calgary crowding) all describe choices made within the legal band. Legal and unusual are different questions.

The s.15(2) exception. For at most four divisions, the population may fall as much as 50% below the average — if the commission is of the opinion that at least three of these five criteria exist:

  • area over 20,000 km² (or total surveyed area over 15,000 km²);
  • more than 150 km from the Legislature Building to the division's nearest boundary, by the most direct highway;
  • no town in the division over 8,000 people;
  • the division contains an Indian reserve or a Métis settlement;
  • part of the boundary runs along Alberta's provincial border.

The provision is permissive: meeting the criteria makes a division eligible, but never obliges the commission to use the allowance. Both 2026 maps invoke s.15(2) for three divisions each. The audit re-checked all six invocations under the corrected statutory thresholds and found every one eligible. The controversy the audit documents is not eligibility but a drawing choice: the minority's Rocky Mountain House–Banff Park division picks up one qualifying criterion — the provincial-border one — only by extending through uninhabited national-park land. On that choice, the commission's own final report (March 23, 2026, p. 10) speaks for itself. The majority commissioners write:

"They propose to retain an electoral division of 'Rocky Mountain House-Banff Park' by artificially extending its boundary to the province's western border with British Columbia (taking part of Banff National Park, where no one lives), which is a bad faith effort to ensure it can be protected under s. 15(2) of the Act."

That characterization — including the phrase "bad faith" — is the commission chair's, made in a public statutory document. It is not the audit's. The audit's own analysis found the division qualifies under s.15(2) even without the extension; quoting the chair records what the primary source says, not what any commissioner intended.

What a commission report is — and is not. Under the EBCA, the commission's report has no legal effect by itself. Boundaries change only when the Legislative Assembly passes a separate Electoral Districts Act. Most provinces make a commission's map effective unless the legislature actively overrides it. Alberta's default is reversed — the governing party controls whether any commission map becomes law. Section 12 of the Act also permits referring a commission's recommendations to a legislative committee, with no statutory minimum notice or public-comment period. That is the door the government used on April 16, 2026. It set both commission maps aside and assigned the work to a committee whose majority comes from the governing party, due to report by November 2, 2026. The audit documents that process choice as a finding about procedure — separate from, and additional to, anything the maps' geometry shows.

The full legal analysis — including the case-law review, the commission-deference literature, and the procedural comparison with other provinces — is in the academic monograph, §2 and §5.9, at the audit repository.

The audit's standing rule

Every version of this audit carries the same discipline: it may report what the tests show, and it may say how unusual a map is against a neutral baseline, but it does not declare any map a gerrymander, does not infer intent from geometry, and does not pronounce on constitutional or statutory compliance. Those are questions for courts, commissions, and readers — with the audit's evidence on the table.
