65. The guidance provided by the legislature in Motion 37 could have been more direct on this point. While
     any change may be considered "necessary" from a certain viewpoint for effective representation, we find
     this interpretation of the mandate of this panel to be outcome driven and not supported by the language
     of Motion 37 or the context of the request for our advice. Had this been the legislature's intention, it could
     have stated it much more clearly. Instead, it passed a 42-word sentence. The heart of the sentence is
     incorporating Recommendation 5 and the restoration of the two rural electoral divisions. The
     "modifications [...] consider[ed] necessary" can accordingly be read as part of an instruction to implement
     Recommendation 5 without perfectly strict adherence to Justice Miller's recommendations, rather than a
     standalone ability to advise on changes across the provincial electoral map.

66. What is an example of a modification to Recommendation 5 that is in fact necessary to implement
     Recommendation 5? We have already identified one: interpreting Justice Miller's conditions too strictly
     would have prevented including any portions of MD of Bighorn or Banff National Park in Rocky
     Mountain House-Sundre. This would have impeded the implementation of Recommendation 5 and the
     restoration of Rocky Mountain House-Sundre. Surely, a change to the conditions in Recommendation 5
     can be made to implement Recommendation 5's purpose and goals. In our view, this is exactly what the
     legislature intended when passing Motion 37.

67. We must consider the text, context, and purpose of Motion 37 in interpreting it. As Justice Morley recently
     noted in Henriksen Estate (Re), 2026 BCSC 1058:

[24] In any interpretative exercise, the court must look at the statute's text, its context and its purpose.
That all three are part of the interpretative enterprise is universally acknowledged and is sometimes
referred to as the "modern approach" to statutory interpretation, although it was arguably well-known
to Pufendorf, Coke and Blackstone. But while the importance of all three is undisputed, exactly how
they relate has become something of a controversy recently: see Hunt v. Canada, 2026 FCA 88 at
paras. 10-13.

[25] As Justice Stratas argues, it is important to recognize that when judges are interpreting a statute
-- as opposed to developing the common law or considering the statute's constitutionality -- text
must be the "anchor of the interpretative enterprise": Quebec (Commission des droits de la personne et des
droits de la jeunesse) v. Directrice de la protection de la jeunesse du CISSS A, 2024 SCC 43 at para 24. That is
because it is of the very nature of interpretation, as opposed to legislating, that it is about figuring out
what the text means. Any discussion of what the law should be that is not anchored to the text is
something other than interpretation.
