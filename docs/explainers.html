Alberta Electoral Boundary Audit

The Fourteen Explainers

Appendices A–N · one concept each · English only for now

Each casing in the audit links to one appendix here, and each appendix owns exactly one concept. It starts from the honest confusion. It builds the idea with one concrete example. It sends you back to the casing's number able to read it unaided. And it ends with what the concept cannot tell you. Read them in any order.

Appendix A — What "cracking" actually does to a vote

The confusion: "Airdrie voters still vote. Nobody took their ballots. How does drawing lines 'dilute' anything?"

Imagine a town of 40,000 that mostly votes Orange, sitting in a region that mostly votes Blue. Give the town its own riding, a district with its own seat in the Legislature, and it elects an Orange MLA: 40,000 people, one seat, a voice. Now slice the town into four pieces and staple each piece to a chunk of Blue countryside. In each new riding the town's 10,000 voters face 30,000 rural voters. Orange loses all four. Same voters, same ballots, zero seats. Nothing was stolen from any single voter. What was taken is the community's ability to elect anyone. That is cracking. Its mirror move is packing (Appendix C): stuff a group into as few ridings as possible, so it wins those by landslides and nothing else.

Now re-read finding 1: 85,805 people, four ridings, a minority in three of them. The same commission's other draft shows two ridings were enough to satisfy the law.

The limit: cracking describes an effect, not a motive. A split can happen because someone wanted dilution. It can also happen because other constraints crowded the map. The geometry alone cannot say which.

Appendix B — Shape analysis, and what s.15(2) protection is for

The confusion: "All electoral districts look weird. Why do these three shapes matter?"

Districts follow geography, so lumpy is normal. Shape analysis looks for work: a boundary doing something no ordinary constraint explains. A corridor that lassoes one neighbourhood into a distant district. An arm reaching into a city's edge. A line extended through land where nobody lives. Alberta's law has a special provision, section 15(2), that lets up to four districts fall below the normal population floor. It was written for remote districts with strong community ties, like northern ridings serving Indigenous communities across vast distances. Extending a boundary through uninhabited national park so a district qualifies for that protection is what the commission's own chair called "a bad faith effort": a rule written for people who live far apart, used to protect a line where no one lives at all.

Now re-read finding 2: three shapes, flagged by the process's own chair, independently confirmed by tests that had no stake in his fight.

The limit: a strange shape is a question, not an answer. One of these shapes has a real, creditable logic underneath: the Banff townsite's placement with Canmore. The chair's objection survives it anyway. It was aimed at the empty-park extension, and the placement does not touch that.

Appendix C — Packing, in a three-district toy example

The confusion: "Bigger districts have more voters. So what? Everyone still gets one vote."

Take 12,000 voters, 7,000 Orange and 5,000 Blue, split into three districts of 4,000. Draw them evenly and Orange wins all three. Now redraw. Cram 4,000 Orange voters into one district (Orange wins it 100–0). Spread the remaining 3,000 Orange across two districts of 4,000, where Blue's 5,000 give it the edge in both. Same 12,000 voters, same 7,000 Orange majority. Now Orange holds one seat and Blue holds two. That is packing: your opponents' votes get warehoused where they pile up as surplus, wasted on margins they didn't need. The tell is district size and concentration. Opponents' districts run big and deep. Yours run lean and efficient.

Now re-read finding 3: in the one Calgary zone where 13 of 17 districts vote NDP, the minority's districts run 11.5% over the provincial average. The majority's, drawn in the same room, run 2.8%.

The limit: oversized districts can also come from honest guesses about growth, or from keeping a community whole. Size asymmetry is the signature packing leaves. Several innocent choices leave it too.

Appendix D — What MAD measures, and why the band exists

The confusion: "Population Mean Absolute Deviation — is this just statistics for 'the districts aren't identical'?"

Almost. Districts can't all hold exactly the same number of people; communities come in awkward sizes. MAD asks a plain question: on average, how far does each district stray from the ideal share? A MAD of 3,180 means districts miss the ideal by about 3,180 people on average. A MAD of 4,707 means the misses average half again larger. The law tolerates straying, inside a band (Alberta allows ±25%), because respecting real communities is worth some inequality. That's the bargain. Deviation is the currency, and you spend it to buy things like whole towns and sensible boundaries. That is what gives finding 4 its point: the minority map spends 48% more of that currency than the majority. And finding 1 shows it didn't buy Airdrie's wholeness with it.

Now re-read finding 4: wider spread than 99 of every 100 neutral maps (maps a computer drew without seeing a single vote; Appendix F), on the one value the statute actually quantifies.

The limit: the law ranks nothing inside the band. "Spent more deviation" is an observation about priorities, not a violation. Every district on both maps is legal.

Appendix E — What municipal anchoring measures, and what it can't show

The confusion: "Why count how often a boundary follows a city or town line?"

Because that is where people already know they live. When a district boundary runs along a municipal boundary, the property-tax line, the school-division line, the local ward line and the provincial-election line coincide. A voter can tell which district they are in without a map. The test measures how much of each map's district boundary length runs within 500 metres of a Statistics Canada municipal boundary. Canadian commissions land between 70% and 85% on that measure (Quebec 78%, Ontario 82%, BC 71%). The majority map scores 80%. The minority scores 72%. Both sit inside the norm, so on overall rates the test does not separate them.

Now re-read finding 5: the overall rate is ordinary on both maps. What stands out is where the minority's departures fall: at the edges the chair flagged.

The limit: the anchoring rate is a norm, not a rule. The Act sets no threshold. And a departure can have a good reason, such as a suburb that has grown across a city limit.

Appendix F — The what-if machine, and how to read a percentile

The confusion: "A million computer maps? A percentile of a simulation? What does that even mean?"

You can't judge whether a map is unusual without knowing what usual looks like. For electoral maps, nobody gets to see the alternatives. So the audit built them. An algorithm starts from a legal map and makes a long chain of random legal edits. Merge two neighbouring districts. Re-split them a random valid way. Repeat: 252,500 times over, in four independent chains, 1,010,000 maps in all. No vote data is consulted while drawing. The machine cannot favour anyone because it cannot see anyone, and that is what "neutral" means here. Then you take the real map, measure it, and ask: out of the million neutral maps, how many score this high? If 500,000 do, your map is ordinary. If 69 do, your map is the kind of map neutral drawing produces 69 times in 1,010,000 tries.

Now re-read finding 6: the majority map at the 78th percentile — ordinary. The minority at the 99.99th — 69 in 1,010,000 score higher.

The limit: the machine respects population and contiguity (each district a single connected piece), but not every statutory nicety a real commission weighs. So its percentiles measure distance from neutral drawing, not from everything a lawful commission might do. And a percentile is a distance, not a verdict.

Appendix G — The efficiency gap, in one worked example

The confusion: "One number that captures gerrymandering? That sounds too convenient."

It is, slightly. That is why it gets a threshold, and a miss, in this report. The idea: every vote that doesn't help win a seat is "wasted." That means all votes cast for losers, plus winners' votes beyond the 50%-plus-one they needed. In a fair map both parties waste about equally. The efficiency gap is the difference in wasted votes, as a share of all votes. Suppose Orange wins a 100-vote district 90–10. Orange wasted 39, its surplus beyond 51. Blue wasted 10, all of them votes for the loser. Packed and cracked maps make one party waste systematically more. In this report's sign convention, positive gaps favour conservatives. The audit's Alberta calibration line (~4.1%) is the level only 5% of the million neutral maps cross.

Now re-read finding 7: minority at +4.0%, 94.5th percentile. That is below the audit's own pre-registered line, locked in public in advance, and it is reported as the miss it is.

The limit: the efficiency gap compresses a whole map into one number, and one number can't tell a gerrymander from lumpy geography. That's why this audit uses four metrics and a locked threshold. And it keeps the miss on the books.

Appendix H — The prosecutor's fallacy, in plain language

The confusion: "One in 568,000 that this map is innocent. That's the finding, right?"

No, and the difference matters enough to have a name. The number says this: if a neutral process drew Alberta's map, the chance it would look like the minority map is at most one in 568,000. That is P(this evidence, given innocence). It is not P(innocence, given this evidence). Swapping them is the prosecutor's fallacy. The classic example: a suspect matches a DNA profile found at a scene, and the profile occurs in one in a million people. "One in a million chance he's innocent"? Wrong. In a city of ten million, about ten people match. On the DNA alone, the suspect is one of ten — a 90% chance someone else is the source. The rarity of the evidence under innocence is not the probability of innocence. You also need to know how many innocent ways the evidence could arise.

Now re-read finding 8: the audit states the number it can defend — the rarity of the map under neutral drawing — and refuses the swap that would turn it into a verdict.

The limit: refusing the fallacy costs the audit its most quotable sentence, and that cost is deliberate. Rarity is evidence. It is not, and never becomes, proof of intent.

Appendix I — What a sampler is, and why swapping it matters

The confusion: "You checked your simulation with… another simulation? How is that a check?"

Because the two machines are built differently, and artifacts (results made by a machine's quirks rather than by the map) don't usually survive a change of machinery. Any map-drawing algorithm has quirks. The standard one, ReCom, has a documented lean toward compact, tidy districts, which could in principle exaggerate how extreme a sprawling real map looks. So the audit swapped the engine. The variant grows districts from random spanning forests instead of trees, with measurably different internal behaviour. It was registered publicly (OSF he53s) before a single map was drawn with it, and its seeds were derived from a public randomness beacon anyone can verify. If the minority's tail position were an artifact of the first engine's quirks, the second engine would move it. It moved less than one percentile point on every metric: a pre-registered ROBUST verdict. The audit also reports the check that failed. The claim that Act I's flagged geometry is the specific mechanism behind the seat numbers did not survive testing, and was dropped.

Now re-read finding 9: three attacks, two survived, one claim honestly lost.

The limit: robustness rules out tooling artifacts. It cannot rule in design. A genuinely extreme map is genuinely extreme, whatever produced it.

Appendix J — Why neighbours voting alike breaks naive statistics

The confusion: "A test gave p = 0.0024 and you threw it away? That looks like hiding a result."

The opposite: it's correcting one. Many tests assume observations are independent, like separate coin flips. Voting areas aren't. Neighbouring areas share demographics, incomes, histories. They vote alike. Treating 100 clustered areas as 100 independent flips is really more like 20 informative observations wearing 100 coats, and it makes coincidences look impossible when they're merely uncommon. The fix is to build a null (a picture of what chance alone would produce) that preserves the clustering. Shuffle blocks of neighbouring areas together rather than each area separately, and ask the question again. The audit's swing-zone test looked significant under the naive null (p = 0.0024) and unremarkable under the corrected one (p ≈ 0.19). When the honest null disagrees with the flattering one, you keep the honest null. And you retire the result.

Now re-read finding 10: two of the audit's own instruments retired, one pre-registered test the minority map outright passed.

The limit: the correction shows the boundary-choice signal doesn't stand. It says nothing against the ensemble findings from the computer-drawn maps, which never assumed independence in the first place.

Appendix K — How to check a stated reason against public data

The confusion: "Commissioners are the experts. Who is an audit to grade their reasons?"

Nobody special, and that's the point: the check requires no special access. The minority report says Cochrane residents "move fluidly" into Calgary. Statistics Canada's journey-to-work tables (free, public) show about a third of Cochrane's workers commute into Calgary, while roughly half work in Cochrane. A real tie, and one that says nothing about the specific Nolan Hill corridor drawn. The report implies shared schools link Red Deer and Sylvan Lake. The two towns' school-division boundaries (also public) put them in different divisions. Each check follows the same recipe. Take the stated reason. Find the public dataset that would show it. See whether the particular line drawn is the line the data supports. Five of six checked rationales fail that last step. The tie is real; the line is not the one the tie points to.

Now re-read finding 11. Notice that the audit dropped one of its own seven draft claims by the same standard, when the source couldn't be located.

The limit: a rationale that fails checking proves the stated reason doesn't fit. It cannot reveal the real one. Sloppiness and pretext leave identical paperwork.

Appendix L — How commissioners are appointed, and what nomination implies

The confusion: "Government-nominated commissioners drew the government-favouring map. Case closed, surely?"

Alberta's commission is built from nominations: a chair, plus commissioners nominated from government and opposition sides. The design assumes partisan perspectives and balances them. It bets that a mixed room converges on one defensible map. For decades the bet paid: commissions signed consensus reports. This cycle the room split along its nomination lines, and the government's nominees produced the statistical outlier. That correlation is real and worth recording. It is also exactly where the inference must stop. Nominated commissioners are not agents. No evidence in this audit's record shows the government directed, coordinated with, or even knew the shape of its nominees' map before filing. History is full of nominees who disappointed their nominators. The correlation invites a question. It cannot answer it. And the audit's instruments cut against the chair's side too when the data went that way. That is what symmetric measurement means.

Now re-read finding 12: the split is the anomaly; the provenance, who nominated whom, is the question mark, not the answer.

The limit: appointment provenance is context, never proof. Treating it as proof is the same fallacy as Appendix H, wearing a political coat.

Appendix M — The Act's process, commission to committee, end to end

The confusion: "How can the legislature just… take over redistricting? Isn't the commission's map the map?"

No. This is the load-bearing surprise of the whole episode. Under Alberta's Electoral Boundaries Commission Act, the commission proposes; only the Legislative Assembly disposes. The commission's final report — hearings, submissions, revisions and all — is advice. It has no legal effect until the Assembly enacts a map. And the Act places no constraints on what the Assembly does instead: no minimum debate, no notice period, no obligation to prefer the commission's work, no hearing requirement for whatever process follows. Nothing in the Act stops the Assembly from sending the work elsewhere. That is what Government Motion 37 did, sending it to a select committee. Select committees of the Assembly are composed of MLAs, so the choice of vehicle itself excluded independence. Lawfully. In every cycle since the Act was amended in 1995, the Assembly's restraint, not the statute, was what kept politicians' hands off the pen. In 1992 it did not. A committee of MLAs drew the map, and in 1994 the Court of Appeal faulted that committee for being unable to explain several of its boundaries.

Now re-read finding 13: sixteen communities of hearings replaced by a body required to hold none. All of it inside the Act.

The limit: this appendix explains what the law permits. Whether what the law permits is what Albertans should accept is a political question — the one the November tests are built to inform.

Appendix N — Conventions versus law, and how norms erode

The confusion: "If politicians drawing their own districts violates our deepest electoral norm, how can it be legal?"

Because Canada's constitution runs on two operating systems. One is law: written, justiciable (a court can rule on it), enforced by courts. The other is convention: unwritten rules of political behaviour, binding in practice, invisible to a courtroom. The Supreme Court described the divide in the 1981 Patriation Reference. Courts may recognize a convention and will still decline to enforce it, because conventions are policed politically — by opposition, press, and voters — not judicially. Independent boundary commissions live on the convention side. Sixty years of practice, in every province and federally, built the rule that politicians do not draw their own ridings. No statute anywhere makes that rule mandatory. So the guardrail's entire strength was that governments kept choosing to honour it. A norm violated once, without consequence, is weaker for every government that follows. Of any party.

Now re-read finding 14: real violation, fully documented, judicially irrelevant — all three at once.

The limit: "unenforceable" is a statement about courts, not about consequences. Conventions are enforced at elections, by people who know what was done. Reports like this one exist so they know.
