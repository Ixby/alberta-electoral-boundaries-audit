# Two Paths, One Map: Inside Alberta's 2026 Boundary Audit

*A plain-language look at the 2025–26 Electoral Boundaries Commission, the math behind the minority map, and what comes next.*

## How to read this report

You may have come here looking for proof that a map was rigged. This report does not have it. It cannot have it — and why it cannot is the most important thing the audit found.

Alberta's 2025–26 Electoral Boundaries Commission finished its work on March 23, 2026, and could not agree. Three commissioners signed one map. The other two, the two the government nominated ([Legislative Assembly notice, March 28, 2025](https://www.assembly.ab.ca/about/news-and-announcements/2025/03/28/electoral-boundaries-commission-to-soon-begin-consultations); copy at `evidence/legislature/assembly_news_ebc_appointed_2025-03-28.html`), signed a different one. Both maps meet the *Electoral Boundaries Commission Act*'s numeric rules, though the majority argued the minority's map would likely fail a Charter challenge. Three weeks later, on April 16, the government gave notice of Government Motion 37 (passed April 21), which set both maps aside and handed the job to a committee of six MLAs (members of the Legislative Assembly), most of them from the governing United Conservative Party (UCP). An independent advisory panel working for that committee delivered a new map on October 22. On October 28 the committee voted 3–2 to end its review, adding no observations of its own. The committee gave the panel's report to the Assembly on November 2, 2026.

This audit measured both commission maps, and the process around them, every way it knew how. Five structural comparisons: four written down on April 24, 2026, after a first measurement of both maps on provisional boundaries, and a fifth, the Airdrie split, added the next day. All are dated in this repository. Four partisan-fairness metrics, scored against 1,010,000 neutral maps a computer drew with no stake in who wins. Independent checks of the reasons the commissioners published. A review of the process against sixty years of Canadian practice. What it found is **fifteen findings**: each one real, each one measured, each of which would count for something in a courtroom somewhere, and none of which, alone or together, can legally prove the conclusion they invite. Evidence that a map has a partisan effect is not evidence of who intended it, or why. That gap between evidence and proof is not a loophole the audit failed to close. In Canada it is the system as written. That, too, is a finding.

So this report is built like an evidence log. Each finding faces the same five questions, in the same order, every time:

- **What we found** — the evidence, with its number.
- **What it could show** — the conclusion you will be tempted to draw, stated at full strength.
- **What the experts say** — how this kind of evidence is treated in places that do fight about it in court.
- **What Canadian practice expects** — the norm.
- **Why it isn't proof** — the specific reason, under Canadian law, this evidence proves less than it seems to.

Each of the eight legal lessons is different. By the fifteenth finding you will have learned, one piece at a time, how Canadian boundary law really works. And why every finding in this log ends the same way.

Several entries in the log point the wrong way for the audit's own story, and all are told in full: among them a test that does not tell the maps apart, a test that missed its line, and a test where the minority map beat the majority. A report that never loses an argument with itself isn't checking. Tests that failed, and tests that found nothing, are part of the record.

> **A NOTE ON LEGAL TERMINOLOGY**
>
> "Gerrymandering" has no legal definition in Canadian law. This report uses the word in its everyday political sense: manipulating electoral boundaries for partisan advantage. The legal tests that apply in Canada are different. Do the boundaries provide "effective representation" under s.3 of the *Charter of Rights and Freedoms*, the constitutional standard the Supreme Court of Canada set in the 1991 *Saskatchewan Reference*? And did the commission follow the rules of Alberta's *Electoral Boundaries Commission Act*? The audit's findings are evidence bearing on those legal questions. They are not proof of a legally-defined wrong, and this report does not describe them that way.

> **A NOTE ON "CHANCE"**
>
> Districts are drawn by people making deliberate choices, not at random. None of the numbers in this report assume otherwise. When the report compares a map with a million computer-drawn maps, those maps are *nonpartisan alternatives*: drawn under the same population and connection rules by a procedure that never looks at votes. A result such as "the 99.99th percentile" means a map's partisan effect is rare among maps drawn without regard to party. A result such as "not unusual" means the effect is common among them. Neither says the lines were random, and neither says they weren't deliberate. A choice made for an honest reason (a highway corridor, a community of interest) can still have a partisan effect that nonpartisan drawing produces often. The statistics stop at "common" or "rare". *Why* a line was drawn has to come from the reasons its drawers gave, and from whether they applied those reasons the same way everywhere. That is why this report checks stated reasons against the map itself.

The audit measures the maps. It does not try to read the commissioners' minds, and it does not say either map breaks the law. The author has disclosed a relevant prior: he has voted for and donated to both conservative and progressive provincial parties in Alberta. Going into this project he held the view that the UCP government's handling of boundary redistribution warranted scrutiny. For about 27 years he has also worked as a contract election officer for Elections Alberta, election by election, administering the vote on election day; nothing in this audit comes from that work. Findings that ran against his expectation were kept in the report anyway; the academic report's author disclosure lists three. Anyone who runs the same scripts on the same data gets the same numbers; the audit is built that way. The code is public on GitHub. The random seeds are derived from published values of a public randomness source (drand, run by the League of Entropy) plus a label, so anyone can reproduce them; that alone does not rule out seed choice. The commission-map boundary files were supplied by Elections Alberta on request; they are not posted on its maps page, and are republished in this repository. The rest of the input data is public.

## The Map on the Cover

The cover map is the best single image in this audit. Here is how to read it.

Alberta is divided into 4,765 Voting Areas, the small zones Elections Alberta uses to count the ballots cast at polling stations. Each one is coloured by how the people in it voted in 2023: orange where NDP votes gather, blue where UCP votes gather. But the colour only turns dark and full where a lot of people live. A Voting Area that covers hundreds of square kilometres of parkland or farmland stays pale, nearly invisible. The map lights up where people are, and fades where they aren't.

This is a very different picture from the Alberta you see on election night. Most election maps paint whole ridings, the districts that each elect a member to the Legislature, solid blue or orange by who won. Rural ridings cover a lot of ground and the UCP wins most of them, so election-night Alberta looks like a wall of blue with small orange pockets in Edmonton and Calgary. The cover map uses the same votes and the same geography, but weights them by where people actually live. What appears is a province where most of the people crowd into a dense arc of cities, and those cities vote very differently from the rural map that usually stands for them.

The lines drawn over the colour are the minority commissioners' 89 proposed electoral districts, the map this audit ends up critiquing. The audit's work is to ask what those lines do to the people underneath.

This was the image that made the stakes clear. On a standard map the province looks like it votes one way. Underneath, many of its people live in areas that vote the other way. Once you can see the people under the boundary choices, those choices stop looking random.

## Act I: What the Map Does {.new-page}

Act I walks the lines themselves. No simulation, and almost no election results (only Finding 3 replays 2023 votes): mostly geometry anyone can check against a paper map. The two drafts were drawn in the same room, by five commissioners working from the same rules in the Act, the same provincial geography, the same archive of about 1,345 public submissions, the same demographic data. They agree on most of Alberta's land: 52 of 89 districts overlap by more than half, covering 87% of the province's area, and 29 of them are nearly identical (58% of the area). They part mostly in Calgary and Edmonton, where most people live. Where they part, they part on choices someone in the room had to make.

### Finding 1 — A city of 85,805 with one seat of its own {#finding-1}

![How each map divides Airdrie's people. The majority gives the city two divisions, both mostly Airdrie. The minority gives it one, Airdrie East, with 54.7% of the city, and puts most of the rest in two Calgary-majority divisions.](../../data/maps/article/figure_airdrie_v3.svg)

**What we found.** The minority map splits the City of Airdrie into four pieces. Airdrie has 85,805 people (2024 municipal census), one council, one tax bill, and every civic system treats it as a unit. The law caps each electoral division at one-and-a-quarter times the provincial average, so Airdrie needs at least two divisions. The majority map gives it two. The minority gives it four: north to Olds-Three Hills-Didsbury, east to Airdrie East, west to Calgary-Foothills-Airdrie West, centre-south to Calgary-Airdrie. Airdrie East takes more than half the city, and nine in ten of its people are Airdrie residents. Nearly all the rest of the city is stapled to two Calgary-edge districts, where Calgary residents outnumber Airdrie's. The north piece holds about 350 people. The split clears the first bar of the audit's cracking test (academic report §5.3.2): more pieces than the city's size requires. It fails the second, that Airdrie's residents be outnumbered in every piece, because of Airdrie East. By the test's own rule, that is no cracking signature. The majority's two-way split fails both bars.

**What it could show.** For the part of the city outside Airdrie East, the textbook case of *cracking*: splitting a community so thinly that it decides nothing. An Airdrie resident with a question for her MLA has to know which part of the city she lives in before she can call the right office. The PTA at her child's school cannot send a single delegation to one MLA about school funding. It has to coordinate three delegations to three offices (the fourth piece holds about 350 people), and two of those MLAs answer first to voters outside Airdrie. If you wanted to dilute a fast-growing, politically contested city, this is one way it could look, though by the audit's test it is not a cracking signature.

**What the experts say.** Splitting a community is one of the main red flags in the research on redistricting, because it is one of the two mechanical moves, with packing as its mirror, by which lines turn votes into fewer seats. A four-way split is invisible to every partisan-fairness statistic except the one that asks: can a voter find their MLA?

**What Canadian practice expects.** Alberta's Act lists municipalities among the communities of interest a commission may consider (s. 14(b)), and commissions treat keeping a city or town whole as a strong default. Splitting a city this size in two is common: the 2017 commission split Airdrie two ways. Splitting it four ways, so nearly half its residents are a minority, is not — and the same commission's majority draft proves a two-way split was there to be drawn on the same geography.

**Why it isn't proof.** The *Electoral Boundaries Commission Act* allows municipalities to be split. Community of interest is one factor among several the Act lists, and it is not binding; the *Saskatchewan Reference* tells courts to defer to how boundary bodies weigh those factors. The audit's review found no Canadian decision that struck down a map for splitting a city. Both maps stay inside the Act's population limits (each uses the separate s.15(2) allowance for three remote divisions); the majority argued the minority map would likely fail a Charter challenge (final report, p. 10). The four-way split is a choice, and under Canadian law the choices inside the statute's wide lanes belong to the mapmaker. *(Legal lesson 1: the statute's factors are discretionary, and courts defer.)*

### Finding 2 — The three boundaries the majority called out {#finding-2}

**What we found.** The commission's majority (the chair and two commissioners), appointed under the same Act as the minority and working from the same submissions, flagged four boundaries on the minority map as geographically anomalous. Among them are these three: the Rocky Mountain House–Banff Park extension into national-park land with few residents; the lasso-shaped corridor of Calgary-Nolan Hill–Cochrane; and the reach of Olds–Three Hills–Didsbury into north Airdrie. In the commission's official final report, the majority called the park extension "a bad faith effort" to claim a population protection written for remote communities where people actually live. The majority gave its own map zero such flags. Criticism that runs only against the other side is what you would expect, not an independent verdict between the two maps. What gives the flags weight is that the audit inspected the four named boundaries and confirmed anomalous geometry at three; a check of the majority's four Calgary hybrids, with questions built from the minority's anomalies, found none. (The fourth named boundary, Calgary-Foothills-Airdrie West, did not meet the audit's confirmation threshold; the majority's published criticism covers seven boundary configurations in total.)

**What it could show.** Three commissioners, the chair among them, read the minority's lines and charged in the official report that a legal protection was being gamed. That is as close to direct evidence as this record gets.

**What the experts say.** The study of bizarre shapes is where gerrymandering detection began, and corridors and empty extensions are its classic signatures. In fairness, the Banff arrangement has a defensible geography under the odd name and the odd shape. The town of Banff sits inside a federal park it cannot grow into, and grouping the townsite with Canmore has sound community-of-interest logic. [The Banff section below](#banff) tells that two-sided story in full; it credits what deserves credit. The townsite's placement does not answer the majority's objection, which is aimed at the empty-park extension. The audit's own check does weaken that objection: the park riding qualifies for the s.15(2) protection even without the extension.

**What Canadian practice expects.** Commissioners disagree in their reports all the time. A commission majority accusing colleagues of bad faith in the official final report is another matter. We know of no earlier case, but did not search every report.

**Why it isn't proof.** The majority's words are opinion, inside a report with no legal force. "Bad faith" in a commission report is an accusation, not a finding any tribunal made. No tribunal ever weighed it. Under the Act the majority's report is the commission's report (s.9), and that report is advice, nothing more. The legislature could set both maps aside. It did. *(Legal lesson 2: commission reports bind no one — the legislature holds the pen.)*

[Know more: Appendix B, shape analysis and what s.15(2) protection is for](#appendix-b)

### Finding 3 — One zone of Calgary, drawn too big {#finding-3}

**What we found.** The same zone of Calgary, drawn by the same commission under the same constraints, comes out about 8.4% larger on one map than on the other. In 17 districts across north, east and central Calgary (including the Airdrie and Cochrane hybrids), the minority map's divisions average 11.5% above the provincial average. On the majority map, 2.8%. Which districts count as this zone matters. The audit's list was first written, on April 22, as the "historically NDP-competitive" part of the city against the "UCP-dominant" part, and later described as a geographic rule with the same membership. Under every coding the audit has since tried, the minority's zone runs 8.5% to 11.5% above the provincial average and the majority's 1.3% to 2.8% (`findings/audit_2026-11-10/README.md`). That clears the first bar of the audit's packing test (academic report §5.3.1). It fails the second. Packing means one party's winners pile up huge margins. Replay the 2023 election-day votes on the official boundaries and the NDP wins 8 of these 17 districts, by an average of 15.0 points, against a provincial average of 27.6. That is 12.6 points *below* the average, where the test needs 15 points above (`data/outputs/packing_signature_rescore_2026-11-01.json`). So by the test's own rule there is no packing signature. What stands is a population-equality finding: these districts hold more people, so each vote in them weighs less. The test was exploratory from the start; it was written in the same commit as its first run.

**What it could show.** Oversized districts where one party's voters live are the first mark of *packing*: crowding one party's voters into fewer, larger districts so each of their ballots weighs less. Packing and *cracking* (Finding 1) are the two classic gerrymandering moves. Both shrink a party's seat count below its share of the vote. Here the second mark, lopsided wins, is missing.

**What the experts say.** Packing and cracking are the two moves the US partisan-gerrymandering cases most often describe. Oversized districts exactly where one party's voters gather: that is the pattern the detection research is built to find.

**What Canadian practice expects.** Deviations, how far a district's population strays from the provincial average, are normal inside the legal band, but Canadian commissions back them with community-of-interest reasons. For this zone, the majority report charges the minority with placing "unjustifiable emphasis on 'electors' as opposed to population in northeast Calgary (which is not contemplated by the Act)" (final report, p. 10).

**Why it isn't proof.** Every one of those districts sits inside the Act's ±25% population band, and Canadian law gives no right to sue over partisan *effect*. Section 3 of the Charter protects effective representation of citizens; it does not promise parties that votes will turn into seats in proportion, and no Canadian court has read it that way. *(Legal lesson 3: inside the band, size asymmetry is lawful discretion — partisan effect is not a recognized legal harm.)*

[Know more: Appendix C, packing in a three-district toy example](#appendix-c)

### Finding 4 — Worse at the one thing the law actually measures {#finding-4}

**What we found.** The minority map spreads its district populations much wider than the majority's. The commission's own population tables show it: the Population Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD), which asks how far the average district strays from its ideal share, is 4,707 on the minority map against the majority's 3,180, which is 48% wider. The audit also used a second measure on the official shapefiles, the digital boundary files: the median absolute deviation (MedAD), how far the typical district sits from the middle-sized one (3,938 vs 2,827). Against computer-drawn neutral maps that keep each new district within ±12.5% of equal, the minority's MedAD sits at the 99th percentile: only about 1 in 100 of those maps is more uneven. Against neutral maps allowed the law's full ±25%, both commission maps are more equal than almost all of them. So that percentile depends on how the neutral maps are drawn. And on 2021 census counts, in place of the commission's 2024 estimates, the two maps' average spreads come out almost the same. (Details: academic report §5.4.9 and Appendix C.)

**What it could show.** Equal votes are the one value the statute puts a number on, and this map paid it away. A map that spreads people this much wider than the map drawn beside it is buying something with it, though neutral maps drawn to the law's full band spread people wider still. The common defence — "they had to draw weird boundaries to equalize population" — runs exactly backwards here: the minority map is *worse* at population equality than the majority drawn beside it.

**What the experts say.** Every formal model of redistricting treats equal population as the first constraint. A spread this wide, on a map drawn at the same time as a tighter one, is a cost signal: evidence that other goals outranked the statute's central value.

**What Canadian practice expects.** Canadian commissions treat the deviation band as a limit, not a budget.

**Why it isn't proof.** The ±25% band is the *only* limit the statute sets on how far an ordinary district's population may stray, and no ordinary district on either map crosses it. Three divisions on each map use the separate s.15(2) allowance, which lets up to four remote divisions fall as far as 50% below average. The law does not rank maps inside the band: a 99th-percentile spread and a median spread are, legally, the same. *(Legal lesson 4: the statute sets exactly one limit on population spread, and this map complies with it.)*

[Know more: Appendix D, what MAD measures and why the band exists](#appendix-d)

### Finding 5 — Municipal anchoring: both maps near the 2019 map {#finding-5}

**What we found.** *Anchoring* is the fraction of an electoral border that lies on a pre-existing municipal line: a city, town or county limit, as Statistics Canada's census maps draw it. The majority map anchors at 80% and the minority at 72%. The 2019 map, drawn by the last commission, anchors at 75% on the same measure. This test does not separate the two maps.

**What it could show.** When a district boundary follows a city or town line, the property-tax line and the provincial-election line match (and often the school-division line too), and voters can tell where their district begins and ends. On that measure the two maps look alike.

**What Canadian practice expects.** Nothing. No Canadian statute or practice guide requires any particular anchoring rate. The 2019 map's 75% describes what the last commission did; it does not prescribe anything.

**Why it isn't proof.** It settles nothing either way. Both maps sit close to the 2019 map, and the Act sets no anchoring threshold.

## Act II: What the Simulation Sees {.new-page}

Act I needed nothing but the lines. Act II asks a harder question: how unusual are these maps, next to what a process with no agenda would draw? To answer it, the audit had a computer draw 1,010,000 Alberta maps with no stake in who wins (4 independent chains × 252,500 steps, base seed derived from a published value of the drand public randomness beacon run by the League of Entropy). It drew them from the same 4,765 voting areas, keeping each new district within ±12.5% of equal (the Act allows ±25%) and every district in one piece; the official Elections Alberta shapefiles place the two commission maps among them. Think of the simulation as a "what-if machine": what would Alberta's maps look like if nobody was trying to do anything? Set a real map among them and its percentile, the share of neutral maps it outscores, tells you how far out into the tail it sits. A note on seat counts: the 2026 commission maps each have **89** districts; the simulation's neutral maps have **87**, the 2019 map's count; the panel's new map has **91**. All percentages are seat *shares*, which puts the three sizes on one scale, though not perfectly. A size-matched check (October 30, provisional) finds the same tilt; a size-matched joint number has not been reported.

One fact about Alberta makes every number below readable. In this province, neutral is not 50/50. Across the 1,010,000 neutral maps, the median map, the middle of the pile, gives the UCP only 44.8% of seats when the province-wide vote splits exactly even. The reason is geography: rural UCP voters win their ridings by wide margins (about 70-30 on average), wasting many "extra" votes, while urban NDP voters win theirs by narrower ones (about 60-40) and waste fewer. Left alone, Alberta's natural political geography mildly favours the NDP in seats at a tied vote. Not on every measure, though: on the efficiency gap (Finding 7), most neutral maps (85%) lean slightly toward the UCP, with a middle value near +1.6%. Keep that baseline in mind: it is what the maps below are measured against.

### Finding 6 — Fewer than 100 maps in 1,010,000 {#finding-6}

**What we found.** Split the vote dead even, 50/50, and ask each map how many seats it hands the UCP:

| Map | UCP seats at 50/50 votes | Where it sits |
|---|---|---|
| 2019 enacted | 46.0% | 65th percentile (it ties about a quarter of the neutral maps; 78th if every tie counts below it) — inside the normal range |
| **Majority 2026** | **46.1%** | **78th percentile — well within bounds** |
| **Minority 2026** | **51.7% (46 seats)** | **99.99th percentile — fewer than 100 of 1,010,000 neutral draws reach this** |

The full neutral spread: median 44.8%, 95th percentile 48.3%, 99th percentile 49.4%, maximum 51.72%, and 69 of the 1,010,000 plans exceed the minority map's 51.69%. Those 69 are not 69 separate maps: neighbouring maps in the chain are near-copies, and the 69 come from a handful of short runs. All of them sit at 45 of 87 seats (51.72%), which edges past the minority's 46 of 89 (51.69%) only because the district counts differ. The million are worth about 1,000 independent draws (roughly 900 to 1,300, depending on the measure), and on this seats measure the pooled estimate is just under the 1,000 the audit set as its own floor (`findings/audit_2026-11-10/ess_uncapped.json`). So a percentile this far out is less exact than the raw count makes it look. The other fairness measures repeat the story: on the mean-median gap the same map lands deeper in the tail than 99.97 per cent of the neutral maps; on declination, deeper than 98.8 per cent. All of them lean the same way, toward the UCP. What does that mean in seats? Replay the 2023 election-day vote, which leaned more UCP than the full count, and the minority map's geometry hands the UCP 60 of 89 seats. On the majority map, 55. On all 2023 ballots, the count the audit now uses ([November](#november)), the two maps give 55 and 48. Those two counts depend on how ballots cast before election day are placed inside each division: on the rule a later check supports, the minority map gives 56 or 57 and the majority 47 to 49.

To measure what "aiming" can do, the audit also ran a targeted hill-climbing search in both directions, the standard tool for exploring maps that are biased but legal (Cannon et al. 2023). It took 40,000 steps each way, under the same constraints. The UCP-aimed search ran on the official boundaries; the NDP-aimed search ran in April 2026 on provisional ones and was not repeated:

| Procedure | Most-extreme value reached | What it tells us |
|---|---|---|
| Neutral simulation, maximum produced | 51.72% UCP seats @ 50/50 | The largest value among the neutral maps (45 of 87 seats) |
| Neutral simulation, minimum produced | 37.9% UCP seats @ 50/50 | The smallest value among the neutral maps |
| Targeted hill-climb, UCP-maximizing | **52.9%** | The best one short search found (46 of 87 seats); nothing shows it is a maximum |
| Targeted hill-climb, NDP-maximizing | **37.9%** | No further than neutral drawing (April run, provisional boundaries) |

The minority map's 51.7% sits at the very top of the neutral range, just under its maximum (51.72%), and short of what the UCP-aimed search reached (52.9%). The search's best and the minority map are both 46 seats: 46 of 87 and 46 of 89. So the search does not separate the minority map from neutral drawing. Aimed the other way, on provisional boundaries, the search did no better than neutral drawing. The majority map's 46.1% sits at the 78th percentile, inside the normal range. And around 50/50 the lopsidedness is starker than the number alone. A fine-grained sweep finds the minority map keeps the UCP at or above the 45-seat majority threshold down to about **49.2%** of the two-party vote: the UCP could lose it by about 1.6 points and still form government. On the majority map, the UCP would need to *win* it by about 9 points to reach the same threshold. Put the other way, on the majority map the NDP could lose the two-party vote by about 9 points and still win most seats. That is Alberta's geography at work, and the minority map removes it. Same geography, same rules. The gap between the two maps is structural, not noise.

**What it could show.** Neutral processes essentially never produce this map. The majority map is the kind of map a neutral procedure turns out all the time. The minority map is the kind you have to set out to draw.

**What the experts say.** Ensemble outlier analysis, setting a real map against a large collection (an ensemble) of neutral maps to see whether it stands out, is the state of the art in redistricting statistics. It is the method US courts found persuasive in the state cases that struck maps down. Three of four metrics in the extreme tail, and all four leaning the same way, is a strong outlier profile. And the "it's just Alberta's natural geography" defence is already answered inside the method, because the simulation runs *on* Alberta's natural geography. That geography's baseline advantage tops out where the 2019 map and the 2026 majority sit: the 65th to 78th percentile on seats, with the 2019 map's highest placement on any partisan measure in the low 90s. The minority sits at the 99.99th percentile on the raw count, *against Alberta's naturally skewed baseline*.

**What Canadian practice expects.** The audit found no Canadian commission that has been required to test its map against a neutral ensemble. Here the audit is importing a standard the process itself never had to meet. That is exactly why the result tells you something. And exactly why it binds nobody.

**Why it isn't proof.** Two reasons, one legal and one statistical. Legal: as far as this audit's review found, no Canadian court has yet weighed ensemble evidence, and in the United States the Supreme Court held in *Rucho v. Common Cause* (2019) that even strong statistical evidence of partisan gerrymandering is not for federal courts to police. A percentile is a description, not a threshold crossed. Statistical: the ensemble respects the population limits and keeps every district in a single connected piece, but it does not enforce every statutory criterion the commission worked under (s.15(2) hybrid protections, community of interest, municipal anchoring). So its percentiles measure how extreme a map is against that reference set, not against "everything a legally compliant commission might have drawn." A strong outside check; not a perfect one. One more honest limit: a real electorate is not frozen at 50/50, and a big enough swing can swamp any map's structural lean. The 50/50 test isolates what the map adds to the outcome, not the outcome itself. *(Legal lesson 5: the best evidence type in the field has no legal home in Canada.)*

### Finding 7 — The test that missed {#finding-7}

![How skewed each map looks on the partisan-fairness number. The majority sits at +0.1% — inside the normal range. The minority sits at +4.0%, just below the audit's outlier line at ~4.1%. The further right the line, the more the map favours the UCP relative to its provincial vote share.](../../data/maps/article/lane1_dotplot.svg)

**What we found.** The fourth partisan-fairness metric is the *efficiency gap*, a single number for how lopsidedly a party's votes turn into seats. For the minority map it came in at +4.0%: the 94.5th percentile. The outlier line was fixed in advance as a rule: the 95th percentile of the neutral maps. Its value, about +4.1%, was computed from the ensemble. It missed. It leans the UCP's way, but it sits below the line, and every table in this report shows it that way. The majority map's efficiency gap is +0.1% (16.5th percentile).

**What it could show.** On its own, almost nothing. That is exactly why it is in the log.

**What the experts say.** An honest set of tests produces near-misses: a report where every test fires should make you more suspicious of the auditor than of the map. This one missed, and it stays a miss. (The efficiency gap's proposed US threshold, about 7%, is an academic proposal no court has adopted.)

**What Canadian practice expects.** Nothing. The audit found no Canadian commission or court that uses the efficiency gap.

**Why it isn't proof.** It isn't even claimed as evidence against the map. It is here to show the evidence was counted honestly. Pre-registration cuts both ways: the same locked line that gives the other metrics their force makes this one a miss. ### Finding 8 — Between one in 20,000 and one in 570,000, and what that number is not {#finding-8}

**What we found.** A statistical model puts the chance that a neutral drawing process produces the minority map's combined four-metric profile at somewhere **between about one in 20,000 and about one in 570,000**, depending on defensible choices in how it is computed. The map is far out in the tail on every version. The model as first published gives about one in 566,000. A version that allows for the million maps not being independent draws gives about one in 390,000 to 450,000. Two of the four measures, the efficiency gap and declination, are close to one measure counted twice; dropping declination gives about one in 97,000. A version built on ranks gives about one in 238,000. A second, much smaller sampler gives about one in 20,000 (`findings/audit_2026-11-10/joint_sensitivity.json`). All of these use election-day ballots only. No all-ballots version exists, because the all-ballots scores of the million maps are not in the repository; on all ballots the two measures that carry most of the distance, the mean-median gap and seats at a tied vote, rank lower ([November](#november)). Each figure is already doubled. The audit first doubled the model's number to cover a second analytical channel it had examined. That channel has since been retired (Finding 10), so the doubling no longer matches any defined set of tests; it is kept as a cautious allowance, and undoubled each chance is half as large. The four measures inside the simulation count as one look, not four, because they are folded into a single joint score before any probability is worked out. (For the technical record: on the published model p ≈ 8.84×10⁻⁷, doubled ≈ 1.77×10⁻⁶.) This is an exploratory result: the joint test and the doubling were not registered before the data were examined. Two limits come with it. On the published measure, no sampled map reaches the minority's distance from the centre; on the rank-based measure, seven of the 1,010,000 do. So each figure is a model's estimate at or past the edge of the sampled maps. It is not a count and not a bound. And the neutral maps have 87 districts against the commission maps' 89. A size-matched check (October 30, provisional) finds the same tilt; a size-matched joint number has not been reported.

**What it could show.** You want this number to be "the probability the map is innocent." It is the audit's strongest single number, and the pull to read it that way is enormous.

**What the experts say.** The number is well defined, and defensible as what it actually is: the probability of the *map's measured profile* arising under a *neutral drawing process*. Statistics has a name for confusing P(evidence given innocence) with P(innocence given evidence). It is the prosecutor's fallacy, and it has sent innocent people to prison: in England, Sally Clark was convicted in 1999 partly on a "1 in 73 million" figure, and the conviction was quashed in 2003. This report refuses the swap. How rare the observation is under the neutral null is not the probability of wrongdoing; for that you would need things no one has, like how often drafting is eccentric but sincere.

**What Canadian practice expects.** No Canadian legal standard uses a number like this. There is no threshold for it to cross, because the law never defined one.

**Why it isn't proof.** A map this rare under the model can still, in principle, come from unusual but honest judgment. The number measures how far outside neutral practice the map sits. It cannot see motive, and this report will not pretend it can. What the audit does claim is weaker, and well supported: drawing that follows the rules and never looks at votes almost never produces this profile. What *is* the explanation is a question the audit puts in your hands. *(Legal-epistemic lesson: no probability of a map is a probability of intent.)*

### Finding 9 — We tried to knock down our own result {#finding-9}

**What we found.** The audit mounted three attacks on its own headline, with mixed results, and all of them are reported. First, the sampler swap, which changes the engine that draws the neutral maps: the audit replaced the simulation's mathematical engine with a structurally different one (a spanning-forest sampler, pre-registered at OSF as registration he53s and executed 2026-07-10). Every metric's percentile moved by less than one point, which meets the registered rule for a "robust" verdict on all four. But the run fell far short of its registered effective-sample target, so the audit reads it as inconclusive at this run size, not as a finding of robustness. This far into the tail a fraction of a point matters: on the new engine more than ten times as many maps reach the minority's seat value as on the main run, and the combined-profile figure on it is about one in 20,000 (Finding 8). So it shows the map stays far out in the tail, not exactly how far. Second, the software swap: an independent implementation (the R `redist` package) drew 5,000 plans, and none reached the minority's value; on that smaller run the majority map also sits just above the 95th percentile ([`findings/redist_python_comparison.md`](../../findings/redist_python_comparison.md)). Third, the mechanism test: the audit planned to test whether low compactness, the irregular shapes Act I flags, is what produces the Act II seat advantage, by comparing the independent plans that reach the minority's seat value with the rest. On the final data it could not be run: none of the 5,000 plans reaches that value, so there is nothing to compare. (At the lower mark the test first used, only 3 plans qualify, and they are slightly less compact than the rest, 0.227 against 0.232 on the Polsby-Popper measure; three plans are too few to mean anything.) Both lanes point at the same map and lean the same way, but the claim that the geometry is the mechanism is untested, and the report does not make it.

**What it could show.** The outlier belongs to the map, not to the audit's tools.

**What the experts say.** Holding up across samplers and implementations is what separates a finding from an artifact. Publishing the checks that fail, or that cannot be run, is what separates an audit from advocacy.

**What Canadian practice expects.** Nobody requires any of this. The audit imposed it on itself, in writing, before running it.

**Why it isn't proof.** Ruling out artifacts is not ruling in design. The sampler and software swaps found no sign that the map's tail position is an artifact of the tools, though the sampler run was too small to be conclusive. They say nothing about *why*. *(Epistemic lesson: robustness strengthens the description, never the accusation.)*

### Finding 10 — The signals that pointed the other way {#finding-10}

**What we found.** Three entries in the log do not help the audit's story, and all are reported. **One the minority passed:** the neighbour-drain test looks for the classic adjacency pattern, where packed districts sit right beside districts won on thin margins. The minority shows 1 coupled chain signal against the majority's 2 (and the 2019 enacted map's 5): a pass, the single test where the minority numerically beats the majority. The test is exploratory and the pass is weak: with the labels shuffled at random it turns up about 91% of the time, and the test's second prediction failed. The pass stands as a pass. **Two the audit retired:** the Swing-Zone Allocation Test asked whether voting areas in swing zones were allocated to districts in a partisan pattern. It looked significant (p = 0.0024) until the audit corrected its null for spatial clustering. Neighbouring voting areas vote alike, which breaks the test's assumption that each area is independent. After the correction it returns p ≈ 0.19. The corrected version is crude too: one cluster holds 2,086 of the 2,110 swing-zone areas, so it has almost no power either way. Neither version can be relied on, and the test was retired to exploratory status on 2026-06-13. And the Fisher combination of the two channels assumes an independence they do not have, so the audit does not use it (Finding 8).

**What it could show.** As first computed, the retired tests suggested that the specific boundary choices, not just the map as a whole, were partisan-loaded. After correction, they suggest nothing.

**What the experts say.** Spatial autocorrelation, the plain fact that neighbours vote like neighbours, is the classic way redistricting statistics fool the people who make them. Correcting for it is the right step, even when, as here, the corrected test is too coarse to say much. A pass on the drain test beside tail results elsewhere is not a contradiction; it is a fingerprint: it tells you *which* mechanism this map does not use.

**What Canadian practice expects.** Not applicable. These are the audit's own instruments.

**Why it isn't proof.** Two of these were the audit's own instruments failing, not the map's, and the third points in the map's favour. They are in the log because an evidence log that records only the convenient entries is not an evidence log. *(Epistemic lesson, stated twice in this report on purpose: the null can win.)*

*Two retired, one pass for the other side. We logged them anyway.* — [Know more: Appendix J, why neighbours voting alike breaks naive statistics](#appendix-j)

## Act III: What People Did {.new-page}

Acts I and II measured paper. Act III reads conduct: what the people in and around the process wrote, claimed and chose. In court, evidence of conduct is usually where questions of intent get answered. Watch what happens to it here.

### Finding 11 — Six reasons, checked {#finding-11}

**What we found.** We checked six of the minority's contested redraws against public data.[^asym] Some of the reasons we checked are our reading of the minority's text, not words it wrote. The point is not that the minority made its reasons up; in most of these cases the general claim is true, and a boundary that honoured it *could have* been drawn. What the data does not reach is the *particular line the minority actually drew*. The minority defends Calgary-Nolan Hill–Cochrane on the idea that Cochrane residents "move fluidly" into Calgary; Statistics Canada's 2021 journey-to-work data shows about a third of Cochrane's workers commute somewhere in Calgary, while roughly half work within Cochrane. That is a real city-to-city tie, but one that says nothing about the Nolan Hill neighbourhood the corridor actually reaches. Airdrie's commute to Calgary (about 76 percent of its out-commuters) and its rapid growth are both real, and could have supported a new Airdrie-area seat — but neither requires splitting the city four ways. Chestermere sends 86 percent of its out-commuters to Calgary, yet shares no transit system with the specific Calgary district the minority slices part of it into. And for pairing Red Deer with Sylvan Lake, the minority says rural residents "work, go to school, and gather at community events" in the city. Public schools are separate (Sylvan Lake is in Chinook's Edge School Division, the City of Red Deer in Red Deer Public), but Red Deer Catholic Regional serves both, so the school tie is partial, not absent. The tally depends on the rule used. Counting only the reasons the minority actually stated, none of the six fails, and four are partly supported: true in general, but short of the line drawn. Counting the audit's own arithmetic on whether population forced the line, which the minority did not claim, four fail.

[^asym]: The audit tested only the minority's stated reasons (and, in Finding 15, the panel's). It did not test the majority's. The majority's part of the final report does give checkable reasons for its own contested choices (final report PDF, pp. 44–60): for example, it kept Airdrie-West 12.4% under average on a growth projection. The six-reason check is reported as a single flag, not as extra rows in the structural comparisons.

**What it could show.** Reasons that do not survive checking look like rationalizations: reasons found after the line, not before. Where the audit's population arithmetic fails a line, it pointed to a boundary the minority could have drawn. The line it drew is a different one.

**What the experts say.** Setting a stated reason against what the evidence supports is called pretext analysis. It is one way US courts probe intent in the districting cases they still hear, which since 2019 are mainly race-based (for example *Cooper v. Harris*, 2017). Reasons that fail on public data are the standard tell.

**What Canadian practice expects.** Commissions publish reasons so the public can weigh them. Reasons that don't reach the line drawn, on free, public Statistics Canada data, defeat the point of publishing them.

**Why it isn't proof.** No Canadian law requires a commissioner's reasons to survive an independent check, and a failed reason fits sloppiness as easily as pretext. Courts reviewing boundaries ask whether the outcome denies effective representation, not whether the reasoning in the file was tidy. *(Legal lesson 6: bad reasons for a lawful line are still a lawful line.)*

### Finding 12 — The commission that couldn't sign one map {#finding-12}

**What we found.** The commission split: two final reports, two incompatible 89-seat maps, filed the same day. The three-commissioner majority included the chair; the two-commissioner minority, Dr. Julian Martin and John D. Evans, KC, the commissioners the government nominated, produced the map every finding so far describes. The Legislative Assembly's appointment notice lists both as "Nominated by the President of Executive Council", and Greg Clark and Susan Samson as "Nominated by the Leader of His Majesty's Loyal Opposition" ([March 28, 2025](https://www.assembly.ab.ca/about/news-and-announcements/2025/03/28/electoral-boundaries-commission-to-soon-begin-consultations); copy at `evidence/legislature/assembly_news_ebc_appointed_2025-03-28.html`). The audit's instruments cut both ways across that divide. The majority report asserted that the minority's changes in five places (Airdrie, Cochrane, Chestermere, Red Deer and St. Albert) had **no public support** in the 1,345 submissions (final report, pp. 59 and 62). The audit first searched the archive by keyword, and its support-or-oppose labels proved unreliable: in a random sample, 26 of 31 were wrong. So we read every record in full: all 1,339 posted written submissions and all 349 hearing presentations ([`findings/submissions_recode/`](../../findings/submissions_recode/README.md); the readers were AI agents working under the author's direction). Counted by record, the number asking for what only the minority drew was 1 at Airdrie, 0 at Cochrane, 3 at Chestermere, 1 at Red Deer and 0 at St. Albert. The number asking for what only the majority drew was 10, 7, 6, 20 and 0; at St. Albert, 22 asked for the pattern both maps keep. So the majority's statement is right at Cochrane and St. Albert, and wrong by one to three records at Airdrie, Red Deer and Chestermere. **Where it is wrong, it cuts against the majority, not the minority**, and it is in this log for exactly that reason.

The criticism ran the other way too, and the log records that as well. The minority report charges the majority with “the Majority's inclination to delete or mangle rural electoral divisions and, in effect, reduce effective rural representation” (final report PDF, p. 356; see also PDF p. 287: the minority “departed from the majority where we believe the legislative direction given to the Commission and the thoughtfulness of public submissions has not been given sufficient effect”). The audit's one structural counter-test aimed at the majority so far asked whether the majority's rural districts are engineered corridors of isolation. It came back supporting the innocent, geographic explanation (exploratory; design and limits at `preregistration/t3_2_majority_rural_isolation_design.md`). The audit also counted the rural divisions themselves, the same day the charge was surfaced (exploratory; hypotheses frozen before execution; full method, names, and classifier limits at `findings/rural_division_count.md`). The direction reverses: against 2019's 31 rural-anchored divisions, the majority keeps 28, absorbing three into city-named districts, merging six into three and creating three new ones, while the minority keeps 23, absorbing seven into city-named districts and merging two more into the majority-flagged Rocky Mountain House–Banff Park. A stricter version of the classifier gives the same order (28 → 24 → 19).

Both maps thin rural representation compared with 2019, which is the true core of the charge and fits the chair's own call for added rural seats. But on the measure the accusation itself names, the minority map does more of what it accuses the majority of. The same page makes a second charge that can be measured — that the majority "decline[d] to use" the Act's ±25% flexibility in rural Alberta, a flexibility the minority insists is "not a benign allowance for errors in mapmaking" but "a deliberate instruction" — and that one holds (`findings/rural_variance_usage.md`). The majority's rural divisions average 6% below the provincial mean, with only four drawn more than 10% light; the minority's average 12% below, with eleven drawn more than 10% light. Tested together, the two charges describe two design philosophies, not one vice: more but fuller rural divisions on the majority, fewer but lighter on the minority. Both directions of every cut are in the log.

**What it could show.** A process built to reach agreement produced two answers, and the outlier map came from the government's nominees.

**What the experts say.** Who appointed whom is standard evidence when researchers ask whether an institution has been captured. It carries weight only when the instruments are applied evenly to both sides, which is why the audit's one correction of the majority, at Chestermere, sits in the log beside its findings against the minority.

**What Canadian practice expects.** One map per commission is the norm, even when a commissioner dissents. The Act does plan for a split: when there is more than one report, the majority's is the commission's report (s.9, added in 1995). It says nothing about a majority accusing colleagues in public.

**Why it isn't proof.** Nomination is not instruction. The audit found no evidence of coordination between the government and its nominees, and none can be inferred from appointment alone. Commissioners, whoever named them, have gone their own way before. *(Epistemic lesson: provenance raises the question; it cannot answer it.)*

### Finding 13 — When the process might have answered, it was replaced {#finding-13}

**What we found.** The commission held hearings in sixteen communities and received 1,345 written submissions (1,147 of them on the interim proposal). On the strength of many of them (the minority report counts at least 56), it reversed its interim proposal to fold Lesser Slave Lake into a larger riding: a system visibly answering the public. Four weeks after its split reports arrived, that system was replaced. The Legislative Assembly passed Government Motion 37 (put on notice April 16; moved, debated and passed April 21, 2026). It set both reports aside and created a Select Special Committee of six MLAs, four UCP and two NDP, chaired by UCP MLA Brandon Lunty, to oversee a new 91-seat map. An independent advisory panel drew it (chaired by retired Court of Appeal justice Brian O'Ferrall, appointed June 2, 2026, on the Premier's nomination; the other four members, two nominated by the Premier and two by the Opposition, were appointed a week later; committee report, p. 9) and gave it to the committee on October 22. The panel itself split. The committee was not required to hold public hearings. The Opposition leader said the process "explicitly says we don't want to hear from the public, we don't want to do any public hearings" ([CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/edmonton/alberta-boundaries-committee-motion-9.7172743)). It held none, and no draft was released for public challenge. On October 28 the committee voted 3–2 to end its review, adding no observations of its own. The committee gave the panel's report to the Assembly on November 2 (Assembly committee page; committee transcript, `evidence/legislature/ceb_transcript_2026-10-28.pdf`, pp. CEB-65, CEB-67, CEB-68, CEB-72 and CEB-73). The Assembly decides. On the floor, the government presented the takeover as loyalty to the commission: "We are adopting the recommendations from the report" (Government House Leader, Hansard, April 16, 2026). The opposition described the same motion as presenting the "minority report in slow motion." Set against the report itself, the loyalty claim reads narrower: the majority asked the Legislature to adopt its 89-seat map; its seat-count fix was aimed at future commissions; and the one recommendation that did propose 91 seats was the chair's alone: "That is why I am alone in making this recommendation." That recommendation also said the change "would need to be studied by an all-party Select Special Committee" (final report, p. 66). And the majority did write that more than two added seats would have made it easier to meet Clearwater County's requests (p. 54).

**What it could show.** When the independent route delivered a split answer, the government took the pen — and the vehicle it chose removed every way the public could contest what it writes before it becomes law.

**What the experts say.** The design gives up every transparency feature the redistricting research treats as protective: published drafts, public hearings, mapmakers kept at arm's length. Civil-liberties observers ([CCLA](https://ccla.org/press-release/ccla-sounds-the-alarm-over-the-politicization-of-albertas-electoral-map/)) raised the concern about politicization too.

**What Canadian practice expects.** This is the deepest norm in Canadian redistribution. Politicians do not draw their own districts. The audit's November tests (below) will measure the committee's process against exactly the features it is not required to provide.

**Why it isn't proof.** Nothing in the Act forbids any of these steps, and no court has ruled on them. The Act makes the commission's report advisory, sets no minimum notice or comment period, and requires no hearing and no released draft for whatever follows. As the audit understands parliamentary sovereignty, the legislature was free to do what it did. The audit does not rule on legality. *(Legal lesson 7: the process that could have created accountability is optional — and nothing in the Act forbids opting out.)*

### Finding 14 — The sixty-year norm, set aside by a single motion {#finding-14}

**What we found.** Canada spent roughly sixty years building independent boundary commissions, the institutional answer, from the federal reform era of the 1960s on, to a once-routine practice: governing parties drawing their own ridings (Courtney 2001). In May, Duane Bratt, Professor of Political Science at Mount Royal University, described the process to the author, in private correspondence, as without precedent in Canada. The audit later found one earlier case, and the distinction that follows is the audit's, not his. Alberta handed a map to MLAs once before, in 1992, after the commission of the time could not agree on a map. In 2026 the commission did not deadlock. A majority signed a report, which the Act treats as the commission's report, and it was set aside anyway. In Quebec, a commission's map takes effect without the National Assembly's final approval. In Alberta, as in most provinces, it takes a bill (*Lalande*, dissent, para. 31).

**What it could show.** The opposition named it on the floor: "a retaliation against the independent Electoral Boundaries Commission for daring to do its job." The injury is not one map. It is the demonstration that the guardrail was a convention, an unwritten rule, and conventions bind only the governments that consent to be bound. Nothing stops the next government, of any party, from citing this cycle as precedent.

**What the experts say.** Much of the comparative research treats independent commissions as among the strongest institutional protections against gerrymandering. Removing the institution matters more than any one boundary it might have drawn.

**What Canadian practice expects.** The federal process and the provinces use arm's-length commissions, with exceptions: Alberta's 1992 committee, and Quebec, whose National Assembly passed a law in June 2026 setting its 127-seat map itself, after the Supreme Court struck down its pause of the commission. That law used the commission's lines for most regions and kept the 2017 lines for 12 divisions. That is the standard this cycle left behind. Mid-cycle. By simple majority vote.

**Why it isn't proof.** Constitutional conventions bind politically, but courts will not enforce them. The Supreme Court said as much in the 1981 Patriation Reference, and courts have held that line since: a court may name a convention and still decline to enforce it. The norm's violation is real and documented. That does not put the process beyond the courts' reach. In 2017 Nova Scotia's Court of Appeal found a map unconstitutional because the government's intervention kept the commission from doing its own balancing, so the final report did not reflect the commission's own view (*Reference re the Final Report of the Electoral Boundaries Commission*, 2017 NSCA 10, as the court's information sheet summarizes it). The same sheet says there is no constitutional requirement for an independent commission, and that the government could have changed the map itself by amending the bill. In 2026 the Supreme Court struck down Quebec's law pausing its commission (*Quebec (AG) v. Lalande*, 2026 SCC 13). What a court reviews is the enacted map, under section 3. *(Legal lesson 8, the last one: norms are not law, but how a map is made can still matter in court.)*

### Finding 15 — The panel's reasons, checked against its own map {#finding-15}

*Added November 7 and 8, 2026. The panel's map has not been released as a shapefile yet. Where this finding uses the map's lines, it uses the audit's reconstruction of them from the panel's report, so those parts are provisional. The populations and quotes are the panel's own.*

**What we found.** The advisory panel published reasons for its changes to the commission majority's map. We checked those reasons the same way Finding 11 checked the commission minority's: does the panel apply each reason the same way to comparable places, and do its own numbers support it? Where a change moves no seat, the reasons mostly hold. Calgary-West-Springbank really is the "much more regular polygon shape" the panel promised. But in the two changes that move a seat on election-day ballots, Lethbridge and Banff, the reasons we could check do not hold up. (On all ballots under rule P, the registered way of placing early ballots, no seat moves; on Amendment 12's rule, kept as a sensitivity, only the Banff change moves a seat; see [November](#november).)

- **Lethbridge, "population to spare."** The panel's main reason for its southern changes is "better regional representation of the people of southern Alberta": the towns around Lethbridge are too small for divisions of their own, while "Lethbridge already has population to spare." The panel itself pairs the two cities: only two Alberta cities have no division reaching past their limits, and "Those two cities are Lethbridge and Red Deer." On the province's 2024 estimates, Red Deer has 113,145 people and Lethbridge 111,776. Each has room for about two divisions, and Red Deer slightly more. The panel moves only a small edge of Red Deer into a rural division, about 1,300 people into Blackfalds-Innisfail, and keeps the rest of the city in two city divisions. It splits Lethbridge into one city division and three that mix city and rural voters.
- **Lethbridge, the panel's own test.** The panel says a mixed city-and-rural division is justified if it "reflects a genuine community of interest or is needed to achieve population-compliance." Population compliance did not require it. The commission's map met the legal band in the south without mixing Lethbridge voters into rural divisions: its two Lethbridge divisions sat 7.0% and 1.0% above the panel's own average of 53,722, and the rural divisions around the city sat between 6.7% below and 2.3% above it. The panel's own report names larger rural divisions as the other way to meet the band. So the split is needed only once the panel chooses smaller rural divisions. That leaves community of interest, which the panel argues and the panel's minority disputes: "Its residents and leaders clearly want two MLAs."
- **Lethbridge, a seat of its own.** Elsewhere the panel's rule is that growing mid-size places deserve "dedicated representation", which it uses to give Cochrane and Okotoks their own divisions. Lethbridge, a larger city, goes from two dedicated divisions to one.
- **Banff, "the necessary population."** The panel calls its Banff change "a minor change ... to capture the necessary population," because the Act forbids an ordinary division more than 25% below average. The commission's Canmore-Banff was 331 people short of that floor, and at most about 1,300 short after a small transfer the whole panel agreed on. The panel added 8,901. It gives other reasons too, which were not tested here: keeping the Bow Valley, Kananaskis Country and the foothills together as one mountain-and-foothills community, and giving Okotoks its own representative.
- **Springbank, "urban with urban."** The panel says its Springbank divisions mix "urban with urban residents." In 2021, the Springbank parts held about 40 to 70 people per square kilometre. The Calgary parts of the same divisions held about 1,500 to 1,900.

**What it could show.** A reason applied in one place and not in a comparable one is a reason that may not be the real one. In Lethbridge and Banff, the two changes that move a seat on election-day ballots, the panel's population reasons do not match its own numbers. Its other reasons there, regional representation and community of interest, are disputed and were not tested. On 2023's votes those changes cost the NDP about one seat. And of the NDP votes the panel's changes move, about four and a half times as many go from a seat the NDP wins or nearly wins into one it loses by 10 points or more as go the other way (14,199 against 3,152).

**What the experts say.** Checking whether stated reasons are applied evenly is the core of pretext analysis, the same method described in Finding 11. A reason that holds everywhere it is used is evidence of a principle. A reason that holds only where it helps one side is the classic sign of a rationale found after the line was drawn.

**What Canadian practice expects.** Commissions and panels publish reasons so the public and the legislature can weigh them. The reasons are meant to be applied the same way across the map. Here they can be checked against the panel's own numbers.

**Why it isn't proof.** Districts are drawn by deliberate choice, and an uneven reason fits several explanations: a judgment that changed between sections of the report, a community tie the numbers cannot see, or a partisan purpose. The statistics in this report can say that the Lethbridge split is rare among maps that respect city boundaries (about 1 in 100). But that is one of 12 such flags among 132 comparisons, none of which survives a correction for the number of tests, and where it puts the city's voters is not extreme by the rule fixed in advance. They can also say that it is not extreme compared with small changes to the panel's own map, and that neutral redraws of the south and central region find the panel's layout there ordinary: much of the four-and-a-half-to-one flow above is a move away from the commission majority's layout, which those redraws find unusual, partly because they cannot draw a district as small as its Canmore-Banff ([November](#november)). They cannot say why it was drawn. A court would ask whether the result denies effective representation, not whether the reasons were tidy. *(Legal lesson 6 again: bad reasons for a lawful line are still a lawful line. But they are the right place to look.)*

>
> The audit measured the effects. It cannot read minds.

**What we found.** A scorecard: the best instrument the author could build to pull the first fourteen findings together into something that can be stated, checked, and shown wrong. > **WHY A LARGE MAJORITY MATTERS**
>
> Under Canada's Westminster parliamentary system, Alberta's Assembly has no two-thirds threshold that unlocks special powers. A governing majority, even a bare one, can already pass laws and budgets, cut debate short with a programming motion or the previous question, change the rules of the House, and set the membership of every committee, which the rules keep in proportion to seats. What a very large majority changes is margin and staying power, not the menu of powers. A government holding 57 of 89 seats, which is what the minority map gives the UCP on all 2023 ballots (55 on Amendment 12's way of placing early ballots, kept as a sensitivity; 60 on election-day ballots alone), is well insulated from dissent inside: a handful of floor-crossings cannot threaten it, and it faces a smaller opposition, with fewer members to sustain committee scrutiny. The concern here is the concentration of seats and the thinning of effective opposition, not a procedural threshold being crossed.

### What running the play again would look like

This report cannot prove a gerrymander; the word has no legal home here. What it can do is say, in advance and in public, exactly what the signature looks like, and then say whether the panel's new map carries it. The audit's November tests were written down in June 2026, before the panel's map was made public, and they are dated in this repository ([`preregistration/november_2026_scoring_spec.md`](../../preregistration/november_2026_scoring_spec.md)). Process: were public hearings held; was a draft released; are the advisory panel's members and terms of reference published; does the map honour the recommendation the government cited when it took the pen — nine process features, now frozen as their own registered scorecard (OSF, registration u47bq, filed July 14, 2026), so the process half is scored the same locked way the map half is. Outcome: where the map lands on every metric in this report, against the same million-map distribution, under the pre-registered thresholds (on all ballots, two of those lines are taken afresh from neutral maps scored the same way: Amendments 12 and 14). That includes whether the two added rural seats are a plain addition or an engineered one.

Suppose the panel's map replicates the minority's signature (drawn by different people, under different constraints, on a different timeline). The structural half of that match would mean little on its own: measured before the map arrived, most neutral maps drawn under the statute's rules would replicate it too, because both commission maps follow municipal lines and balance populations more tightly than neutral maps do. So it is the partisan half that carries the weight. If it lands inside the neutral bands, the finding stands as an isolated observation about one map that was never enacted, and this report will say exactly that. The scoring runs within 72 hours of the map's release, whichever way it points. That commitment was registered before anyone knew the answer. (The registered checklist was scored late, on November 9; see [November](#november).) Pass or fail, you will not have to trust this audit's judgment. Only its arithmetic: public, seeded from a public beacon, and reproducible by anyone.

Against neutral redraws of the south and central region, the panel's layout there is ordinary and the commission majority's is the unusual one, partly because of how the redraws are built (below), so much of this flow is a move away from an unusual starting point.
- **City limits in past maps.** Here a division counts as crossing a city limit when 5% to 95% of its people live in the city; one that reaches past the limit but holds almost only city residents is not counted. At Calgary's limit, the 2010 and 2017 maps and the commission majority's map have none, and the 2003 map had three. The panel's map has two (the Springbank divisions); the commission minority's had six. At Edmonton's limit, the 2003, 2010 and 2017 maps had none; the commission majority's map and the panel's each have two, and the minority's three (`findings/city_weight/big_city_lines.json`).
- **Rural voters.** In Lethbridge County, the number of rural residents placed in divisions that are mostly city goes from 0 on the commission majority's map to 8,138 on the panel's. Rocky View County goes from 4 pieces to 6. On the check's own measure, one of the panel's own changes overrides a rural preference on 2023 votes: Edmonton-Nisku, which holds about 7,000 rural residents (`findings/rural_voice/`).
- **Which population the comparisons use.** The neutral maps balance 2021 census population; the real maps were drawn on 2024 estimates. Calgary and Edmonton grew faster, and on 2024 figures they hold about two seats' worth more people (51.4 against 49.5). On each map's own published figures, city divisions average 9 to 10 points more people than the rest, compared with what the 2021 figures show (the panel's map: +13.4% against +4.3%). That is a limit on every city comparison in this report. A rerun of the neutral maps on 2024 figures is proposed (`findings/population_basis/`).
- **Every changed region.** Each group of divisions the panel changed was redrawn without reference to party, at each map's own number of seats. The panel's layouts sit inside the normal range in every region, on every measure. The commission majority's layout of the south and central region is the unusual one: it packs more NDP votes into wide wins (Lethbridge-West and Canmore-Banff) than any of the redraws (50,000, drawn each of two ways), and puts fewer in lopsided losses. Part of that is built in. The redraws keep every district within 25% of the region's average and have no exception for remote divisions, so none can hold a district as small as the majority's Canmore-Banff or the panel's Rocky Mountain House-Sundre. Even Lethbridge-West on its own packs more NDP votes than 95% of the redraws do across the whole region, both ways. The panel's changes lower that packing (`findings/changed_districts/`).
- **All of it together.** The newer checks made 132 percentile comparisons (and 11 other tests). 12 fall beyond the 5th or 95th percentile: 8 for the panel's map and the ¶167 map, and 4 for the commission majority's layout of the south. None holds up after correcting for the number of tests.

**What it could show.** Statistics can say whether a pattern is common or rare among maps drawn by the same rules without looking at votes. Most of what the panel's changes do is common. The four-way Lethbridge split is rare among maps that respect city limits (about 1 in 100), but where it puts the city's voters is not extreme by the rule fixed in advance. Why a line was drawn is a different question. It has to be answered from the panel's stated reasons and whether it applied them the same way to comparable places, which is Finding 15.

**Why it isn't proof.** The map is a reconstruction until the official file is posted. The newer checks are unregistered, and their author knew related results first. The effects are small, one or two seats, and nothing in the Act forbids any of them, as the audit reads it.

## The Author's View {#authors-view}

*Everything above this line is the audit. Dated tests. Documented corrections. Evidence you can check without trusting me. Delete this section and not one number changes. What follows is not a finding. It is an opinion — signed, severable, and mine.*

The audit cannot prove intent. I have kept that discipline on every page above. This page is where I set it down.

Start with the two facts I cannot make innocent. Government-nominated commissioners drew a map that neutral map-drawing almost never produces — fewer than a hundred matches in a million draws. And when the commission split rather than endorse it, the government took the pen.

Each fact has an innocent reading. A commissioner can be eccentric in good faith. A legislature can lose patience with a split commission. But put the facts in sequence and the innocent readings strain.

Here is what alarms me. It is not that a law was broken. No law was broken — I checked, and that checking is most of what this audit is. The statute makes the commission's report advisory. It sets no notice period. It requires no draft, no hearings, no consultation. Every step of the takeover was legal. That is the alarm. The guardrail was never law. It was a habit — and habits only bind governments that choose to keep them.

Look at what the habit used to buy. The commission held hearings in sixteen communities. It read 1,345 written submissions. It changed its mind in public, because the public gave it reasons to. The committee that replaced it is six MLAs: four government, two opposition, government chair. No rule required an independent voice at that table, and none sits there. There is an advisory panel — chaired, to its credit, by a retired judge. But advisers advise. The votes belong to the MLAs, and the map the Assembly enacts is the map they will run in. It faced no public hearing. No draft was released for the public to contest. It arrives finished.

And this government has been here before. Its first bill, as written, would have let cabinet rewrite laws without the legislature. That power was stripped — after the outcry, not before. Its own ethics commissioner found the Premier breached the conflicts law by pressing her attorney general about a criminal case, and called interference with the administration of justice "a threat to democracy." It passed a law letting cabinet repeal some local bylaws and remove elected councillors when it judges that in the public interest. And when a court paused one of its laws in June 2025, it invoked the notwithstanding clause that December; a week later the injunction was gone. The ethics breach aside, every one of those moves was lawful. Every one spent a norm to get past an independent check. Redrawing the electoral map through a committee the government controls is not a departure. It is the pattern, applied to elections.

Political scientists have names for governments that keep the letter of the law while hollowing out the norms beneath it. The gentlest is *democratic backsliding*. The literature is blunt about how it works. Democracies rarely die of coups anymore. They die of this — one lawful, norm-breaking step at a time, each with a plausible reason, none of them illegal.

Canada spent sixty years building one norm above the rest: politicians do not draw their own districts. Voters choose their representatives. Not the reverse. In April 2026, Alberta set that norm aside with a single motion.

I worry about what the map buys. Replay the 2023 election-day vote and the minority map's geometry yields sixty seats of eighty-nine. That is not a mandate. It is insulation — from floor-crossings, from committees, from any opposition large enough to sustain scrutiny. I would worry about that insulation in any hands. I worry more in the hands of a government that has just shown me, in this very file, what it does with procedural control when a process threatens to answer back.

I could be wrong. Unlike most opinions about this government, mine ships with the terms of its own destruction. The panel's map went to the Assembly on November 2. The tests were locked before it existed. Were hearings held. Are the advisers named, their terms of reference published. Does the map honour the recommendation used to justify the takeover. Where does it land against a million neutral maps. If it lands inside the neutral bands, my suspicion took real damage — and this site will say so within seventy-two hours, because I committed to that before I knew the answer. That is the difference between an opinion and an accusation. An opinion tells you what would change it.

— Will Conner

## The Invisible Part

This audit ran into two data problems that have nothing to do with the commission and everything to do with how Alberta's electoral system is designed. Both can be fixed.

**Elections Alberta may already have the data to tell us where advance voters live. It doesn't publish it.** About half of all Alberta votes are now cast before election day: advance polls, mobile polls, special ballots. Elections Alberta reports these results as totals for each electoral division, not by specific Voting Area. So roughly 810,000 NDP and UCP votes cast in 2023 cannot be pinned to any neighbourhood on a map. They are counted; they just can't be located from the published results. The audit's all-ballots tests place them by estimate ([November](#november)). Our understanding is that every advance voter is checked against a voters list that ties each voter to a Voting Area. If so, the information exists at the moment of voting, and the published results do not carry it. Then no change to the voting process would be required, only a change to what EA reports.

In at least one community in northern Alberta, the gap is total. In the northern part of the Lesser Slave Lake division sits a Voting Area of 4,832 km², more than four-fifths the size of Prince Edward Island, where not one vote in 2023 was cast at an election-day poll; all came by mobile poll or special ballot. The votes from those 844 residents are counted in the divisional total but cannot be pinned to any location on a map. In the published election results, that community is invisible. Entirely.

When the commission first considered eliminating the Lesser Slave Lake division and merging it into a larger riding, it was working from population, not votes. In the end it kept the division, after many public submissions (the minority report counts at least 56), by invoking s. 15(2) of the *Electoral Boundaries Commission Act*: the provision that lets up to four ridings that meet at least three of five criteria (a large area, distance from Edmonton, no town of more than 8,000 people, an Indian reserve or Métis settlement, a stretch of provincial border) have populations as much as 50% below the provincial average. They got there. The published vote data still cannot show how those communities voted.

### Banff: the town and the park {#banff}

Here is the other side of that story. The whole commission's interim report folded Lesser Slave Lake into a larger riding; both final maps restored it and protected it under s.15(2). The minority used another protected slot on Rocky Mountain House–Banff Park, drawing its boundary through Banff National Park, a stretch the majority described as a place "where no one lives" (the audit's estimate: a few hundred residents). The name itself holds a clue. The minority calls that riding "Rocky Mountain House–Banff Park" — but the town of Banff is not in it. The Banff townsite, along with Canmore, sits in a neighbouring riding the minority names "Canmore-Kananaskis." The district that carries Banff's name is the park; the place where Banff's residents actually live was drawn into a different seat. The commission's majority, its own chair among them, called the move "a bad faith effort" to claim the legal protection, and that phrase is in the commission's official final report. A protection designed for remote communities, like those in Lesser Slave Lake, was used, on the minority's map, to defend a boundary through national-park wilderness. The communities it was designed to help had to fight for it through public submissions.

In fairness, there is a coherent geography under the odd name and the odd shape. It needs saying, because on a map the arrangement looks like an error and by name it sounds like one. Banff is unusual among Alberta towns: it sits *inside* a national park, on federal land it is not permitted to grow into. Of the people who live in Banff National Park, about nine in ten live in the townsite itself (8,305 in 2021); the park around it is federal land with few residents. Seen that way, grouping the townsite with Canmore, the next town down the Bow Valley, is a defensible community-of-interest choice: it keeps the valley's residents together in one seat and leaves the park as a separate, sprawling rural division. It is also why the boundary looks wrong on a screen. The line wraps tight around the town because the town is the only populated pocket for miles, and the riding carries a different name than the park because Alberta names many of its divisions for the land they cover, not the towns inside them. None of this dissolves the majority's objection; it pulls apart two questions that the shape on the map runs together. *Where* Banff's residents were placed has a sound logic, and that logic deserves credit. The majority's objection was aimed at something the placement does not touch, and it is the part that matters for this audit: in the commission's official final report, the majority called the minority's extension of the riding through the park "a bad faith effort" to claim a population protection — one written for remote communities where people actually live — for a boundary drawn through land it described as a place "where no one lives". The defensible geography of the town's placement does not answer it, and is not offered here as if it did. What does weaken it is the audit's own check: the park riding qualifies for the protection even without the extension through the park.

[**See it on the map ↗**](https://ixby.github.io/alberta-electoral-boundaries-audit/?poi=banff-town) — opens the explorer on Banff, where the town's boundary and the park riding to its north are both annotated.

**Alberta should draw its next maps on the most current census data available.** Canada counts its population every five years, and the wait between the count and usable map data is long. The 2026 census was taken in spring 2026, but Statistics Canada will not release usable data below the provincial level until roughly 2027 or 2028. The commission that drew the maps assessed in this audit had to start from the 2021 census, updated to mid-2024 with provincial estimates: the census already four years old when the maps were drawn, and potentially fourteen years old by the time the new boundaries retire. Fast-growing cities like Airdrie and Chestermere will change by 40% or more over that window. Rural communities will shrink. The map will be wrong from the day it is used. A straightforward change to the *Electoral Boundaries Commission Act* could require that any new commission be appointed only after Statistics Canada releases the most current dissemination-area data from the preceding census. The result: maps that reflect where Albertans actually live, not where they lived a decade ago.

Neither of these is a finding about the current commission's maps. They are observations about a system that makes accurate electoral analysis harder than it needs to be. Practical suggestions, offered as such. Not conclusions.

## Appendices: From Confusing to Clear {.new-page}

Each finding above links to one appendix here. Each appendix owns exactly one concept, starts from the honest confusion, builds the idea with one concrete example, and sends you back to the finding able to read its number unaided. Then it ends with what the concept *cannot* tell you. Read them in any order.

### Appendix A — What "cracking" actually does to a vote {#appendix-a}

*The confusion:* "Airdrie voters still vote. Nobody took their ballots. How does drawing lines 'dilute' anything?"

Imagine a town of 40,000 that mostly votes Orange, in a region that mostly votes Blue. Give the town its own riding and it elects an Orange MLA: 40,000 people, one seat, a voice. Now slice the town into four pieces and staple each piece to a chunk of Blue countryside. In each new riding, the town's 10,000 voters face 30,000 rural voters. Orange loses all four. Same voters, same ballots, zero seats. Nothing was stolen from any single voter; what was taken is the *community's* ability to elect anyone. That is cracking. Its mirror, packing (Appendix C), stuffs a group into as few ridings as possible, so it wins those overwhelmingly and nothing else.

Now re-read Finding 1: 85,805 people, four ridings, a minority in three of them. And the same commission's other draft shows two ridings were enough to satisfy the law.

*The limit:* cracking describes an effect, not a motive. A split can happen because someone wanted dilution, or because other constraints crowded the map. The geometry alone cannot say which. [Back to Finding 1](#finding-1)

### Appendix B — Shape analysis, and what s.15(2) protection is for {#appendix-b}

*The confusion:* "All electoral districts look weird. Why do these three shapes matter?"

Districts follow geography, so lumpy is normal. What shape analysis looks for is *work*: a boundary doing something no ordinary constraint explains. A corridor that lassoes one neighbourhood into a distant district. An arm reaching into a city's edge. A line run out through land where almost nobody lives. Alberta's law has a special provision, section 15(2), that lets up to four districts fall below the normal population floor. Past commissions have described it as being for vast, sparsely populated, remote districts, most of them in the north. The commission's majority, the chair among them, called the minority's extension of a boundary through national park "a bad faith effort" to qualify a district for that protection: using a rule written for people who live far apart to protect a line through land where almost no one lives. The audit's check undercut the charge: the district qualifies even without the extension.

Now re-read Finding 2: three shapes, flagged by the commission's own majority, confirmed when the audit inspected them.

*The limit:* a strange shape is a question, not an answer. The Banff section of this report shows how one of these shapes has a real logic underneath that deserves credit, and why that logic does not answer the majority's objection. [Back to Finding 2](#finding-2)

### Appendix C — Packing, in a three-district toy example {#appendix-c}

*The confusion:* "Bigger districts have more voters. So what? Everyone still gets one vote."

Take 12,000 voters, 7,000 Orange and 5,000 Blue, split into three districts of 4,000. Draw them evenly and Orange wins all three. Now redraw: cram 4,000 Orange voters into one district (Orange wins it 100–0), and spread the remaining 3,000 Orange across two districts of 4,000, where Blue's 5,000 give it the edge in both. Same 12,000 voters, same 7,000 Orange majority — but now Orange holds one seat and Blue holds two. That is packing: your opponents' votes get warehoused where they pile up as surplus, wasted on margins they didn't need. The tell is district *size and concentration*: your opponents' districts run big and deep, yours run lean and efficient.

Now re-read Finding 3: in 17 north, east and central Calgary districts, the minority's run 11.5% over the provincial average; the majority's, drawn in the same room, run 2.8%. That is the size half of the pattern. The depth half, big winning margins, is missing, so the audit finds no packing signature.

*The limit:* oversized districts can also come from honest guesses about growth, or from keeping a community whole. Lopsided sizes are the signature packing leaves, and also the signature several innocent choices leave. [Back to Finding 3](#finding-3)

### Appendix D — What MAD measures, and why the band exists {#appendix-d}

*The confusion:* "Population Mean Absolute Deviation — is this just statistics for 'the districts aren't identical'?"

Almost. Districts can't all hold exactly the same number of people; communities come in awkward sizes. MAD asks: on average, how far does each district stray from the ideal share? A MAD of 3,180 means districts miss the ideal by about 3,180 people on average; 4,707 means the misses average half again larger. The law tolerates straying (Alberta allows ±25%) because respecting real communities is worth some inequality. That's the bargain: deviation is the currency, and you spend it to buy things like whole towns and sensible boundaries. That is what gives Finding 4 its point: the minority map spends 48% more of that currency than the majority, and Finding 1 shows it *didn't* buy Airdrie's wholeness with it.

Now re-read Finding 4: 48% more deviation than the majority on the commission's own tables, on the one value the statute actually puts a number on. The "99 of every 100 neutral maps" figure holds only against neutral maps held to ±12.5%.

*The limit:* the law ranks nothing inside the band. "Spent more deviation" is an observation about priorities, not a violation; every district on both maps is legal. [Back to Finding 4](#finding-4)

### Appendix F — The what-if machine, and how to read a percentile {#appendix-f}

*The confusion:* "A million computer maps? A percentile of a simulation? What does that even mean?"

You can't judge whether a map is unusual without knowing what usual looks like, and for electoral maps nobody gets to see the alternatives. So the audit built them. An algorithm starts from a legal map and makes a long chain of random legal edits: merge two neighbouring districts, re-split them a random valid way, repeat — 252,500 times over, in four independent chains, 1,010,000 maps in all. No vote data is consulted while drawing; the machine cannot favour anyone, because it cannot see anyone. Then you take the real map, measure it, and ask: out of the million neutral maps, how many score this high? If 500,000 do, your map is ordinary. If 69 do, your map is the kind of map that neutral drawing produces 69 times in a million tries. One catch: each map in the chain is a small edit of the one before, so neighbouring maps are near-copies, and the million are worth about 1,000 independent draws (roughly 900 to 1,300, depending on the measure).

Now re-read Finding 6. The majority map at the 78th percentile: ordinary. The minority at the 99.99th: 69 of 1,010,000 score higher.

*The limit:* the machine respects population limits and keeps districts connected, but it does not weigh every fine point of the statute a real commission weighs. So its percentiles measure distance from *neutral drawing*, not from *everything a lawful commission might do*. And a percentile is a distance, not a verdict. Nor is it a claim that real districts are drawn at random: the computer maps are the nonpartisan alternatives a real map is compared with, nothing more. [Back to Finding 6](#finding-6)

### Appendix G — The efficiency gap, in one worked example {#appendix-g}

*The confusion:* "One number that captures gerrymandering? That sounds too convenient."

It is, slightly, which is why it gets a threshold and a miss in this report. The idea is that every vote that doesn't help win a seat is "wasted": all votes cast for losers, plus winners' votes beyond the 50%-plus-one they needed. In a fair map both parties waste about equally. The efficiency gap is the difference in wasted votes, as a share of all votes. Suppose Orange wins a 100-vote district 90–10: Orange wasted 39 (its surplus beyond 51); Blue wasted 10 (all of them for the loser). Packed and cracked maps make one party waste more, district after district. In this report's sign convention, positive gaps favour conservatives; the audit's Alberta calibration line (~4.1%) is the level only 5% of the million neutral maps cross.

Now re-read Finding 7: the minority at +4.0%, the 94.5th percentile. Below the line whose rule the audit set in advance, and reported as the miss it is.

*The limit:* the efficiency gap squeezes a whole map into one number, and one number can't tell a gerrymander from lumpy geography. That's why this audit uses four metrics and a locked threshold, and keeps the miss on the books. [Back to Finding 7](#finding-7)

### Appendix H — The prosecutor's fallacy, in plain language {#appendix-h}

*The confusion:* "One in 20,000, maybe one in 570,000, that this map is innocent. That's the finding, right?"

No, and the difference matters enough to have a name. The number says: *if* a neutral process drew Alberta's map, a map that looks like the minority map would turn up somewhere between once in 20,000 and once in 570,000 tries, depending on how the audit's statistical model is set up. That is P(this evidence, given innocence). It is not P(innocence, given this evidence), and swapping them is the prosecutor's fallacy. The classic example: a suspect matches a DNA profile found at a scene, and the profile occurs in one in a million people. "One in a million chance he's innocent"? Wrong. In a city of ten million, about ten people match; on the DNA alone, the suspect is one of ten — a 90% chance *someone else* is the source. How rare the evidence is under innocence is not the probability of innocence, because you also need to know how many innocent ways the evidence could arise.

Now re-read Finding 8: the audit states the number it can defend, the rarity of the map under neutral drawing, and refuses the swap that would turn it into a verdict.

*The limit:* refusing the fallacy costs the audit its most quotable sentence, and that cost is deliberate. Rarity is evidence. It is not, and never becomes, proof of intent. [Back to Finding 8](#finding-8)

### Appendix I — What a sampler is, and why swapping it matters {#appendix-i}

*The confusion:* "You checked your simulation with… another simulation? How is that a check?"

Because the two machines are built differently, and artifacts don't usually survive a change of machinery. Every map-drawing algorithm has quirks — the standard one (ReCom) has a documented lean toward compact, tidy districts, which could in principle exaggerate how extreme a sprawling real map looks. So the audit swapped the engine: the replacement grows districts from random spanning *forests* instead of trees, behaves measurably differently inside, and was registered publicly (OSF he53s) before any map of the registered run was drawn (a 500-step software test came first), with seeds derived from a public randomness beacon anyone can verify. If the minority's tail position were an artifact of the first engine's quirks, the second engine would move it. It moved less than one percentile point on every metric, which meets the registered rule for a "robust" verdict. But that run drew far fewer effective samples than registered, so the audit reads it as inconclusive at this run size. And this far into the tail a fraction of a point is a big change. The check shows the map stays far out, not exactly how far. The audit also reports the check that could not be run: whether Act I's flagged geometry is the specific mechanism behind the seat numbers. On the final data no independent plan reached the minority's seat value, so there was nothing to compare, and the claim is not made.

Now re-read Finding 9: three attacks. One pointed the same way but was too small to settle it, one survived, and one could not be run.

*The limit:* robustness rules out artifacts of the tooling. It cannot rule in design: a genuinely extreme map is genuinely extreme, whatever produced it. [Back to Finding 9](#finding-9)

### Appendix J — Why neighbours voting alike breaks naive statistics {#appendix-j}

*The confusion:* "A test gave p = 0.0024 and you threw it away? That looks like hiding a result."

The opposite: it's correcting one. Many tests assume each observation is independent, like separate coin flips. Voting areas aren't: neighbouring areas share demographics, incomes, histories, and they vote alike. Treating 100 clustered areas as 100 independent flips is really more like 20 informative observations wearing 100 coats, and it makes coincidences look impossible when they're merely uncommon. The fix is to build a null, a picture of what drawing without regard to party would produce, that keeps the clustering: shuffle *blocks* of neighbouring areas together, not each area on its own, and ask the question again. The audit's swing-zone test looked significant under the naive null (p = 0.0024) and unremarkable under the corrected one (p ≈ 0.19). The corrected one is coarse: nearly all the swing areas fall in one block, so it can say little either way. But once the flattering null is known to rest on a false assumption, its answer cannot stand, so you retire the result.

Now re-read Finding 10: two of the audit's own instruments retired, and one exploratory test the minority map passed.

*The limit:* the correction shows the *boundary-choice* signal can't be relied on; it says nothing against the ensemble findings, which never assumed independence in the first place. [Back to Finding 10](#finding-10)

### Appendix K — How to check a stated reason against public data {#appendix-k}

*The confusion:* "Commissioners are the experts. Who is an audit to grade their reasons?"

Nobody special, and that's the point: the check needs no special access. The minority report says Cochrane residents "move fluidly" into Calgary; Statistics Canada's journey-to-work tables (free, public) show about a third of Cochrane's workers commute into Calgary while roughly half work in Cochrane — a real tie, and one that says nothing about the specific Nolan Hill corridor drawn. The report says rural residents near Red Deer "go to school" in the city; the school-division maps (also public) put Sylvan Lake and Red Deer in different public divisions, though one Catholic division serves both. Each check follows the same recipe: take the stated reason, find the public dataset that would show it, and see whether the *particular line drawn* is the line the data supports. How many fall short at that last step depends on the rule. Against the reasons the minority actually stated, none of the six fails, and four are partly supported. Against the audit's own arithmetic on whether population forced the line, four fail. The tie is real. The line is not the one the tie points to.

Now re-read Finding 11: the same recipe, applied to six stated reasons.

*The limit:* a rationale that fails checking proves the *stated* reason doesn't fit. It cannot reveal the real one: sloppiness and pretext leave identical paperwork. [Back to Finding 11](#finding-11)

### Appendix L — How commissioners are appointed, and what nomination implies {#appendix-l}

*The confusion:* "Government-nominated commissioners drew the government-favouring map. Case closed, surely?"

Alberta's commission is built from nominations: a chair, plus commissioners nominated from the government and opposition sides. The design assumes partisan points of view and balances them, betting that a mixed room settles on one defensible map. From 1995 to 2017 the bet paid: even when a commissioner dissented, each commission sent the Assembly one map. This cycle the room split along its nomination lines, and the government's nominees produced the statistical outlier. The Legislative Assembly's appointment notice names Dr. Julian Martin and John D. Evans, KC, the minority report's authors, as "Nominated by the President of Executive Council" ([March 28, 2025](https://www.assembly.ab.ca/about/news-and-announcements/2025/03/28/electoral-boundaries-commission-to-soon-begin-consultations); copy at `evidence/legislature/assembly_news_ebc_appointed_2025-03-28.html`). That correlation is real and worth recording. It is also exactly where the inference must stop. Nominated commissioners are not agents; no evidence in this audit's record shows the government directed, coordinated with, or even knew the shape of its nominees' map before filing. History is full of nominees who disappointed the people who nominated them. The correlation invites a question. It cannot answer it, and the audit's instruments cut against the majority too when the data went that way, which is what symmetric measurement means.

Now re-read Finding 12: the split is the anomaly; the provenance is the question mark, not the answer.

*The limit:* who made the appointment is context, never proof. Treating it as proof is the same fallacy as Appendix H, wearing a political coat. [Back to Finding 12](#finding-12)

### Appendix M — The Act's process, commission to committee, end to end {#appendix-m}

*The confusion:* "How can the legislature just… take over redistricting? Isn't the commission's map the map?"

No. This is the surprise the whole episode rests on. Under Alberta's *Electoral Boundaries Commission Act*, the commission proposes; only the Legislative Assembly disposes. The commission's final report, hearings and submissions and revisions and all, is advice. It has no legal effect until the Assembly enacts a map, and the Act places no limits on what the Assembly does instead. No minimum debate. No notice period. No obligation to prefer the commission's work. No hearing requirement for whatever process follows. By its own motion, Government Motion 37, the Assembly referred the work onward to a select committee. Select committees of the Assembly are made up of MLAs, so MLAs, not an independent commission, oversaw the advisory panel that drew the new map. Nothing in the Act forbids that, as the audit reads it, and no court has ruled on it. In every cycle since 1995, the Assembly left the commissions' boundaries alone, changing at most a few names. It was that restraint, not the statute (which lets the Assembly approve a map "with alterations"), that kept politicians' hands off the pen.

Now re-read Finding 13: sixteen communities of hearings replaced by a body required to hold none. None of it forbidden by the Act, as the audit reads it.

*The limit:* this appendix explains what the law permits. Whether Albertans should accept what the law permits is a political question, the one the November tests are built to inform. [Back to Finding 13](#finding-13)

### Appendix N — Conventions versus law, and how norms erode {#appendix-n}

*The confusion:* "If politicians drawing their own districts violates our deepest electoral norm, how can it be legal?"

Because Canada's constitution runs on two operating systems. One is law: written, open to challenge in court, enforced by judges. The other is convention: unwritten rules of political behaviour, binding in practice, not enforced in a courtroom. The Supreme Court described the divide in the 1981 Patriation Reference: courts may *recognize* a convention and still decline to *enforce* it, because conventions are policed politically — by opposition, press, and voters — not by judges. Independent boundary commissions live on the convention side. Decades of practice, federally and in the provinces, built the rule that politicians do not draw their own ridings. Some jurisdictions (federally, and in Manitoba) give a commission's map effect without a vote of the legislature; Alberta's Act does not. So here the guardrail's whole strength was that governments kept choosing to honour it. A norm violated once, without consequence, is weaker for every government that follows. Of any party.

Now re-read Finding 14: real violation, fully documented, no convention a court will enforce — all three at once. A court can still review the map the process produces.

*The limit:* "unenforceable" is a statement about courts, not about consequences. Conventions are enforced at elections, by people who know what was done. Reports like this one exist so they know. [Back to Finding 14](#finding-14)
