# Section D — Procedural Audit

> **Status.** This is an early desk pass (April 2026). The current account of the process is `reports/academic/report_academic.md` §5.9. The comparator table in D1 and the Quebec 1992 and British Columbia 2008 paragraphs in D4 have no source filed under `evidence/` and are not relied on.

**Sanity-check preamble.** The cartographic question (Sections A, B, C) asks whether the maps themselves advantage one party. The procedural question asks whether the *process* that produced them — and the process that dispositioned them after tabling — met the fairness standards Canadian provinces have built up since *Reference re Provincial Electoral Boundaries (Saskatchewan)* [1991] 2 SCR 158. These are related but distinguishable: a fair process can produce flawed maps, and a flawed process can produce defensible maps. Treated in its own section.

## D1 — Independence of the drawing process

**The original commission.** Alberta's Electoral Boundaries Commission Act (s. 2(1)) establishes a five-member independent commission: the chair (appointed by the Lieutenant Governor in Council from eligible roles that include judges and retired judges), two government-nominated members, and two opposition-nominated members. The 2025–26 commission was constituted on this model and produced both the interim report (October 2025) and the final report (March 23, 2026), with the majority recommendation reflecting the chair plus the two opposition-nominated members and the minority recommendation reflecting the two government-nominated members.

The table below was compiled from general knowledge. It is unsourced and not relied on:

| Province        | Commission composition                                             | Appointment authority                            |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------ |
| British Columbia | 3 members, chair is a judge                                        | Judiciary + Lt-Gov in Council                   |
| Manitoba         | 3 members, chair is Chief Justice of Court of Queen's Bench       | Judiciary                                        |
| Ontario          | Follows federal Electoral Boundaries Readjustment Act process      | Federal appointment chain                        |
| Quebec           | Commission de la représentation électorale — 3 members, permanent | National Assembly and Chief Electoral Officer   |
| Saskatchewan     | 3 members, chair is Chief Justice                                  | Judiciary + Lt-Gov in Council                   |
| **Alberta**      | **5 members, chair appointed by the Lt-Gov in Council**            | **Lt-Gov in Council + gov/opp nominees (appointed by the Speaker)** |

The Alberta model is actually slightly *more* pluralistic than neighbouring provinces (BC, Saskatchewan) because it adds explicit partisan-nominated members on both sides rather than relying on the judiciary + Lt-Gov chain alone. This is a strength of the original Act: disagreements are expected and institutionalized.

**The April 2026 departure.** Government Motion 37 was put on notice on April 16, 2026, and moved, debated and passed on April 21. It set both commission reports aside and established a six-MLA committee chaired by Brandon Lunty (UCP) to oversee a new advisory panel, with a report-back date of November 2, 2026. An MLA committee with a government-caucus majority and a government-chair is a categorically different body from the independent commission. Under the standard Canadian framework:

- The commission's independence is what gives its output the legitimacy to bind the electoral map.
- A government-majority committee reviewing or overwriting the commission's output introduces partisan discretion at precisely the point where neutrality is most needed.
- The fact that a dissenting minority report exists does not, on its own, justify substituting the majority report with a government-drafted alternative. The dissent's purpose in the commission framework is to inform legislators and the public, not to displace the majority recommendation as the starting point for the legislature's review.

On the audit's understanding, the motion is not, by itself, a violation of the Act; the audit does not rule on legality. A commission report takes effect only through an Electoral Divisions Act passed by the Assembly, so the Assembly may enact a different map by ordinary legislation (s. 15 is the population-variance rule, not an amendment power). But it does represent a departure from the *spirit* of the commission-based process, where the legislature accepts or lightly amends the recommendation rather than replacing the entire map.

## D2 — Public input alignment

**Baseline.** The commission received **1,345 written submissions** (198 before the interim report and 1,147 after it). Submissions were published on the commission's website; the commission held 28 public hearings in 16 locations before the interim report, plus two virtual hearings, and further hearings after it (commission report p. 14).

**Majority report's claim.** The majority (the chair and the two opposition-nominated members) states that the minority's hybrid configurations for **Airdrie, Cochrane, Chestermere, Red Deer, and St. Albert had no public support in the consultation record** (final report, pp. 59 and 62). This is a checkable claim, because the submissions are public. A direct reading of the submissions finds the statement right in four of the five places and partly wrong on Chestermere, where three submissions support the minority's arrangement (EBC-2025-2-0384, 0811, 1091) and three oppose it (0687, 0785, 0787). In Red Deer no supporter was found.

**Interpretation.** For four of the five configurations, no clear public support was found in the submissions. The significance is that the standard defense of a disputed boundary is "the community asked for it"; that defense is unavailable for those four and partly available for Chestermere. The absence of public support does not by itself prove partisan intent, but it removes the most common non-partisan explanation for unusual boundary choices.

## D3 — Procedural fairness (interim → final)

**Interim report (submitted October 27, 2025).** The interim report is a consultation document, published for public comment before the final report.

**Final report (March 23, 2026).** Five months after the interim report, the commission split 3–2 with a majority and minority report. The majority largely extends the interim framework with adjustments responsive to the submissions; the minority departs from the interim framework in several specific ways — most notably the Airdrie 4-way split, the Nolan Hill-Cochrane hybrid, and the Rocky Mountain House-Banff Park extension documented in Sections A and C.

**The procedural-fairness question.** Did the minority report give the public fair notice of its departures before tabling? On a strict reading of the Act, the commission has no obligation to pre-publish minority positions — the public hearing process is between interim and final. But the departures from the interim framework that appear only in the minority (and not in the majority) were not tested in the public process. This means:

- The majority's ridings have been seen by the public, debated, and adjusted based on the submissions.
- The minority's departures have not.

Motion 37 did not adopt the minority's map. It set both reports aside and directed the new panel to incorporate the chair's Recommendation 5, which builds on the majority's map. The procedural-fairness concern is that the map which had been through the public process was set aside as the starting point. That concern is distinct from any partisan-bias concern.

## D4 — Override mechanism comparisons

Instances where Canadian provincial governments have altered or rejected independent boundary commission reports:

**Quebec 1992 (Ryan–Parizeau redistribution) — unsourced, not relied on.** The Commission de la représentation électorale's report in the early 1990s faced legislative amendment by the National Assembly before adoption. Amendments were limited in scope and did not replace the commission's map wholesale. Considered in retrospect as a stress test that the commission framework absorbed without structural damage.

**Ontario 1996 (Fewer Politicians Act).** The Harris PC government reduced Ontario's seat count from 130 to 103 by adopting federal (Chrétien-era) boundaries rather than running a provincial commission. This is a different class of action — adopting *another* independent commission's output, not substituting political judgment. (An earlier draft said the Supreme Court upheld the approach; that could not be sourced and has been dropped.) It did not establish a precedent for rejecting a provincial commission's recommendation outright.

**British Columbia 2008 (Campbell Liberals, North) — unsourced, not relied on.** The Campbell Liberal government initially rejected the Electoral Boundaries Commission's recommendation to consolidate Northern ridings and passed legislation retaining additional Northern seats. The issue was not partisan bias but a political commitment to Northern representation.

**Alberta April 2026 (current matter).** Where does Motion 37 fall on this spectrum?

- It resembles BC 2008 in that the rejection is public and the government has stated its reasons.
- It departs from BC 2008 in that the rejected recommendation had no public-commitment conflict: the majority's map works from the same ±25% variance framework, the same s.15(2) rules, and the same public submissions as the minority's.
- It resembles no prior precedent in that the replacement process is not a second independent commission or a return to an earlier map, but a government-majority MLA committee overseeing a new advisory panel.

This last point is the material difference. BC 2008 did not substitute a new government-controlled process — it modified specific recommendations via ordinary legislation. Alberta April 2026 is not the first instance of substituting a government-controlled drafting process for an independent commission's output: Alberta itself did so in 1992, when a select committee of MLAs drew the 1993 map after a deadlocked commission (Motion 24, July 2, 1992; the Court of Appeal criticised the result in 1994). Quebec's National Assembly also legislated its own 127-seat map on June 12, 2026, after the Supreme Court struck down its pause of the commission (*Lalande*, 2026 SCC 13); how that compares is not analysed here. If the November 2, 2026 report-back produces a map materially different from the commission's majority recommendation, the process departure becomes a substantive one rather than merely procedural.

## Symmetric Assessment

On procedural grounds, the commission itself operated normally: five members, standard appointment, an interim report published for comment, divided final — exactly the process the Act contemplates. The three commissioners who signed the majority and the two who signed the minority both acted within their role.

The procedural-fairness concern is not about the commission's work. It is about the **Assembly's April 2026 response** to the commission's work, on a government motion:

- Rejecting the majority report *as a starting point* rather than working from it with legislative amendments is a departure from historical Canadian practice.
- Creating a government-majority MLA committee to oversee the replacement process is without precedent among the cycles reviewed. MLAs last drew Alberta's map in 1992, but that followed a deadlocked commission whose five members filed five separate reports; in 2026 a commission's majority report was set aside (Amendment 5, Part 25).
- Setting aside the map that had been through the public process is a procedural-fairness concern. Motion 37 did not promote the minority's map: it builds on the majority's map through Recommendation 5, and in Question Period on April 16 the government did not defend the minority report (`findings/hansard_april16_motion19.md`).

This is distinct from the cartographic question. A government could override an independent commission with a legitimate-sounding alternative rationale (e.g., Northern representation in BC 2008) and that override would be procedurally concerning but not necessarily an attempt to manufacture partisan advantage. The audit found no evidence of coordination between the government and its nominees, and it draws no inference about motive.
