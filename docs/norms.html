Alberta Electoral Boundary Audit

What the Norms Ask of an Electoral Map

A plain-language guide · Not a ruling on anyone

Three questions run through this audit. The law asks what is allowed. The numbers ask what is unusual. This page asks what is customary: what Canada has come to expect of how a map gets made. That third question is the one people fight over. "The government broke a sixty-year norm" is not a measurement. It is the heart of the Opposition's charge. So this page does not just list the norms. It tests them: where they came from, what they are for, the best case for setting them aside, and what is left once each side's rhetoric is cleared away.

The norm, in one line

Politicians do not draw their own ridings. That is the norm: an unwritten rule, kept by practice, not by law. Canada spent about sixty years building it. From the federal reforms of the 1960s on, Ottawa and the provinces moved the job to boundary commissions that work at arm's length from the politicians. There were exceptions, including Alberta in 1992 and Quebec in 2026. The person who will run in a riding does not hold the pen that draws it.

The norm exists because of what came before it. Governing parties once drew their own ridings as a matter of routine. The conflict of interest is plain. Someone who has to win under a map has every reason to draw it in their own favour. And no one watching can tell an honest line from a self-serving one. A commission does not make maps perfect. It separates the person who draws the lines from the person who gains from them. That is what lets everyone else trust the result.

What the norm delivered this time

The commission that worked in 2025 and 2026 shows the norm at its best. It held hearings in sixteen communities. It received 1,345 written submissions, by its own count. It published a draft map, heard the criticism, and changed its mind. After many submissions (the minority report counts at least 56), it dropped its plan to remove the riding of Lesser Slave Lake. That is the system answering the public where everyone can see it. Whatever else the commission got wrong, the process around it worked as designed. It was public. It was on the record. And it answered to what Albertans said.

The break: MLAs take over the map

In March 2026 the commission split 3 to 2 and filed two maps. On April 21, 2026, the Assembly set both aside. Government Motion 37 gave the job to a committee of six MLAs: four UCP and two NDP, chaired by UCP MLA Brandon Lunty. The committee appointed an advisory panel of five, chaired by a retired Court of Appeal justice, to draw a 91-riding map. The committee did not have to hold public hearings, and it held none. The panel reported on October 22. No draft was published for the public to challenge. NDP members said the panel took no public submissions.

On May 6, 2026, before the panel's chair was named, Alberta's acting chief justice wrote to the committee. The courts, she said, would not circulate its request for a judge or give any input on the choice of chair. Her reason: "the irregularity of this process". She added that this passed no judgment on the legislation. The Alberta branch of the Canadian Bar Association then declined too. Both letters are reproduced in the Opposition members' part of the committee's report (pp. 15–16). The panel only advises. The committee's MLAs review its report and send it to the Assembly, where MLAs decide. At the committee's October 28 meeting, its UCP members voted 3–2 to end the review of the panel's report before NDP motions on it were considered. They also voted 3–2 against an NDP motion to disclose what the panel and its consultants were paid (committee transcript, pp. CEB-73 and CEB-74). The committee defeated an NDP motion to publish whom the panel consulted. A UCP member cited privileged legal advice and the panel's independence (p. CEB-75). The committee did not meet again. It had to send the panel's report to the Assembly by November 2.

In May, Duane Bratt, Professor of Political Science at Mount Royal University, described the move to me, in private correspondence, as without precedent in Canada. I later found one earlier case, Alberta's own, in 1992. I treat it as a different case, and that distinction is mine, not his. In 1992 the commission could not agree on a map, and MLAs drew one; the law page tells that story in full. In 2026 a majority of the commission signed a report, which the Act treats as the commission's report. It was set aside anyway. What that means is where the argument starts.

Recommendation 5: the one the government cited

To justify giving the job to a committee, the government cited one recommendation in the commission's report: Recommendation 5. One person wrote it: the commission's chair, Justice Dallas Miller, in an addendum to the majority report. He was "reasonably certain" that with 91 seats no riding outside Edmonton and Calgary would be lost. He added: "My majority colleagues do not agree with me on this point. That is why I am alone in making this recommendation" (final report, p. 66). He also said why he wrote it: it "is formulated for the express purpose of dissuading the Legislature from accepting the minority report." So it was the first thing I checked the panel's map against. The result for each part is below.

It is a fallback, and a narrow one. The commission's majority removed two ridings outside Edmonton and Calgary. If the Legislature would not accept that, the chair asked it to change the Electoral Divisions Act from 89 seats to 91, bring those two ridings back, and keep "the rest of the province as we propose" (the majority map) "to the extent possible." Bringing back the two ridings should:

  • (a) Leave Airdrie and the south alone. Change no riding in Airdrie, and none with land south of Airdrie's southern city limit, except Drumheller-Stettler. On the majority map that is 41 ridings: both Airdrie seats, all 28 Calgary seats, and the 11 ridings around and south of Calgary. Result: not met. The panel's map changes 10 of the 41. By their names on the majority map, they are Calgary-Bow, Calgary-West-Elbow Valley, Canmore-Banff, Cochrane-Springbank, High River-Vulcan-Siksika, Lethbridge-East, Lethbridge-West, Livingstone-Macleod, Okotoks-Diamond Valley and Taber-Cardston.
  • (b) Leave north Edmonton alone. Change no Edmonton riding north of the North Saskatchewan River: 11 ridings on the majority map. Result: met. All 11 are unchanged.
  • (c) Put south Edmonton back. Every Edmonton riding south of the river goes back to the commission's October 2025 draft map, the one it published for comment before the final split. The committee may add Nisku to Edmonton-South East if it wants Leduc and Beaumont back in one riding. That is 10 ridings. Result: not scored. My check needs the draft map's lines, and no file of them exists. The draft's population table matches 9 of the 10 to the person, and the tenth is the Nisku option. That is strong evidence, not proof, that the panel followed it.
  • (d) Consider a Clearwater seat. Consider bringing back a riding made mostly of Clearwater County and western Mountain View County, with s.15(2) status. That is the Act's exception that lets up to four remote ridings have as many as 50% fewer people than average. This is the only restored seat the recommendation places. It names no place for the second. Result: adopted. Rocky Mountain House-Sundre has 61% of its people in that area.

On my check, the panel's map does not meet Recommendation 5, because it fails condition (a). These results are provisional: the official map files are not public yet, so I rebuilt the panel's lines from its report. Airdrie, Calgary, Cochrane and Chestermere all fall under condition (a). That is where the commission's minority split Airdrie four ways and drew ridings that join parts of Calgary to the countryside. Red Deer and St. Albert are covered only by the softer clause, "to the extent possible." Together, the conditions cover most of the ground the commission split over. A map that reopens Airdrie or Calgary may still be lawful. But it is not the map Recommendation 5 describes. Motion 37 itself lets the panel apply the recommendation "subject to any modifications the independent advisory panel considers necessary." The map explorer outlines the area under each of the four conditions, and each one's pin says whether Recommendation 5 changes it or leaves it alone.

The best case for setting the norm aside

A check that only repeats one side's outrage is not checking anything. So here is the government's case at its strongest, put as well as I can put it, before I test it:

  • The commission failed at its one job. A commission is meant to give one answer, at arm's length. This one gave two maps that could not both be used, and its majority publicly accused the minority of bad faith. When the independent route splits, someone has to decide. The law says who.
  • The law gives the Legislature the pen, on purpose. Under Alberta's Act, a commission's report has no legal force. Ridings change only when the Assembly passes an Electoral Divisions Act. Motion 37 used a door the law built on purpose. Nothing was smuggled in.
  • MLAs answer to voters; commissioners answer to no one. The oldest argument for letting the Legislature control the map is accountability. An MLA who draws a self-serving map can be voted out. A commissioner cannot.
  • The committee is not purely partisan. Two of its six members are NDP MLAs. An advisory panel drew the map. That panel included two members nominated by the Opposition, and a retired Court of Appeal justice chaired it. It split too: its report includes a minority opinion.
  • The government cited the chair's own recommendation. Its stated reason for giving the committee a 91-seat job was Recommendation 5, written by the commission's chair himself. That recommendation says its change “would need to be studied by an all-party Select Special Committee or other equivalent Legislative Committee” (p. 66). And the government told the Legislature exactly that: “We are adopting the recommendations from the report” (Government House Leader, Hansard, April 16, 2026).

Where that case gives way

Each of those points is real. The record also pushes back on each one:

  • The split explains replacing the maps. It does not explain shutting out the public. Recommendation 5 proposed an all-party committee to study a two-seat fix. Motion 37 used that kind of committee for a much wider redraw. A rule requiring hearings, a published draft, or a panel whose map was final would also have settled the split. The route chosen gives up every openness safeguard that studies comparing countries treat as protective. The split forced none of those losses.
  • "The law allows it" is a question for the law page, and it is answered there: the Act sets no rule for what the Assembly does if it does not approve a commission's report. The norms question is different. In Quebec a commission's map takes effect without the National Assembly's final approval. In Alberta, as in most provinces, it takes a bill, so the governing party always controls whether any commission map becomes law. What is lawful and what is customary are different questions.
  • The accountability argument proves too much. It is an argument against boundary commissions everywhere. Yet Canada, the UK and Australia moved the other way, with rare exceptions such as Alberta in 1992 and Quebec in 2026. They did so because elections are weak protection against a map designed to shape those same elections.
  • The panel drafts. A committee with a government majority reviews the draft and sends it on. The Assembly's majority decides. Advice from both sides, with the decision in the government's hands, is a different thing from an independent commission. That difference is the norm at issue.
  • The chair wrote Recommendation 5 to talk the Legislature out of adopting the minority map, and his majority colleagues did not endorse it. It is also narrow. It leaves Airdrie, Calgary and everything south of them as the majority drew them, except Drumheller-Stettler. Those are the very lines the commission split over, and nothing in the committee's mandate held it to them. Citing a recommendation against its author's stated purpose leaves a tension on the record. It does not resolve it.
  • The government also read page 58 of the majority report into the record. That page regrets the cap on the number of seats. But the remedy it proposes is for the Legislature to let future commissions set the number of seats. Motion 37 took that freedom in this cycle, for a committee of MLAs, while turning down the report's actual request: adopt the 89-seat map.

Whose charge is it?

This page exists to separate fact from judgment. These are documented facts. The break happened. I found no earlier case of a commission's majority report being set aside; Alberta's 1992 committee followed a commission with no majority. The openness safeguards were given up. In a public report the law requires, the commission's majority called one minority boundary a "bad faith effort". That is the majority's charge, which my own check undercuts (finding 2 in the full record). The Canadian Civil Liberties Association publicly raised the concern that the map was being politicized. The Opposition voted against the motion. And the charges run both ways. The minority report charges “the Majority’s inclination to delete or mangle rural electoral divisions and, in effect, reduce effective rural representation”. Rural representation, in the minority's own words, is “not merely a question of numbers; it is a question of access, voice, and practical governability” (p. 356). I tested that charge the day I found it. The test was added later, not set in advance, and its method and every riding's label are published with the finding. The result runs the other way. The minority map keeps fewer ridings centred on rural areas than the majority map: 23 against 28, where the 2019 map has 31. It also folds more of the 2019 map's rural ridings into ridings named for cities: seven against three. Both maps thin rural representation compared with 2019, so the charge's core observation is real. But on the measure the charge names, it lands on its author. Its companion charge was tested the same day, and it holds. It says the majority would not use the Act's room to let a riding sit up to 25% below or above the average population, room that could give rural ridings fewer people. The majority drew its rural ridings near the average. The minority drew them with fewer people. One charge reversed, one supported, both published. I discount the majority's complaints about the minority map because they come from one side of a split commission. The same discount applies here, in both directions.

What follows is not a documented fact. It is a political judgment: that the break was illegitimate. That judgment turns on how much respect the norm deserved once the commission had split, and reasonable people weigh that differently. I document the break and what it cost. I do not adopt the Opposition's conclusion, and nothing in the audit's statistics can carry it either. A number can say a map is rare. It cannot say a process was wrong.

Norms are not law, and one province made the norm law

Could a court enforce the norm? Not while it stays a norm. Unwritten rules of political conduct, which lawyers call constitutional conventions, bind politicians, not courts. The Supreme Court said so in the 1981 Patriation Reference, and courts have held that line since: a court may name a convention and still decline to enforce it. That gap, between what is customary and what a court can order, is where this whole episode lives.

Quebec shows the other choice. It wrote the norm into law: its boundary commission is permanent and independent. When Quebec's government passed a law to pause its commission, the courts did act, because a law was then in play. On April 22, 2026, the Supreme Court of Canada upheld, 7 to 2 and ruling from the bench, the finding that the pause broke the voting rights in section 3 of the Charter. Its reasons followed on May 1 (Quebec (AG) v. Lalande, 2026 SCC 13). Where the norm was written into law, a court could enforce it. Alberta's norm was never written into law, so no court can enforce it as a norm. A court can still review a boundaries law once one passes. The contrast cuts both ways, though. Even in Quebec, on June 12, 2026, the National Assembly passed a law setting its own 127-seat map.

The process scorecard: putting the norms question to a test

Talk about norms is cheap on both sides. My answer is a third scorecard. The tests and statistics elsewhere on this site measure the maps. This scorecard measures the process, on nine safeguards that the sixty-year practice provided. Each is a yes-or-no question that public documents can answer, and the same nine score every process this audit examines.

Another way to see this scorecard: it adds a third dimension. The audit's chart places each map on two: how its lines are drawn, and how it turns votes into seats. All three maps on that chart so far (the commission's majority and minority maps and the 2019 map) came from commission processes, which score nine of nine here by design. So the third dimension has had nothing to tell apart. The panel's map is the first in the audit with a different process score behind it.

Read the table fairly, in both directions. The commission scores nine of nine by design. The safeguards describe the institution the norm built, and the commission was that institution. The telling part was up to the committee. Four of its No answers were safeguards it could have added before it reported: hearings, a published draft, new submissions, and the response to the public that those make possible. It added none of them. It ended its review on October 28 and held no further meetings.

How firm this is. The scorecard's safeguards, sources and expected directions were registered on the OSF (u47bq, July 14, 2026), a public site that dates and freezes a research plan. That was months before the committee reported. I wrote it partway through the episode, after the findings you have just read. That is a risk of bias, and the registered document says so openly. Signed corrections (Amendment 5, Part 25, November 1, before any committee map was seen) fix some of its facts, such as the committee's size. They change no safeguard and no result, and the OSF text is unchanged. Applied backward to the commission, the scorecard describes; it does not predict. With the panel's map tabled, the map tests run as written in June and amended in November, and this scorecard rates the process that produced the map. So the norms debate stops being a shouting match about tradition. It becomes a scored comparison: did the committee act like the institution it replaced?

The audit's standing rule

The same rule holds everywhere on this site. The audit records what happened, how rare it is, and who claimed what. Whether the norm deserved to survive the split, and what setting it aside should cost at the ballot box, if anything, is for readers and voters to decide. Not for a statistical audit.
