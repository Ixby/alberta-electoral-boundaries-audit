     7. The Legislative Assembly's response to the 2025-2026 Alberta Electoral Boundaries Commission
            Report was not irregular, unprecedented, or even surprising, given the provisions of the Electoral
            Boundaries Commission Act, which are not unlike those in electoral boundaries legislation
            elsewhere in Canada. Alberta, Saskatchewan, B.C., P.E.I. and Nova Scotia's legislation expressly
            provides the Legislature the power to reject electoral boundaries commission's recommendations.
            In the Yukon, it appears that the Legislature itself determines electoral boundaries. In
            Newfoundland and in the Northwest Territories, after tabling an electoral boundaries commission's
            report, the report may be debated by their respective Legislatures. Recently, in the Northwest
            Territory's electoral boundaries commission's recommendations were rejected by the Legislature.

     8. It is true that in a number of provinces, the government must table a bill implementing the
            commission's recommendations. What is not clear, likely deliberately, is whether such bills are
            subject to the usual debate and possible amendment to which all proposed legislation is subject.

     9. New Brunswick's legislation and that of the federal Parliament provide for a process whereby a
            certain number of MLAs or MPs can file written objections to an electoral boundaries commission's
            recommendation. Such objections require the electoral boundaries commissions to reconsider
            their recommendations; although in the end, the electoral boundaries commissions' "rulings" on
            such objections appear to be binding on the New Brunswick Legislature and Parliament.

     10. At least two Supreme Court justices have opined that there is no one right or correct way to
            determine electoral boundaries. Differences of opinions are to be expected.

                  Coté and Rowe, J.J. in Quebec (A.G.) v. Lalande 2026 SCC 13

                  "...when it comes to electoral representation, `there are dozens, even hundreds of plausible
                  solutions, each with its own advantages and disadvantages. Giving effect to the right to
                  effective representation of all electors necessarily involves balancing various factors and
                  reconciling competing interests. Each conceivable alternative will have an impact on the
                  electoral map such that it is impossible to identify a single `correct solution'..."

     11. There is a right in every citizen not to have his or her vote unduly diluted, although some dilution is
            inevitable. However, there is no right in the citizen to not have his or her vote diluted, so long as it is
            not unduly diluted and the reason for the dilution is to give the citizen in question or other citizens
            better representation.

     12. Hybrids have been sanctioned by the Courts. "We accept the reasonableness of `hybrid areas' ": the
            Alberta Court of Appeal in 1991 (citation above). The Court of Appeal was commenting on a provision
            in the 1990 Electoral Boundaries Commission Act, which actually mandated hybrids for Red Deer,
            Medicine Hat, St. Albert and Grande Prairie. However, public resistance to hybrids however was noted
            by the Court of Appeal in its subsequent electoral boundaries decision three years later:

                  "The immediate cause of the problem faced by the Legislature in 1993 was the conviction,
                  by the Commission and by the Committee, and by most of the parties before us, that
                  the people of Alberta simply would not accept the idea that agrarian and non-agrarian
                  populations would both feel adequately represented in the same constituency. We
                  accept this sentiment as a dramatic example of the importance of the idea of effective
                  community representation."

Regardless, the use of hybrids has continued unabated since that observation was made some 30-plus
years ago. Elsewhere in this report we explain the many reasons why hybrid electoral divisions are necessary.
