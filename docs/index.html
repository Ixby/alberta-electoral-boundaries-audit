Alberta's Electoral Map, Audited

A proposed new electoral map for Alberta, with 91 ridings, is before the Legislature. I checked it, and the two maps that came before it, against tests I wrote down in advance.

Where things stand. An advisory panel drew the new map. A committee of six MLAs sent it to the Legislature on Monday, November 2, 2026. It becomes law only if MLAs pass a bill. My results for it are provisional, because the official map files are not public yet. I will score those files within 72 hours of their release.

The short answer #

The new map. On the tests I fixed in advance, the panel's 91-seat map does not repeat the lean toward the UCP that I found in one of the commission's maps. It sets off 2 of 5 flags on how its lines are drawn, and 1 of 4 on how it turns votes into seats. A match needed 3 of 5 and 2 of 4. These results are provisional: the official map files are not public yet, so I rebuilt the lines from the panel's report.

Three things still deserve a look. First, the 50-50 test. Shift every riding's vote by the same amount until the province splits exactly in half, then count seats. The panel's map gives the UCP 44 of 91, or 48.35%. Maps a computer draws without seeing a vote usually give the UCP fewer than half the seats in a tie (44.8% for the typical one), because rural UCP wins are wider than urban NDP wins. 48.35% is more than 98.8 in every 100 of those maps give, on 2023 election-day ballots. Against maps with the same 91 ridings, it is more than 90 to 97 in 100. Second, in Lethbridge and Banff the panel's population reasons do not match its own numbers. Against the panel minority's own alternative map, those changes cost the NDP two seats on 2023 election-day ballots. Counting all ballots, the registered count gives one, and a newer rule for placing early ballots, not yet adopted, gives none. Each is too close to call: the winning margin is smaller than the votes along the lines in question. Third, Recommendation 5, the one recommendation the government cited, asked that the 41 ridings in Airdrie and south of it stay as the commission's majority drew them. The panel's map changes 10 of them.

The map that started it. In March 2026 the independent commission split 3 to 2 and filed two maps. I compared both with 1,010,000 maps a computer drew without seeing a vote. The majority's map looks like them. The minority's map leans toward the UCP further than almost any. Split the vote 50-50 and only 69 of them give the UCP a larger share of seats, and only by a hair. The Legislature adopted neither map.

The process. The commission held public hearings and published a draft. The committee and its panel held no public hearings on the new map and released no draft. As I read the law, nothing forbids that. It does depart from a practice Canada built over about sixty years: an independent body draws the map, at arm's length from the MLAs who will run in it.

What happens next. MLAs decide whether the map becomes law. A court challenge can come only after a boundaries law passes. When the official files are out, I will rerun every test within 72 hours and post the result here, whichever way it points.

What I can say, and what I can't

  • ✓I can say the commission's minority map sits far outside the range of the computer-drawn maps. On four fairness measures combined, none of the 1,010,000 is as far out in the UCP's favour; on a rank-based version, seven are. One at a time, three of the four put it at the far edge (election-day ballots).
  • ✓All four of those measures lean the same way, toward the UCP.
  • ✗That any commissioner meant to do this. My tests measure what a map does. They can't show motive.
  • ✗That the government directed its nominees. I found no evidence of coordination. Appointing someone is not evidence of it.
  • ✗That the pattern will come back. The map that has it was never enacted, and on provisional results the panel's map does not repeat it.
  • ✗How a court would rule, or what the Assembly will enact.

Who drew the maps #

Alberta redraws its ridings every so often. A riding is the area that elects one MLA. Where the lines fall can decide who wins. Three bodies handled the new map, in this order: the commission, the committee and the panel.

The commission. The independent Electoral Boundaries Commission has a chair, who is a judge, and four members: two nominated by the Premier and two by the Opposition. On March 23, 2026 it split 3 to 2. The chair and the Opposition's two nominees signed the majority map. The Premier's two nominees signed the minority map (Legislative Assembly of Alberta, March 28, 2025). Both maps fit the Act's number rules. The majority argued the minority's map would likely fail the Charter's test; no court has ruled.

The commission worked in public. It held hearings in sixteen communities and received 1,345 written submissions. After many asked (the minority report counts at least 56), it dropped its plan to remove the Lesser Slave Lake riding.

The committee. The Legislature adopted neither map. It gave the job to a committee of MLAs instead.

Four weeks after the split, Government Motion 37 (put on notice April 16; moved, debated and passed April 21) created the Select Special Committee on Electoral Boundaries, with six MLAs:

  • four UCP: Brandon Lunty (chair), de Jonge, Sawyer and Wiebe;
  • two NDP: Ganley and Gray.

The panel. The committee appointed an Independent Advisory Panel to draw a 91-seat map. Its chair is retired Court of Appeal justice Brian O'Ferrall, nominated by the Premier. Its members are the Hon. Monte Solberg and Darwin Durnie, nominated by the Premier, and Dr. Gerard Kennedy and Brent Robinson, nominated by the Opposition (committee report, p. 9). The panel split too: its minority offered an alternative map. The panel only advises. MLAs decide.

  1. March 23, 2026. The commission splits 3 to 2 and files two maps.
  2. April 21. Government Motion 37 sets both maps aside and creates the committee of six MLAs.
  3. June 2 and 9. The committee appoints the panel: first its chair, then four members.
  4. October 22. The panel reports its 91-seat map. On October 28 the committee votes 3 to 2 to end its review without adding any observations of its own.
  5. November 2. The committee sends the panel's report to the Legislature. Next, MLAs decide whether to pass a bill.

As I read the Act, nothing in it forbids these steps, and no court has ruled on them. But this time the MLAs who oversee the map will also run in it. And a future government of any party can point to this as a precedent.

November: the panel's map, checked #

These results are provisional. The official map files are not public, so I rebuilt the panel's lines from its report. I call that my reconstruction. Its first two accuracy checks failed: 49.0%, then 13.8%, of voting areas landed in the wrong riding, against a 10% limit. A rule change (Amendment 10) allowed a third try. It passed, but only as a repeat run on pages already seen, not as a fresh test. I will rerun everything on the official files.

The tests set in advance (run November 4). Their rules were amended seven times from November 2 to 7: four times before I scored the panel's map, three times after I had seen results (findings/pre_registration_amendment_log.md).

  • How the lines are drawn: 2 of 5 flags. A match with the commission minority's map needed 3. Not repeated.
  • How votes turn into seats: 1 of 4 flags. A match needed 2. Not present. The one flag is the 50-50 test, at the 98.8th percentile: higher than 98.8 in every 100 computer-drawn maps, on election-day ballots. The other three measures sit in the normal range.
  • Recommendation 5: the panel changed 10 of the 41 ridings it asked to leave alone, so the map does not follow it on my registered test.

So, on this provisional reading, the panel's map does not carry the commission minority map's pattern on either half.

The panel's own reasons (finding 15). Where a change moves no seat, they mostly hold. In Lethbridge and Banff they do not. The panel said Lethbridge “already has population to spare”, yet split it four ways while Red Deer, about the same size, kept two city ridings. Banff's riding was at most about 1,300 people short; the panel added 8,901. Against its own minority's alternative map, the panel's map gives the NDP 2 fewer seats on election-day ballots. On all ballots it gives 1 fewer on the registered count, and none on the newer rule P for early ballots, which is not yet adopted. Each seat that differs is too close to call: its margin is smaller than the votes in the areas where the two lines differ.

Which ballots count (November 7). Election-day ballots leave out about half of 2023's ballots: the early ones, cast in advance, by mobile poll, by special ballot or anywhere. Elections Alberta reports those only for each old riding. On the 2019 map, election-day ballots alone elect 57 UCP members; the real 2023 result was 49. Early ballots leaned 6.2 points more NDP than election-day ballots, and more NDP in 83 of 87 ridings. So I changed the rule after seeing some results (Amendment 12): every vote-based test uses all ballots, with election-day results kept beside them. That change was not blind. Where early ballots fall inside a new riding is an estimate. A newer rule, rule P, places them only among people who did not vote on election day, using Alberta data only. It is written up as draft Amendment 14, which I have not signed. It was chosen after its effect on the maps was known, so it is exploratory and not blind. Until it is signed, the registered counts stand, and rule P's are given beside them.

  • The panel's map gets the same counts either way: 2 of 5 and 1 of 4.
  • The commission minority's map would move: on all ballots its efficiency gap is +2.55%, the 98.8th percentile, above the new line of +1.39%, and its count on how votes turn into seats would read 4 of 4 flags, not 3 of 4. That rests on how early ballots are placed. On the newer rule P, not yet adopted, its gap is +5.64% and its seats 57 to 32; against the computer-drawn maps that gap ranks at the 99.98th percentile. This does not reopen finding 7.
  • Comparison sets rerun (November 8): the smaller sets of computer-drawn maps with 91 and 89 ridings were rerun on all ballots; the main 1,010,000 maps were not. Two runs that did not repeat exactly were dropped (Amendment 13, also written after results were seen). The reading did not change: the only flag is still the city-versus-rest test in the narrowest set.

The newer checks, run after November 4 and not set in advance, look at seats, Lethbridge, city limits and rural voters. Of 132 comparisons, 12 fall outside the usual range; none holds up once the number of tests is counted. They are in the full record, after finding 15.

The commission's two maps #

The majority map looks like what drawing with no aim produces. On the measures that use votes, the minority map does not. The first two points compare the maps with each other; the last two use votes.

  • The minority map splits Airdrie four ways. Outside Airdrie East, Airdrie's residents are outnumbered in every riding they land in.
  • It makes ridings in north-east and central Calgary bigger, so each vote there counts for a little less.
  • Split the vote exactly 50-50 and it gives the UCP 46 of its 89 seats (51.7%): a majority of seats without a majority of votes. Only 69 of 1,010,000 computer-drawn maps give more, and all 69 give 45 of 87 (51.72%), just above it.
  • Replay the 2023 election on election-day ballots, and the minority map gives the UCP 60 of 89 seats; the majority map, 55. Count all ballots and the registered count is 55 and 48. A newer way of placing early ballots, rule P, gives 57 and 48. Rule P is not yet adopted, and both counts are partly an estimate, because early ballots have to be placed inside the new ridings.

Airdrie. Airdrie has 85,805 people (2024 municipal census) and one city council. The law caps each riding at one and a quarter times the provincial average, so Airdrie needs at least two ridings. The majority map gives it two.

The minority map gives it four. Airdrie East takes more than half the city. Most of the rest goes to two ridings shared with Calgary, where Calgary residents outnumber Airdrie's; about 350 people go to a rural riding. Splitting a community so thinly that it can't decide any seat is called cracking. My cracking test was added later: I wrote its rules in the same session that first ran it. It needs Airdrie's residents outnumbered in every piece. Airdrie East fails that, so by the test's own rule this is not cracking. The majority map's two-way split is not cracking either.

Calgary's north-east and centre. The minority map's seventeen ridings here average 11.5% more people than the provincial average; the majority map's, 2.8%. (On a literal reading of how the zone is defined, 8.5% to 8.9% against 1.3% to 1.6%. The zone's list was first written as NDP-competitive ridings against UCP-dominant ones, and later described by geography, with the same ridings.) A bigger riding means each vote counts for less. Crowding one party's voters into a few lopsided wins is called packing, but my packing test, also written in the same session that first ran it, does not find it: the NDP wins eight of the seventeen by margins below the provincial average.

Population spread. Equal populations are the one value the law puts a number on. On the commission's own tables, the minority map's ridings miss the ideal size by 4,707 people on average, the majority's by 3,180. On 2021 census numbers the averages are close, but the minority map's middle riding is still further off (3,938 against 2,827). On the numbers the commission itself used, the defence that odd lines were needed to balance populations does not work here.

Hybrid ridings join part of a city to rural areas. Counting a riding as hybrid when 5% to 95% of its people live in the city, the minority map has six at Calgary's limit and the majority map none.

Seats. On 2023 election-day ballots, shifting every riding by the same amount, the minority map keeps the UCP at the 45 seats it needs for a majority even if its share of the UCP and NDP vote falls to about 49.2%. On the majority map it would need to win that vote by about 9 points.

How I checked #

Maps drawn by a computer

A computer drew 1,010,000 maps of Alberta from the official files, each with 87 ridings, the 2019 number. Every riding is close to the same population and in one piece. The computer never sees a vote, so these maps show what lines drawn with no aim look like. That is all “neutral” means here. Maps drawn with 89 ridings show the same lean; those results are provisional.

Why a tie does not mean half the seats

Rural UCP voters win their ridings by wide margins, about 70-30. Urban NDP voters win by closer ones, about 60-40. So in a 50-50 tie the typical computer-drawn map gives the UCP only 44.8% of seats. That is the starting point.

What the odds mean

How often would drawing that never sees votes produce the minority map's mix of results on all four measures? Rarer than 1 in 10,000, on every way I computed it. On the published measure no computer-drawn map is as far out (on a rank-based version, seven are), so the figure comes from a statistical model: a model estimate, not a bound. It uses election-day ballots. No all-ballots version exists, because the computer-drawn maps' all-ballots scores are not in the repository.

That figure is not the chance the map was drawn in good faith. A map this rare can still come from unusual but honest choices. My claim is narrower: drawing that follows the rules and never sees votes almost never produces it.

What didn't hold up

Some tests came back against my findings. They stay on the page.

  • The test of following town and city lines came back neutral. The minority map follows them 72% of the time and the majority map 80%. The 2019 map, drawn by the last commission, scores 75%.
  • The efficiency gap missed. The minority map came in at +4.0%, the 94.5th percentile. The line set in advance was the 95th percentile, about +4.1%, so I report it as a miss.
  • The test of whether the odd shapes cause the seat advantage could not be run. No computer-drawn map in its planned run reaches the minority map's seats, so there was nothing to compare. The link between the shapes and the seats is untested.

What the law requires, and what it doesn't #

Section 3 of the Charter gives every citizen the right to vote. The Supreme Court of Canada reads it as a right to effective representation: votes of roughly equal weight, with room for real reasons such as geography or community, as long as each gap is justified. Canadian law has no rule against partisan advantage as such.

About the word. Gerrymandering means drawing lines to give a party an advantage. Canadian law has no offence by that name, so no court will rule on gerrymandering as such. That doesn't mean it can't happen here. I measure what maps do; I don't call any of them a gerrymander.

  • The Legislature doesn't have to adopt a commission's map. A map becomes law only if MLAs pass a bill.
  • Courts don't review a law while it's being made. A challenge can only come after a boundaries law passes.

Once a new map becomes law, it can be challenged in court on the same test: effective representation, with every population gap justified. Reasons matter. I score the map and the process. Whether it is constitutional is for a court to say.

What would change my mind #

Each main finding has a written condition that would withdraw it, most set on April 24, 2026. If one is met, I will publish the retraction within 48 hours of learning of it, as the project's rule says.

  • Someone shows the trade-off was forced. If no legal map can meet the minority's stated reasons at Airdrie, Cochrane, Nolan Hill and Rocky Mountain House–Banff Park while following town and city lines as often as the majority map, I'll retract the finding that those reasons don't explain those lines. The challenge is open at Issue #14.
  • A document shows the reasons came first. If an internal commission document, dated before the minority's final choices, shows those choices were driven by community submissions I haven't seen, I'll retract the inference that they were drafting decisions.
  • The neighbour-drain pass turns out to be an artefact. If the continuous version of that score falls in the extreme upper tail (p < 0.05) of random permutations, I'll withdraw the minority's pass.
  • An election contradicts the computer-drawn maps. If the 2027 election, on whatever map becomes law, gives a seat split that contradicts what 2023 votes predict on that map, I'll revisit the percentile findings.
  • A court sets Lalande apart. The Supreme Court struck down Quebec's law pausing its commission (Quebec (AG) v. Lalande, 2026 SCC 13). If a court finds Alberta's route different in law, I'll retract the argument on the norms page that the two belong together.

The panel's map could have weakened my conclusion. On provisional results it does not repeat the pattern, so the pattern stays a finding about one map, never enacted. I correct mistakes in public and don't delete them (technical report, Documented Corrections).

The author's view #

Everything above is the audit. This section is my opinion. Delete it and no number on this page changes.

Two facts are hard to explain innocently. The commissioners the government nominated drew a map that neutral map-drawing almost never produces: fewer than a hundred in a million draws lean that far. And when the commission split instead of endorsing that map, the government moved the job to a committee its own MLAs control.

Each fact, on its own, has an innocent explanation. A commissioner can be eccentric in good faith, and a legislature can lose patience with a split commission. Put the two facts in order, though, and the innocent explanations get harder to hold.

What worries me is that no law was broken. I checked, and that checking is most of what this audit is. The Act makes the commission's report advisory. It sets no notice period, and requires no draft, hearings or consultation before the Legislature acts. The protection was a convention, not a law, and a convention binds only a government that chooses to follow it. In 1995, after its 1992 committee, Alberta changed how commissions are appointed.

The committee that replaced the commission has a government majority and a government chair. It has an advisory panel, chaired by a retired judge who was appointed after the province's acting chief justice declined to help find one. The panel only advises. The MLAs vote, and they will run in the districts they approve. The map won't have a public hearing before it's final.

This government has set a convention aside before. Its first bill, as written, would have let cabinet rewrite laws without the Legislature, and that power was removed only after public outcry. Its own ethics commissioner found the Premier breached the conflicts law by pressing her attorney general about a criminal case. It passed a law letting cabinet repeal local bylaws and trigger the removal of elected councillors. When a court paused one of its laws in June 2025, it invoked the notwithstanding clause that December. Apart from the ethics breach, each of those moves was lawful, and each set aside a convention to get past an independent check.

I also worry about what the map buys. Replay the 2023 election-day vote and the minority map gives the UCP sixty seats of eighty-nine. Sixty seats would protect a government from floor-crossings and leave fewer opposition members to keep up scrutiny in committee. I'd worry about that in anyone's hands.

I could be wrong, and I've set out how you'd know. The panel's map went to the Assembly on November 2, and the rules that decide its verdict were fixed in June, before it was made public. If it lands inside the neutral range, my suspicion has taken real damage, and this site will say so within 72 hours of its release. I committed to that before I knew the answer.

— Will Conner

Two fixes for next time #

Two problems make Alberta's maps harder to check. About half of all votes are cast before election day, and Elections Alberta reports them only by riding, so about 810,000 UCP and NDP votes from 2023 can't be traced to a voting area. And maps are drawn from census counts that are years old by the time they take effect. The fixes page shows how to fix both.
