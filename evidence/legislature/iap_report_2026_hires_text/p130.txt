41. The view is wrong in itself because it should be uncontroversial that those exercising delegated authority
     must act within the ambit of the delegation. This is a key element of the rule of law: Roncarelli v Duplessis,
     [1959] SCR 121. The mere fact that someone exercising delegated power can achieve a better policy
     outcome by acting outside their authority does not justify acting outside of their authority: Canada
     (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration) v Vavilov, 2019 SCC 65 ["Vavilov"] at, e.g., para 108.

42. Placing this objection in principle to the side, however, it is particularly reckless for a panel making
     recommendations concerning electoral boundaries to be acting outside its mandate. An indicium of a map
     that provides fair and effective representation is the process that created it. Carter made it clear that no
     particular process is required, and this has been repeatedly affirmed. However, this emphasis on process
     means that a substantively justifiable map (which our colleagues may have provided with respect to
     Calgary) will be tainted if the process that led to its creation was flawed: see, e.g., the Nova Scotia Reference.
     Courts are rightly reluctant to conclude that particular maps fail to provide effective representation--but
     they have far fewer reservations about holding that a body exercising delegated authority exceeded the
     scope of the delegated power. The Panel exceeding its mandate decreases the legitimacy of the legislature
     relying on the Panel's advice--even vis-à-vis the unanimous advice that restores Lac Ste. Anne and Rocky
     Mountain House-Sundre2 when tainted by our colleagues' proposed redraws of the Calgary and Lethbridge
     electoral divisions.

43. The consequences of our exceeding our mandate will not be an administrative law professor's pedantic
     screed--the consequences could be the legislature being constitutionally prohibited from relying on the
     IAP Majority's advice. There are accordingly asymmetrical consequences between accepting the IAP
     Majority's interpretation of the Panel's mandate, should they be incorrect, and accepting the IAP Minority
     interpretation of the Panel's mandate, should we turn out to be incorrect.

44. We elaborate in paragraphs 45-84 why we consider the IAP Majority to have unreasonably interpreted this
     Panel's mandate. This is more dense and legalistic than we would normally consider desirable in a report
     on electoral boundaries. But we consider it important in these unusual and irregular circumstances when
     there are such serious stakes and consequences to getting this wrong. Some readers may prefer to proceed
     to paragraph 85. In sum, we are of the opinion that:

a) This Panel's mandate to conduct a review and divide Alberta into 91 electoral divisions is qualified by
    Recommendation 5, which restricts how this Panel is to conduct that review and division;

b) Recommendation 5 mandates preserving the ABEBC Majority map to the extent possible, and
    prohibits making changes to "any electoral division in Airdrie or containing territory south of Airdrie's
    southernmost border, with the exception of Drumheller-Stettler";
