Minority Opinion - Panel of the Special Select Committee on Electoral Boundaries  6

III) This Process is Constitutionally Irregular, But Constitutionally
        Permissible

15. In Carter, Justice McLachlin, for a majority of the Supreme Court of Canada, declined to hold that an
     independent process is constitutionally required to draw electoral boundaries.

16. Justice Cory put greater emphasis on the importance of an independent and unconstrained process. We
     discuss below whether a court in 2026 would be more amenable to his position. However, it is not our
     role to query a majority decision of the Supreme Court of Canada.

17. More generally, we recognize that non-independent, irregular processes have been constitutionally
     permitted in the past. The reasons have varied, but have included:

a) the Parliament of Canada unanimously agreeing that an electoral division ought not to be removed
    from Quebec at the last federal redistribution: Preserving Provincial Representation in the House of Commons
    Act, SC 2022, c 6;

b) the National Assembly agreeing to change the number of electoral divisions from 125 to 127 after
    Quebec's independent commission recommended the removal of a seat from Montreal and a seat
    from the Gaspésie: Bill 3, Act to ensure effective representation of electors;

c) Ontario freezing its Northern Ontario boundaries as they were in the 1990s: Representation Act, 2005,
    SO 2005, c 35, Sched 1, save for the Far North, where representation has been expanded: Representation
    Statute Law Amendment Act, 2017, SO 2017, c 18; and

d) Various irregular processes in Nova Scotia concerning districts preserving Acadian and Nova Scotia
    Black representation, some of which have been mandated by the courts: Reference re the Final Report
    of the Electoral Boundaries Commission, 2017 NSCA 10 ("Nova Scotia Reference"); Fédération
    Acadienne de la Nouvelle-Écosse et al v Nova Scotia (Attorney General), 2024 NSSC 339 ("Fédération
    Acadienne").

18. If there is a common thread through these processes, it is that particular areas of a province, or populations
     therein, have unique representational needs. Realizing those needs requires some departure from the
     typical redistribution process. We nonetheless observe that these irregular processes of redistricting have
     typically been confined to particular areas of a province. There is no precedent for the redrawing of the
     entirety of a province after an independent commission has completed that task in at least the past three
     decades of Canadian history. As the Nova Scotia Reference in particular notes, interference in an independent
     redistricting process is constitutionally perilous.

19. We recognize that none of the aforementioned processes we cite is precisely analogous to the one in which
     we are participating. A court may disagree with our concession about the constitutionality of this process.
     We are strengthened in our conclusions by the fact that we are an arm's-length panel (if admittedly not an
     independent commission) tasked to implement specific directions of the judge who chaired the ABEBC.
     We have also been able to review all written and oral submissions made by thousands of Albertans and
     received by the ABEBC in its independent process. This heavily constrains our irregular mandate.

20. We recognize that there is some ambiguity about the Panel's mandate, but it certainly includes providing
     advice on the appropriate boundaries to restore the two rural electoral divisions. We address that first
     before returning to the more expansive interpretation of the Panel mandate made by our colleagues.
