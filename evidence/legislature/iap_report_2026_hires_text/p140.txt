82. We grant that these contemporaneous statements to the adoption of Motion 37 are merely contextual
     clues as to the Panel's mandate, rather than strictly part of it. They must be treated with caution because
     it is the words of the legislature that matter rather than the subjective intentions of the legislators or their
     advisors. However, it is nonetheless appropriate to rely on such statements in cases of genuine ambiguity,
     especially where the legislature is purported to have ousted the common law. Justice Moldaver addressed
     a similar issue for a unanimous Supreme Court in Grant Thornton LLP v New Brunswick, 2021 SCC 31
     in response to an argument that the legislature had ousted the common law of discoverability. He wrote:
     "the Minister's statement can hardly be taken as evidencing the `clear legislative language' needed to oust
     or limit the common law rule [...] If anything, it demonstrates the opposite." Similar considerations are
     applicable here.

83. For the foregoing reasons, we are of the view that the text, context, and purpose of Motion 37 indicate
     that the Panel's mandate is fundamentally limited to making a proposal for 91 electoral divisions that
     restores the two rural electoral divisions and the other subsequent boundary changes necessary for those
     two restorations. As Justice Jamal noted last month for the Supreme Court in Sinclair-Desgagné, public
     confidence in the electoral system must guide the interpretation of legislation (and, by analogy, legislative
     motions) concerning electoral fairness. It is our view that the legislature has passed Motion 37 with
     electoral fairness in mind--indeed, this infuses all six contextual clues we identify. This is a general purpose
     that further supports our interpretation of this Panel's mandate. The consequences of our exceeding our
     mandate could be quite serious.

F. In the Alternative, "Necessary" to Comply with the Charter Remains A High Standard

84. Even if the Panel has the unprecedented mandate to consider ways to ensure effective representation
     beyond the protection of representation in discrete areas, the "necessary" condition applies to the
     constitutional guarantee of effective representation. In other words, any changes, beyond those necessary
     to implement Recommendation 5, must be constitutionally necessary. Our colleagues, in our view,
     interpret the term "necessary" to essentially be synonymous with "preferable". Everyone knows that
     saying something is "necessary" is a higher standard than saying it is "preferable", much less "an
     alternative". Given the irregularity of the independent process and the Panel's procedural limitations (as
     compared to the ABEBC's timeline and resources), this is quite rightly a high threshold. The fact that the
     Panel must "consider" them necessary should not render this a purely subjective standard given the link
     to the Charter. The consequences of unreasonably interpreting this standard of "necessary" could also be
     quite serious.
