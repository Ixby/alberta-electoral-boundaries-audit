10. Hybrid Electoral Divisions

The 2025-2026 Electoral Boundaries Commission devoted an entire section in its Final Report to hybrids. We
would recommend that those who have objections to hybrids read what the Commission had to say.

The Commission concluded, as many boundaries commissions before it concluded, that one cannot
draw an electoral map of Alberta which achieves effective representation without creating hybrid electoral
divisions. Hybrid electoral divisions have been in almost every electoral map of Alberta since its inception.
They were given judicial sanction by the Alberta Court of Appeal in the early 1990's.

There are 19 cities in the province of Alberta and all but Lethbridge have been a part of a hybrid. Even Red
Deer was hybridized in the past to give it enough population for two MLAs. In fact, the Electoral Boundaries
Commission Act of 1990 required Red Deer and a number of other cities to be hybridized.

There are currently (since 2017 )19 hybrid electoral divisions. The 2025-2026 Electoral Boundaries
Commission recommended eight more, most of which were in Calgary and Edmonton, for a total of 27
hybrids out of 89 or 91 electoral divisions overall.

The independent advisory panel is now proposing an additional three quasi-hybrids in Calgary and three real
hybrids in southern Alberta. The panel also proposes to borrow a bit of population from Red Deer to give the
Blackfalds-Innisfail the population it needs.

The 2025-2026 Electoral Boundaries Commission developed principles which the Commission endeavoured
to apply in determining where hybrids should be used. The advisory panel considered these principles and
used them in deciding whether or not to propose a hybrid electoral division. The principles applied by the
Commission and now by the advisory panel are worth repeating. They are quoted as follows:

   1. "For some time, hybrids have been a necessity for smaller cities and town to achieve effective
   representation
   2. As population per electoral divisions increased, it has become untenable for smaller cities to remain
   intact as a single electoral division. For example, in the early 1990s the cities of Red Deer and Medicine
   Hat were too large for one electoral division and not large enough for two urban-only electoral divisions.
   Consequently, hybrid electoral divisions were used in those cities.
   3. A form of blended electoral division which contained all or part of a smaller or mid-size city along with
   a surrounding rural municipality became commonplace in the 1990s and early 2000s. The population
   numbers and geography along with the need to maintain relative voter parity forced this transition.
   4. Currently, high population growth in the major cities along with a change in the Act has made the option
   of hybrids available and necessary province-wide.
   5. The province-wide evolution of hybrids has resulted in the City of Red Deer being the only urban centre
   that does not have a hybrid electoral division (although this Commission minimally used the hybrid model
   for one constituency in Lethbridge).
   6. Extending hybrids to the major cities must be done carefully and may be particularly appropriate when:

       a) Infrastructure such as shared water or sewer exists, similarly built areas are in place (i.e. density
       and style of housing), and where transportation routes predominantly or exclusively require non-urban
       residents to travel through the city;
       b) Indigenous reserves that are immediately adjacent to large cities, especially where there is evidence
       of travel and substantive interactions in both directions, including and members living off-reserve in
       adjacent communities, Indigenous and non-Indigenous housing developments on-reserve, recreation,
       shopping, and other interactions. This should be supported by direct input from the Indigenous
       communities themselves.
