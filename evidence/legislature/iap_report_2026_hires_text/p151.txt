126. Alan Stewart, in "Community of interest in redistricting", a chapter in Drawing the Map: Equality and Efficacy
     of the Vote in Canadian Electoral Boundary Reform (1991, Lortie Commission at pages 117-174) describes the
     purpose of the concept of "community of interest" as follows:

         The rationale of the principle of community of interest is that electoral districts should be
         more than arbitrary, random groupings of individuals. They should be, as far as possible,
         cohesive units, areas with common interests related to representation. Existing districts and
         municipal boundaries should be respected where possible. The outer borders of the
         district should not divide distinct communities and neighbourhoods. Unrelated and
         geographically isolated areas should not be artificially attached to districts with whose
         "core" population they share no significant links [emphasis added]

     It is worth noting that the concept of community of interest is used to describe the Commonwealth
     tradition of drawing electoral boundaries in contrast to the American practice of insisting that every
     constituency have the same population (generally within +/-0.5% population variation) and that
     boundaries can be drawn to ignore a community of interest to ensure equality of population.

127. When looking at what a "community of interest" actually is, both the legislative definition and in Mr.
     Stewart's description of the rationale, the primary community under consideration is the municipality. The
     reason for this was somewhat described in Courtney, John C. "Community of Interest in Electoral
     Boundary Readjustments." Electoral Insight 4.2 (2002): 9-13 for the following reasons:

parliamentary electoral districts are frequently constructed to accord with local district
boundaries. This makes a good deal of sense, for the local district itself constitutes a
perceptible and understandable community of interest for the majority of its residents. City,
municipal, or county boundaries may never change in the lifetime of the residents, whereas
electoral district boundaries may be altered every decade or so.

This "perceptible and understandable community of interest" was also reflected in the public feedback of
the public members from Lethbridge that provided written submissions to the ABEBC. The written
submissions that were provided by an overwhelming majority (approximately 75 to 14 on those that
specifically weighed in on the question) expressed a desire for the boundaries of the Lethbridge electoral
divisions to generally reflect the municipal boundaries of Lethbridge. Simply put, the residents of
Lethbridge see themselves as a distinct community with interests that differ from the rural municipalities
that surround it. They see themselves as having different interests, different matters of concern and
different priorities than their rural neighbours. This difference of interest is frequently reflected in the
different ways that urban municipalities, even mid-size urban municipalities, experience and resolve
problems related to infrastructure, crime, recreation, and social cohesion. Following existing boundaries
and municipal boundaries is very natural and logical in terms of communities of interest, because they map
onto how persons receive services from their governments. This is another reason we are skeptical of the
inclusion of Bearspaw and Springbank in Calgary electoral divisions.
