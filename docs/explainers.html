Alberta Electoral Boundary Audit

The Fourteen Explainers

Appendices A to N · one idea each

Each finding in the audit links to one explainer here. Each explainer takes one idea. It starts with a fair question a reader might ask. It builds the idea with one simple example. Then it sends you back to the finding, so you can read its number on your own. It ends with what the idea can't tell you. Read them in any order.

Appendix A — What "cracking" does to a vote

The question: "Airdrie voters still vote. Nobody took their ballots. How can drawing lines 'dilute' anything?"

Picture a town of 40,000 that mostly votes Orange. The region around it mostly votes Blue. Give the town its own riding, the area that elects one MLA, and it elects an Orange MLA. That is 40,000 people, one seat, a voice. Now cut the town into four pieces. Join each piece to a chunk of Blue countryside. In each new riding, 10,000 town voters face 30,000 rural voters. Orange loses all four. Same voters, same ballots, no seats. No single voter lost anything. What the town lost is the power to elect anyone. That is cracking. Its opposite is packing (Appendix C): crowd a group into as few ridings as you can, so it wins those by huge margins and nothing else.

Now go back to finding 1. Airdrie has 85,805 people. The commission's minority map splits it into four ridings, and Airdrie's people are outnumbered in three of them. The fourth, Airdrie East, keeps more than half the city together. So by my cracking test, which I count as added later rather than set in advance, this is not the cracking pattern. Outside Airdrie East, the pieces do what the town's pieces did above. The same commission's other map shows that two ridings were enough to meet the law.

What it can't tell you: cracking describes an effect, not a motive. Someone may have wanted to weaken a town's vote. Or other rules may have crowded the map. The lines alone can't say which.

Appendix B — Odd shapes, and what the remote-riding exception is for

The question: "All ridings look strange. Why do these three shapes matter?"

Ridings follow rivers, roads and town lines, so lumpy shapes are normal. What I look for is a line doing a job that no ordinary rule explains. A narrow strip that pulls one neighbourhood into a distant riding. An arm that reaches into the edge of a city. A line run out through land where almost no one lives. Alberta's law has a special rule, section 15(2), the remote-riding exception. It lets up to four ridings have far fewer people than usual. Past commissions have described it as being for huge, thinly settled, remote ridings, most of them in the north. The minority map runs one riding's line out through a national park. The commission's majority, the chair among them, charged that this was done in bad faith, to make the riding qualify: a rule written for people who live far apart, used to protect a line where almost no one lives. My check undercuts that charge. The riding qualifies even without the extension.

Now go back to finding 2. The commission's own majority flagged three shapes. Tests with no stake in that fight confirm that the shapes are odd. They also undercut the motive the majority charged.

What it can't tell you: a strange shape is a question, not an answer. One of these shapes has a real logic behind it that deserves credit: the Banff townsite is placed with Canmore. The majority's objection still stands. It was aimed at the line through the empty park, and the townsite's placement does not touch that.

Appendix C — Packing, in a three-riding example

The question: "Bigger ridings have more voters. So what? Everyone still gets one vote."

Everyone does. But the size of a riding is a clue to how votes are grouped. Take 12,000 voters: 7,000 Orange and 5,000 Blue. Split them into three ridings of 4,000. Mix them evenly and Orange wins all three. Now redraw. Put 4,000 Orange voters into one riding, and Orange wins it 100 to 0. Spread the other 3,000 Orange voters over the two ridings left. Blue's 5,000 voters now win both. Same 12,000 voters. Orange still has more of them. But now Orange holds one seat and Blue holds two. That is packing. One side's votes get piled up where they win by far more than they need, and the extra votes are wasted. The sign to look for is size and depth. One side's ridings run big and safe. The other side's run lean and close.

Now go back to finding 3. In one zone of north-east and central Calgary, the commission's minority map has seventeen ridings. They average 11.5% more people than the provincial average. The majority's ridings there, drawn in the same room, average 2.8% more. That is the size half of the pattern. The other half is missing. On 2023's votes the NDP wins eight of the seventeen, by margins below the provincial average. So my packing test does not find packing.

What it can't tell you: big ridings can also come from honest guesses about growth, or from keeping a community whole. Packing leaves ridings of uneven size. So do several innocent choices.

Appendix D — What MAD measures, and why the law allows a range

The question: "Population mean absolute deviation? Is that just a fancy way to say the ridings aren't identical?"

Nearly. Ridings can't all hold exactly the same number of people, because communities come in awkward sizes. The mean absolute deviation, or MAD, asks one plain question: on average, how far is each riding from an equal share? On the commission's own population tables, the majority map's MAD is 3,180. That means its ridings miss an equal share by about 3,180 people on average. The minority map's MAD is 4,707, so its misses are about half again as large. The law allows ridings to differ, within a range: up to 25% above or below the average. That is because keeping real communities whole is worth some inequality. Think of it as a budget. You spend some of the equal share to keep towns whole and lines sensible. That is the point of finding 4. The minority map spends 48% more of that budget than the majority. On 2021 census counts, the two maps' average misses come out almost the same. And finding 1 shows the minority map didn't spend it on keeping Airdrie whole.

Now go back to finding 4. Of the two maps, the minority's ridings stray further from an equal share. That is the one thing the Act puts a number on. Some computer-drawn maps were held to 12.5% above or below the average. These maps were drawn without seeing a single vote (Appendix F). Measured at the middle riding, the minority's spread is wider than 99 of every 100 of them. Against computer-drawn maps allowed the law's full 25%, both commission maps are more equal than almost all of them.

What it can't tell you: the law ranks nothing inside the range. "Spent more of the budget" is a note about priorities, not a breach. Every riding on both maps is within the law.

Appendix E — Following town and city lines, and what it can't show

The question: "Why count how often a riding's line follows a city or town line?"

Because those are the lines people already know. When a riding's line runs along a town's line, the tax line, the ward line and the election line all match. Often the school line does too. A voter can tell which riding they are in without a map. The test measures how much of each map's riding lines run within 500 metres of a town or city line, using Statistics Canada's boundaries. The 2019 map, drawn by the last commission and in use today, scores 75%. The commission's majority map scores 80%. The minority map scores 72%. Both are close to the 2019 map.

Now go back to finding 5. Both maps follow town and city lines at an ordinary rate, close to the 2019 map's. On this test, the two maps can't be told apart.

What it can't tell you: following town lines is a custom, not a rule. The Act sets no minimum. A riding can also leave a town line for a good reason, such as a suburb that has grown past a city limit.

Appendix F — Computer-drawn maps, and how to read a percentile

The question: "A million computer maps? A percentile of a simulation? What does that even mean?"

You can't tell if a map is unusual without knowing what usual looks like. With electoral maps, nobody ever sees the other maps that could have been drawn. So I made them. A computer program starts from a valid map. Then it makes a long chain of small random changes, each one valid. It joins two neighbouring ridings, then splits them again a different way. It repeats that 252,500 times, in four separate chains, for 1,010,000 maps in all. The program never sees a vote while it draws. It can't favour anyone because it can't see anyone. That is all "neutral" means here. Then I take the real map, score it, and ask: how many of the 1,010,000 computer-drawn maps score this high? If 500,000 do, the real map is ordinary. If 69 do, the real map is the kind that this kind of drawing turns out 69 times in 1,010,000 tries. That is a percentile: where a map ranks among the computer-drawn maps. One caution. Each map in a chain is a small change from the one before, so maps next to each other are near-copies. The 1,010,000 maps are worth about 1,000 truly separate maps (roughly 900 to 1,300, depending on the measure). So the exact rank is less precise than it looks.

Now go back to finding 6. Split the vote exactly 50-50, counting 2023 election-day ballots, and count the UCP's seats. The commission's majority map sits at the 78th percentile: ordinary. The minority map sits at the 99.99th percentile, further out than all but about 1 in 10,000. Only 69 of the 1,010,000 maps give the UCP more seats.

What it can't tell you: the computer-drawn maps keep each riding near the same population and in one piece. They do not weigh every fine point of the Act that a real commission weighs. So a percentile measures how far a map is from neutral drawing, not from every map a lawful commission might draw. A percentile is a distance, not a ruling. Nor does it claim that real ridings are drawn at random. The computer-drawn maps are simply what a real map is compared with.

Appendix G — The efficiency gap, in one worked example

The question: "One number that captures gerrymandering? That sounds too easy."

It is, a little. That is why it gets a line set in advance in this audit, and why its miss is reported. The idea: a vote that doesn't help win a seat is "wasted". That means every vote for a loser, plus a winner's votes beyond the 50% plus one it needed. On a fair map both parties waste about the same number. The efficiency gap is the difference between the two parties' wasted votes, as a share of all votes. Say Orange wins a 100-vote riding 90 to 10. Orange needed 51, so it wasted 39. Blue wasted all 10, since every one went to the loser. Packed and cracked maps make one party waste more, riding after riding. On this site a positive gap favours the UCP. My line for Alberta is about +4.1%. Only 5% of the 1,010,000 computer-drawn maps go past it.

Now go back to finding 7. Counting 2023 election-day ballots, the commission's minority map has a gap of +4.0%, at the 94.5th percentile. That is below my line, the 95th percentile, a rule I wrote down in advance. So it is reported as a miss. The all-ballots score is given in finding 7.

What it can't tell you: the efficiency gap squeezes a whole map into one number. One number can't tell a gerrymander from lumpy geography. That is why this audit uses four measures and a line fixed in advance. And it keeps the miss on the record.

Appendix H — The prosecutor's fallacy, in plain words

The question: "A one in 570,000 chance that this map is innocent. That's the finding, right?"

No. The difference matters so much that it has a name. Here is what the number says. If neutral drawing made Alberta's map, a map like the commission's minority map would turn up somewhere between about once in 20,000 tries and once in 570,000. The range depends on how the statistical model is set up. It counts 2023 election-day ballots. It is a model estimate, not a bound. The computer-drawn maps reach that far out rarely or never, so the figure comes from a statistical model, not a count. And it is the chance of the evidence if the map is innocent. It is not the chance the map is innocent, given the evidence. Mixing up those two is the prosecutor's fallacy. A classic example: a suspect matches a DNA profile found at a crime scene. One person in a million has that profile. Is there a one in a million chance he's innocent? No. In a city of ten million, about ten people match. On the DNA alone, the suspect is one of ten. That is a 90% chance that someone else is the source. How rare the evidence is, if he is innocent, is not the chance he is innocent. You also need to know how many innocent ways the evidence could come about.

Now go back to finding 8. The audit states the number it can defend: how rare the map is under neutral drawing. It refuses the swap that would turn that number into a ruling.

What it can't tell you: refusing the fallacy costs the audit its most quotable sentence, and that cost is deliberate. Rarity is evidence. It is not proof of intent, and it never becomes proof.

Appendix I — What a sampler is, and why swapping it matters

The question: "You checked your simulation with another simulation? How is that a check?"

Because the two programs are built differently. A result that comes from a program's quirks, rather than from the map, does not usually survive a change of program. Every program that draws maps has quirks. The program that draws maps for a study like this is called a sampler. The standard one, ReCom, is known to lean toward compact, tidy ridings. In theory, that could make a sprawling real map look more extreme than it is. So I swapped the engine. The new one grows ridings a different way, from random "forests" instead of "trees", and it behaves differently inside. I registered it publicly (OSF he53s) before it drew any map of the run that counts. Only a 500-step software test came first. Its random starting numbers came from a public source that anyone can check. If the minority map's far-out position came from the first engine's quirks, the second engine would move it. It moved by less than one percentile point on every measure. That meets the rule I set in advance for calling the result robust. But the run drew far fewer usable maps than I had planned. So I read it as inconclusive at this run size. This far out, a fraction of a point is a big change. The check shows that the map stays far out, not exactly how far. I also report a check that could not be run. It would test whether the odd shapes in Act I are what produce the extra seats. That needs computer-drawn maps that reach the minority map's seat count, and the separate 5,000-map run has none. So that claim is untested, and I do not make it.

Now go back to finding 9. I tried three ways to knock down my own result. One pointed the same way but was too small to settle it. One held up. One could not be run.

What it can't tell you: checks like these can rule out a result made by the tools. They cannot show that anyone designed the map. A truly extreme map is truly extreme, whatever produced it.

Appendix J — Why neighbours voting alike can fool simple statistics

The question: "A test gave p = 0.0024 and you threw it away? That looks like hiding a result."

The opposite: it corrects one. A p-value says how often chance alone would give a result this strong. Small means rare. Many tests assume each piece of data is separate, like coin flips. Voting areas aren't. Neighbouring areas share incomes, histories and ways of life, and they vote alike. Treating 100 such areas as 100 separate coin flips is like counting 20 real pieces of information 5 times each. It makes a coincidence look impossible when it is only uncommon. The fix is a fairer baseline, a picture of what chance alone would give, that keeps neighbours together. You shuffle blocks of neighbouring areas, not each area on its own, and ask the question again. My swing-zone test asked whether close-fought voting areas were placed in ridings in a partisan pattern. It looked significant on the simple baseline (p = 0.0024). On the fairer one it looked ordinary (p ≈ 0.19). The fairer test is crude: nearly all the swing areas fall in one block, so it can say little either way. But once the simple baseline is known to rest on a false assumption, its answer can't stand. So I retired the result.

Now go back to finding 10. It has three entries that don't help the audit's story: two of my own tools retired, and one test, added later, that the minority map passed.

What it can't tell you: the correction shows that the signal about particular line choices can't be relied on. It says nothing against the findings from the computer-drawn maps. Those never assumed that each voting area was separate.

Appendix K — How to check a stated reason against public data

The question: "Commissioners are the experts. Who is an audit to grade their reasons?"

Nobody special, and that's the point. The check needs no special access. The commission's minority report says Cochrane residents "move fluidly" into Calgary. Statistics Canada's free journey-to-work tables show about a third of Cochrane's workers commute into Calgary, while about half work in Cochrane. That is a real tie. But it says nothing about the Nolan Hill strip of Calgary the riding was drawn to take in. The report says rural residents "go to school" in the city. For Red Deer and Sylvan Lake that holds in part. The two have different public school divisions, but one Catholic division serves both. And 54% of Sylvan Lake's workers who leave town to work go to Red Deer. Each check follows the same recipe. Take the stated reason. Find the public data that would show it. Then ask whether the particular line drawn is the line the data supports. I checked six reasons, and the count depends on the rule. Judged on the grounds the minority stated, none is contradicted, and four are supported only in part. On a separate test of whether population forced the line, four of the lines were not forced. The tie is real. The line is not the one the tie points to.

What it can't tell you: a reason that fails the check shows that the stated reason doesn't fit. It can't reveal the real one. Carelessness and a pretext leave the same paperwork.

Appendix L — How commissioners are chosen, and what a nomination tells you

The question: "The commissioners the government nominated drew the map that favours the government. Case closed, surely?"

Not closed. Start with who sits in the room. Alberta's commission is built from nominations: a chair, plus members nominated by the government side and the opposition side. This time the Premier nominated the two members who wrote the minority report (Legislative Assembly of Alberta, notice of March 28, 2025). The design expects partisan views and balances them. It bets that a mixed room will settle on one map it can defend. From 1995 to 2017 the bet paid. Even when a member dissented, each commission sent the Assembly one map. This time the room split along the lines of who nominated whom. The government's nominees drew the map that sits far outside the range of the computer-drawn maps. That link is real and worth recording. It is also exactly where the reasoning must stop. A nominee is not an agent. Nothing in this audit's record shows that the government directed its nominees, worked with them, or even knew the shape of their map before it was filed. At the October 28 meeting of the committee, the MLAs who oversaw the new map, the Opposition Leader alleged that maps presented at the commission's hearings "were created by the government" and matched the style of the minority's maps (committee transcript, p. CEB-71). That is an allegation, and I have not checked it. History is full of nominees who let down the people who nominated them. The link raises a question. It can't answer it. And my tests cut against the majority too, where the data went that way. That is what measuring both sides the same way means.

Now go back to finding 12. The split is what stands out. Who nominated whom is a question mark, not the answer.

What it can't tell you: who made an appointment is context, never proof. Treating it as proof is the same mistake as in Appendix H, dressed in political clothes.

Appendix M — How the Act works, from commission to committee

The question: "How can the Legislature just take over the redraw? Isn't the commission's map the map?"

No. This is the surprise the whole story rests on. Under Alberta's Electoral Boundaries Commission Act, the commission proposes and only the Assembly, Alberta's elected MLAs, decides. The commission's final report is advice, hearings and all. It has no legal effect until the Assembly passes a map into law. And the Act puts no limits on what the Assembly does instead. There is no minimum debate and no notice period. There is no duty to prefer the commission's work, and no duty to hold hearings in whatever process comes next. Nothing in the Act stops the Assembly from sending the work elsewhere. That is what Government Motion 37 did on April 21, 2026. It created the committee: six MLAs, four UCP and two NDP. Recommendation 5 had itself proposed an all-party committee of MLAs, for a fix to two seats. Motion 37 used that kind of committee for a much wider redraw. Because the committee is made of MLAs, the decision left independent hands. The panel, an advisory panel the committee appointed, drew the new map. The committee sent it to the Assembly on November 2, 2026, and MLAs decide whether it becomes law. As I read the Act, nothing in it forbids any of this, and no court has ruled on it. In every cycle since 1995, the Assembly left the commissions' lines alone, changing at most a few names. That restraint, not the Act, kept politicians' hands off the pen. The Act itself lets the Assembly pass a map "with alterations". In 1992 there was no single commission report to honour: the commission deadlocked, with five members filing five reports, and a committee of MLAs drew the map. The Law page tells that story.

Now go back to finding 13. The commission held hearings in sixteen communities. The body that replaced it did not have to hold any, and held none on the map. As I read the Act, none of it is forbidden.

What it can't tell you: this explainer says what the law allows. Whether Albertans should accept what the law allows is a political question. The November tests on the main page are built to inform it.

Appendix N — Conventions and law, and how a custom wears away

The question: "If politicians drawing their own ridings breaks our deepest election custom, how can it be legal?"

Because Canada's constitution runs on two kinds of rules. One is law. It is written down, a court can rule on it, and judges enforce it. The other is convention: unwritten rules of political conduct. They bind in practice, but no court enforces them. The Supreme Court set out the difference in the 1981 Patriation Reference. A court may recognize a convention and still refuse to enforce it. Conventions are policed by politics: by the opposition, the press and voters, not by judges. In Alberta, an independent commission's map sits on the convention side. About sixty years of practice, first federally and then province by province, built the rule that politicians do not draw their own ridings. In Quebec a commission's map takes effect without the National Assembly's final approval. In Alberta, as in most provinces, it takes a bill. Alberta's Act does not make the rule binding. So here the rule's whole strength was that governments kept choosing to follow it. A custom set aside once, at no cost, is weaker for every government that follows. Of any party.

Now go back to finding 14. It finds three things at once. There is a documented break from long practice, which the opposition calls a violation. There is no convention a court will enforce. And a court can still review the map that the process produces, once it becomes law.

What it can't tell you: "a court won't enforce it" is a statement about courts, not about consequences. Conventions are enforced at elections, by people who know what was done. Reports like this one exist so that they know.
