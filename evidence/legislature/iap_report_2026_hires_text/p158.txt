155. In this vein, we also note that the "effective representation" principle, explained by Justice McLachlin in
     Carter, has been subject to significant academic criticism from those who argue for a stricter adherence to
     representation-by-population: see, e.g., Michael Pal, "The Fractured Right to Vote: Democracy, Discretion,
     and Designing Electoral Districts" (2015) 61:2 McGill LJ 232; Colin Feasby, "Reimagining Democratic
     Rights" Speech at Queen's University Faculty of Law (2 March 2026); online:
     <https://law.queensu.ca/news/podcast-reimagining-democratic-rights>. Advances in technology since
     1991 have not eliminated, but have certainly mitigated, many of the challenges that come with representing
     vast, sprawling rural areas. The courts in the mid-to-late 2020s are less likely to be as tolerant of the
     population variances that they permitted in the early-to-mid 1990s. Indeed, in Toronto (City) v Ontario
     (Attorney General), 2021 SCC 34, Chief Justice Wagner and Justice Brown, for a majority of the Supreme
     Court, underscored that "effective representation connotes voter parity which, while not exhaustive of the
     requirements of effective representation, is the overarching concern and the condition of `prime
     importance'" (para 46). Greater emphasis on voter parity may well be mandated in 2026.

156. To repeat, we are not suggesting that population must be considered to the exclusion of everything else.
     The American experience is a cautionary tale in that respect. A map with 53,722 individuals in each
     electoral division would doubtless be undesirable and look ridiculous. Any suggestion that we are
     advocating for that is a straw man argument. The maps proposed in this IAP Minority Report have
     greater disparities than any maps adopted in Alberta for decades. If we were able to use the 2026 Census
     data, we are certain they would be even greater. Logistical challenges in representing areas, ensuring clear
     boundaries, and respecting communities of interest must all be taken into account. It may well be that the
     2017 redistribution weighed population parity excessively. But these non-population considerations are
     discretionary and at times can be in tension with each other (which is a theme we return to below). In the
     face of reasonable disagreement, population parity must be weighted significantly. Our Commonwealth
     brethren in the United Kingdom, Australia, and New Zealand have maximum variances of 5-10%. This
     works very reasonably. If those drawing boundaries cannot be trusted to weigh the non-population factors
     in a reasonable and non-partisan manner, the courts may order even greater emphasis on population parity.
     The risks of litigation is a matter to which we now turn.
