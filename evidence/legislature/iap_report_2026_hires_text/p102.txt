The point of all of this is to suggest that Recommendation 5 does not deserve deification. It was not "The
Fifth Commandment". It was simply a bit of political "give and take". It was the making of yet another
proposal (which is what electoral boundaries commissions are mandated to make), except that this
proposal was not that of the Commission or of the Commission majority, but that of the Commission
Chairman sitting alone.
Though not accepted in full, the Chairman's proposal was accepted in part. Whether the rest of the
Chairman's proposal will be accepted or not remains to be seen. It is still open to the Legislature to approve
the Commission's majority recommendations.
The advisory panel would propose instead that the Legislature consider some of its proposals; but the
advisory panel understands that its proposals, like the Commission's proposals, need not be approved by
the Legislature.
Some argue that by making the compromise proposal, the Chairman undermined the Majority Report. The
panel's view is that there was nothing untoward about the bargain or compromise the Chairman proposed,
although his fellow commissioners, for reasons which were not disclosed, were apparently opposed to it.
Compromises are what electoral boundary making sometimes requires. Trade-offs or horse-trading are
not unconstitutional so long as the public is not denied its right to effective representation. In this case the
proposal was made with the intent of giving two parts of rural Alberta more effective representation, hardly
an unconstitutional purpose. The point is that Recommendation 5 was little more than a creative proposal by
the Commission Chairman that he hoped would dissuade the Legislature from approving the Commission's
Minority Report.
For those who say this kind of give and take is wrong in principle, some of us might agree. But it is the reality
of electoral boundary making. And in fairness to the advisory panel, it did its utmost to incorporate the
Chairman's recommendations in its proposals, not only the letter but the spirit of them as well. Furthermore,
the advisory panel deferred to most, but not all, of the Commission's electoral boundary recommendations.
But none of the foregoing detracts from what was the advisory panel's mandate, namely, to review and make
proposals with respect to the rest of the Province's electoral divisions.
