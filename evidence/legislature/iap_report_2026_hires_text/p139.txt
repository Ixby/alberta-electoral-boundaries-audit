77. We grant that the doctrines of issue estoppel and cause of action estoppel do not technically apply to this
     Panel's mandate. This is because the ABEBC was not, strictly speaking, an adjudicative process, nor are
     the same "parties" before the ABEBC and this Panel. However, the residual doctrines of collateral attack
     and abuse of process certainly can apply: Toronto (City) v CUPE, Local 79, 2003 SCC
     63; Behn v Moulton Contracting Ltd, 2013 SCC 26 at para 40. These are flexible doctrines designed to
     preserve the integrity of the administration of justice. Such integrity is certainly undermined by revisiting
     issues decided by an administrative process--decisions that have not been formally or properly challenged.
     Even though the ABEBC's recommendations were not legally binding in themselves, they are binding on
     this Panel given Recommendation 5. More importantly, the flexibility of the collateral attack/abuse of
     process doctrines is broad enough to prohibit a constitutionally irregular advisory panel from revisiting
     determinations of a constitutionally compliant, more consultative statutory commission.

78. Our colleagues contend that the legislature has already exercised its constitutional authority to directly
     attack the ABEBC Majority Report by creating this Panel. This would, in our view, require clearer
     legislative language. Clear legislative language is necessary to displace the common law, particularly when
     the stakes are (quasi-)constitutional. For example, in Lizotte v Aviva Insurance Company of Canada,
     2016 SCC 52, Justice Gascon held that "The [Supreme] Court has therefore imposed strict requirements
     for the amendment or abrogation of certain fundamental common law rules" (para 57). He was addressing
     litigation privilege in that case, but concerns related to the administration of justice are also tremendously
     important common law rules.
