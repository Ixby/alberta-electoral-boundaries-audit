9. Effective Representation

Two mandatory factors which must be considered in electoral mapping are relative voter parity and effective
representation.
Relative voter parity has been defined by the Electoral Boundaries Commission Act as plus or minor 25%.
"Effective representation" is a term that has never been authoritatively defined, perhaps because a precise
definition is not possible.
The term effective representation raises more questions than it answers. Firstly, who is being represented?
Individuals? Communities? Municipalities? Interests? Political parties? Does the elected representative
represent his or her constituents or does he or she represent the political party he or she is affiliated with?
The Supreme Court's ruling that effective representation is what is guaranteed in the right to vote provision
of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms suggests that effective representation is the right of the
individual, the Charter being an individual rights statute. That is, the individual serves as the relevant unit of
effective political representation, yet the population being represented is the aggregate of individuals living
in a defined geographic area.
The question is whether the right to effective representation is the right of the individual or the right of
substantial and identifiable communities of interest. In other jurisdictions where protection of visible
minorities, or even visible majorities, is an issue, the right to effective representation is the right of significant
(in terms of population) communities of interest to have the opportunity to elect a representative of their
own choosing to the Legislature. It is not a guarantee that the community of interest will be represented in
the Legislature, but the community must be given a realistic opportunity to elect a representative of that
community's choice. The foregoing definition, as far as the panel knows, has not been adopted in Canada.
Suffice it to say that effective representation, to date, has been defined narrowly by the courts in terms of
the citizen's right to a relatively undiluted vote and to the representative's ability to discharge his role as an
ombudsman (which, of course, is only one of many roles an elected representative plays in an electoral
system where the voter is effectively restricted to voting for candidates which have already been chosen for
him or her by registered political parties).
There are prescribed criteria for determining whether effective representation might be achieved. Those
criteria are to be found in Section 14 and in Electoral Boundaries Commission Act. They are also found in
decided cases. Criteria such as communities of interest, regional, rural or urban municipalities, geographic
constraints and clear and understandable boundaries, etc. are prescribed. And that is why successive
electoral boundaries commissions have recommended electoral divisions in accordance with these criteria,
sometimes at the expense of population parity. But these criteria or "relevant considerations" are often the
subject of debate among reasonable, well-meaning electoral boundaries commissioners. And based on the
review we did of the submissions made to the 2025-2026 Electoral Boundaries Commission, the weighing of
relevant considerations is also the subject of debate among electors.
As a consequence, there can be understandable differences of opinion as to how effective representation is
best achieved and therefore different electoral division configurations proposed.
