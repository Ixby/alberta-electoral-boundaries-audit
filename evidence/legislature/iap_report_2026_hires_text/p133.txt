53. It is notable that Justice Miller imposed significant conditions on the restoration of the two rural electoral
     divisions. We suggest the purpose of his conditions was obvious: to preserve the work of an independent
     commission that completed extensive public consultations, avoid re-litigation of issues the ABEBC has
     decided, and prevent unconstitutional dilution of urban votes. We have shown above that it is possible to
     achieve this by affecting only 14 non-Edmonton electoral divisions,3 and no electoral divisions in
     Lethbridge or Calgary.

54. We also note that his conditions are markedly non-partisan. From a purposive analysis, Justice Miller
     clearly sought to impose terms on the restoration of the two rural electoral divisions that would prevent
     inappropriate partisan gamesmanship. While giving the legislature some flexibility, he protected most of
     the ABEBC's work in revising the province's 89 electoral divisions. This maximizes the extent to which
     an independent commission drew the electoral boundaries and prevents a reoccurrence of the ABEBC
     Minority Report. As Professor Sigalet has noted for the Macdonald-Laurier Institute, the partisan
     implications of following the plain meaning of Justice Miller's conditions are undeniable insofar as he
     recommends restoring two rural electoral divisions. But they are also confined to the restoration of two
     rural electoral divisions and attenuated by mandating that Beaumont leave Edmonton: see Geoffrey
     Sigalet, "Lines, power, and trust - Inside Alberta's electoral boundaries fight" Inside Policy (4 May 2026),
     online: <url=https://macdonaldlaurier.ca/lines-power-and-trust-inside-albertas-electoral-boundaries-
     fight-geoffrey-sigalet-for-inside-policy/>.

55. However, our colleagues propose further changes that, though discrete, could have extraordinarily
     consequential partisan implications. Presumably, the clear but minimal partisan implications of the
     restoration of the two electoral divisions on his specific conditions are why Justice Miller recommended
     that an all-party committee study this process. An all-party committee, where the government has a
     majority of the membership, would normally not be an acceptable way to draw electoral divisions given
     the risks of partisan gamesmanship.

56. Justice Miller's Addendum prohibited making any changes to electoral divisions "in Airdrie or containing
     territory south of Airdrie's southernmost border, with the exception of Drumheller-Stettler". Presumably,
     he made Drumheller-Stettler an exception to his general rule because the cascading consequences of
     creating Rocky Mountain House-Sundre would enable parts of Lacombe County to leave Drumheller-
     Stettler. In other words, he was practical. It goes without saying, however, that Calgary and Lethbridge--
     which are where Alberta's most competitive electoral divisions are disproportionately found at present--
     are south of Airdrie. His conditions prevent further gamesmanship concerning these electoral divisions.
