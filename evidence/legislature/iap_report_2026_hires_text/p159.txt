158. Nor is the remedy guaranteed to be the imposition of this, the IAP Minority, map of 91. We consider our
     map to have struck the right balance between representation-by-population, other statutory and
     constitutional criteria, and the challenges of representing sparsely populated rural areas. However, we
     recognize that others may not agree and instead take the view that a "Rocky Mountain House-Sundre"
     electoral division in particular would only be justified as being minimally impairing of urban Albertans'
     right to vote in a map of 93 or 95, which would add additional urban seats. A court may accordingly
     impose the ABEBC Majority Report's final recommended map of 89--the map of 87 failing to provide
     effective representation to those in central and north Calgary and south Edmonton given population
     growth since 2017. The Supreme Court has also recently confirmed that freezing an entire electoral map
     to protect two electoral divisions is itself unconstitutional: Quebec (Attorney General) v Lalande, 2026
     SCC 13.

159. In the 1994 Reference, the Alberta Court of Appeal expected that the variance in population between urban
     and rural electoral divisions (and thus the gap in voting power) would inevitably decrease over time. That
     has indeed been the trend: until this year. In March, the ABEBC Majority Report proposed maps with a
     greater variance in population between rural and Calgary and Edmonton electoral divisions than the 2017
     redistribution, ending a decades-long trend. Our map of 91 increases that variance further with higher
     populations in urban electoral divisions and lower populations in more rural electoral divisions. While we
     view our map to be constitutionally compliant, for reasons we have explained, going against the trend of
     the last several decades is something we do with hesitation. Our colleagues increase the gap even more,
     creating further--and unnecessary--constitutional risk.

160. On the note of the legislature's constitutional purview in this area, while the Supreme Court in Carter held
     that independent commissions are not constitutionally required (over Justice Cory's doubts in this regard),
     we are less certain that it would find the same 35 years later. Since Carter, the Supreme Court has held that
     independent commissions are required for judicial compensation given the constitutional stakes: Reference
     re Remuneration of Judges of the Provincial Court (PEI), [1997] 3 SCR 3. The stakes for democracy in
     fair elections are equal if not greater. Irregular processes have been tolerated in the past, but usually in the
     face of all-party agreement. In 2026, it is completely possible that a court would hold that disregarding an
     independent commission's recommendations regarding electoral boundaries itself amounts to a limit on s
     3 that would require justification under R v Oakes, [1986] 1 SCR 103. This could result in the imposition
     of the ABEBC Majority Report's map of 89.

161. To be sure, we are not predicting that those events will transpire in litigation. But litigation is unpredictable
     and this saga may well prompt courts to revisit the Carter framework.

VIII) Conclusion

162. Much of life in a healthy democracy depends not on laws but on norms. This is good. Resorting to the
     courts forces rigid legal solutions rather than nuanced compromises onto complex problems. This is why
     allowing a pluralistic society to resolve these issues through established norms is always preferable. We
     have sought to participate in this process bearing this in mind.
