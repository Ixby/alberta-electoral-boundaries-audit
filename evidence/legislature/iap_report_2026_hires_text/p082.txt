Nevertheless, the Commission's definition and treatment of hybrid electoral divisions is instructive. The
Commission defined a hybrid electoral division as one that contains at least a part of a city and a portion of
a municipality or county outside the city. The definition also included rural electoral divisions that contain
cities within them. The Commission also recognized that sometimes a city requires population from
surrounding rural areas in order to obtain representation it could reasonably call its own. Cochrane and
Okotoks are examples which immediately come to mind. Hybridization works both ways. It can help cities
receive the representation they need. It can help rural electoral divisions to obtain the population they need
to be relatively compact and manageable.
The 2025-2026 Commission identified principles or guidelines it used in creating hybrids. One of the
situations in which the Commission proposed that hybrids should be used is where all or part of a city is
blended with a surrounding population which is essentially suburban. Such hybrids became commonplace
in the 1990's and early 2000's because population numbers and the need to maintain relative voter
parity forced the transition to blended or hybrid ridings. To quote the Commission, ".... the need to ensure
effective representation for all Albertans may necessitate at times that a hybrid be used purely to satisfy the
numerical challenge (underlining added) across the province that a Commission faces." Thirty years and
several electoral boundaries commissions later, only Red Deer and Lethbridge do not share population with
an electoral division outside their municipal boundaries. The Commission itself recommended eight new
hybrid electoral divisions bringing the total number of hybrid electoral divisions in the province to 27.
In his Addendum to the Majority Report, 2025-2026 Electoral Boundaries Commission Chairman Miller
stated:

    "In addition, I wish to clarify that I do not consider all of the minority's hybrids to be unreasonable, even
    the ones the majority did not adopt. We did not adopt the hybridization of Bearspaw or Springbank with
    Calgary, nor the extent of the hybridization of Lethbridge, for reasons explained elsewhere in this report.
    However, I do not consider those hybrids necessarily unreasonable or procedurally unfair, given the
    extensive submissions about them. Indeed, had the minority chosen to advocate for them, instead of
    insisting upon an otherwise unreasonable and unconstitutional map, I may will have agreed with them."
In other words, it was not that the Commission's Chair opposed hybrids for Lethbridge. It was not
hybrid proposals for Lethbridge which the Commission Chair thought were necessarily unreasonable or
procedurally unfair. Indeed, he said he might have agreed with them. Unfortunately, having said that, the
Commission was apparently not able to assess the merits of the hybrids proposed, perhaps because they
were proposed at the 11th hour.
The advisory panel has now conducted such a review of the merits of borrowing population from Lethbridge
to create proposed electoral divisions for southern Alberta which strive to achieve effective representation
for rural residents of southern Alberta as well as for residents of the City of Lethbridge. Below we deal with
the merits of the proposed Lethbridge hybrids, both their advantages and disadvantages. Having conducted
that review, it is the recommendation of three of the five advisory panel members that they be seriously
considered by the Legislative Assembly.
