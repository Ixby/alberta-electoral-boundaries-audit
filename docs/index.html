Alberta's Electoral Map, Audited

Alberta's boundary commission split in 2026 and filed two maps. I set both beside 1.01 million maps a computer drew with no stake in who wins. The majority's map sits among them. The minority's leans to the UCP further than almost any of them.

The next map, with 91 seats, comes from an advisory panel that a committee of MLAs oversaw. The committee sent it to the Assembly on Monday, November 2, 2026. It becomes law only if MLAs pass a bill. My provisional scores are below; I'll score the official map files within 72 hours of their release.

What happened #

Every so often Alberta redraws its ridings, the districts that each send one MLA to the Legislature. Where the lines fall can decide who wins.

On March 23, 2026 the independent commission doing the redraw split three to two and filed two maps. Both fit the Act's numeric rules. The majority argued the minority's map would likely fail the Charter's test; no court has ruled. The chair and the two commissioners nominated by the opposition wrote the majority map. The two commissioners nominated by the government wrote the minority map (Legislative Assembly of Alberta, March 28, 2025). Same rules, same data, same 1,345 public submissions. Two maps.

The majority map looks like what a neutral process draws. The minority map doesn't:

  • It splits Airdrie, a city of 85,805, into four districts. Outside Airdrie East, which holds more than half the city, Airdrie's residents are outnumbered.
  • In north-east and central Calgary, its seventeen districts run 11.5% above the provincial average population. On 2023's votes the NDP wins eight of them.
  • Split the vote dead even and it hands the UCP 51.7% of the seats (46 of 89). Only 69 of 1,010,000 computer-drawn maps give more, and all 69 give 45 of 87 (51.72%), a hair above it.
  • Replay the 2023 election-day vote and the UCP takes 60 of 89 seats. On the majority map, 55. On all 2023 ballots, the count the audit now uses, the two maps give 55 and 48.

The Assembly adopted neither map. On April 21 it passed Government Motion 37, which handed the job to the Select Special Committee on Electoral Boundaries, six MLAs with a UCP majority. An advisory panel under the committee drew a 91-seat map and reported on October 22. The panel itself split: its report includes a minority opinion. On October 28 the committee voted 3–2 to end its review without observations. It held no public hearings on the map and released no draft for the public to comment on. It had to send the panel's report to the Assembly by November 2. The map becomes law only if MLAs pass a bill.

What I can say, and what I can't

  • ✓On the four fairness measures combined, the minority map sits further out than all 1.01 million neutral maps, in the UCP's favour. Taken one at a time, three of the four put it deep in the UCP's tail.
  • ✓All four partisan-fairness measures lean the same way.
  • ✗That any commissioner meant to do this. My tests measure what a map does. They can't show motive.
  • ✗That the government directed its nominees. I found no evidence of coordination. Appointing someone is not evidence of it.
  • ✗That the pattern will happen again. The map that has it was never enacted. November will show whether it comes back.
  • ✗How a court would rule, or what the Assembly will enact.

On the word. Gerrymandering means drawing lines to give a party an advantage. You recognise it by what a map does, and by what is known about how it was drawn. Canadian law has no offence by that name, so a court won't rule on gerrymandering as such. That doesn't mean it can't happen here. The closest legal test is effective representation under section 3 of the Charter, covered below.

What the minority map does #

The two commission maps agree on most of Alberta. Where they differ, here is what the minority map chose, and what each choice does.

One city, four districts

Airdrie has 85,805 people (2024 municipal census), one city council. The law caps each district at one-and-a-quarter times the provincial average, so Airdrie needs at least two districts. The majority map gives it two.

The minority map gives it four: north to Olds-Three Hills-Didsbury, east to Airdrie East, west to Calgary-Foothills-Airdrie West, and centre-south to Calgary-Airdrie. Airdrie East takes more than half the city, and nine in ten of its people are Airdrie residents. Nearly all the rest of the city goes to the two Calgary-edge districts, where Calgary residents outnumber Airdrie's. The north piece holds about 350 people. Splitting a community so thinly that it can't decide any of its seats is called cracking. The split clears the first bar of my cracking test (exploratory: I wrote its rules in the same session that first ran it): more pieces than the city's size requires. It fails the second, that Airdrie's residents be outnumbered in every piece, because of Airdrie East. By the test's own rule, that is no cracking signature. The majority's two-way split fails both bars.

On the minority map, the test doesn't flag the city as a whole. But the part of Airdrie outside Airdrie East, about 45% of the city, is outnumbered in every district it lands in, which is what cracking describes. No registered test scores that part on its own. Airdrie would have four MLAs. Most of the voters who elect three of them live outside Airdrie.

Bigger districts in north-east and central Calgary

In Calgary's north-east and central districts, the minority map's seventeen average 11.5% above the provincial average population. The majority's average 2.8%. A bigger district means each vote in it counts for less, so this is a finding about equal population. Putting one party's voters into fewer, larger districts is called packing, but my packing test doesn't detect it here. On 2023's votes the NDP wins eight of the seventeen, and their average winning margin is 15.0 points, 12.6 below the provincial average; the test needs margins 15 points above it. The test is exploratory: I wrote its rules in the same session that first ran it.

Districts further from equal size

Equal district populations are the one value Alberta's law puts a number on. By the commission's own population tables, the minority map's districts across the province miss the ideal size by 4,707 people on average. On the majority map, 3,180. The minority's spread is 48% wider. On 2021 census numbers the gap narrows: the two maps' average deviations are almost the same, though the minority's typical district is still further from equal (3,938 against 2,827). The usual defence of odd lines is that they were needed to balance populations. On the numbers the commission itself used, that defence doesn't work here.

Rural tails on urban districts

Still, I'm not saying rural Alberta has too many seats. The Act lets district populations vary by up to 25%, so one rural MLA doesn't have to cover an area the size of southern France. Both maps use that room.

But in several places the minority map ties rural communities to a district whose population centre is in a city.

Three boundaries the majority called out

The commission's majority, the chair among them, flagged four of the minority's boundaries as geographically anomalous, among them these three:

  • the Rocky Mountain House–Banff Park extension into uninhabited national-park land;
  • the lasso-shaped corridor of Calgary-Nolan Hill–Cochrane;
  • the reach of Olds–Three Hills–Didsbury into north Airdrie.

The flags come from the majority's reply to the minority, so you would expect them to criticise only the minority. So I checked all four against the published maps myself, and three of them hold up.

The majority called the park extension "a bad faith effort" to claim a population protection, section 15(2), that is written for remote communities where people actually live. My check undercuts that charge: the division qualifies under s.15(2) even without the extension. The Banff townsite is a separate question: putting it with Canmore makes sense as a community of interest, because the town sits inside a national park it can't grow into. Its objection to the empty-park extension still stands. The townsite's placement doesn't affect it.

The seat advantage

Split the province-wide vote dead even, and this is the UCP's share of seats on each map:

  • the median neutral map gives 44.8%, less than an even share, for a reason covered below;
  • the majority map gives 46.1%, the 78th percentile;
  • the minority map gives 51.7% (46 of 89), the 99.99th percentile. Only 69 of 1,010,000 neutral maps exceed it, all with 45 of 87 seats (51.72%), a hair above.

On the minority map the UCP keeps the 45 seats it needs for a majority even if its vote share falls to about 49.2%. On the majority map it would need to win the popular vote by about 9 points. Replay the 2023 election-day vote and the minority map gives it 60 of 89 seats. On the majority map, 55. On all 2023 ballots, the count the audit now uses, the two maps give 55 and 48.

Sixty seats is a two-thirds majority. In Alberta, that unlocks no special power. The Assembly decides every question by a simple majority of the votes cast. A simple majority can already pass laws and budgets, cut debate short with a programming motion, change the rules of the House and set every committee's membership, which the rules keep in proportion to seats. Conventions don't need a supermajority either: a simple majority set this one aside. Sixty seats would give the government a cushion. A few floor-crossings couldn't threaten it, and a smaller opposition would have fewer members to keep up committee scrutiny.

None of the minority map's choices breaks the Act's numeric rules. The majority argued the map would likely fail the Charter's test of effective representation; no court has ruled. The Act allows splitting cities, and every district on both maps sits inside the ±25% band, apart from three on each that use the s.15(2) exception for remote divisions. Canadian law has no rule against partisan advantage as such. Its test is whether voters get effective representation.

What the law requires, and what it doesn't #

The Constitution

Section 3 of the Charter gives every citizen the right to vote. The Supreme Court of Canada reads it as a right to effective representation. Roughly equal voting power is the starting point, a "prime condition", but strict equality isn't required. A district can be bigger or smaller for a real reason, such as geography, community history, shared local interests or a minority community's voice. The difference has to be justified.

Courts give legislatures a lot of room here. They ask whether reasonable people, applying those principles, could have drawn the lines this way. And no government can switch the right off: the notwithstanding clause doesn't apply to section 3.

Courts have also looked at how a map gets made. In 2017 Nova Scotia's Court of Appeal found the province's map violated section 3, because the government had directed its independent commission to change course and the final map didn't reflect the commission's own view. The court added that a legislature may still draw a map itself.

In 2026 the Supreme Court of Canada struck down a Quebec law that paused Quebec's commission to protect ridings in the Gaspésie (Quebec (AG) v. Lalande, 2026 SCC 13, 7–2, reasons May 1). Quebec could have protected them more narrowly. The law had passed unanimously. That didn't save it. On June 12, 2026 Quebec's National Assembly passed its own 127-seat map.

Alberta's statute

The Electoral Boundaries Commission Act sets the commission's rules:

  • Who sits. A chair appointed by cabinet from eligible roles that include judges. Four non-MLA members, two nominated by the Premier and two by the Opposition, appointed by the Speaker.
  • Hearings. Public hearings before and after its draft map.
  • Deadlines. A draft within seven months, and a final report within five months after that.
  • Seats. 91. The Act said 89 when the commission drew its maps. Bill 32, introduced April 23, 2026, raised it to 91.
  • Population. Every division within 25% of the average. Up to four remote divisions may go as low as 50% below, if the commission finds at least three of five criteria met.
  • Factors. The commission must consider effective representation, and may weigh growth, communities of interest, geography and other factors.

Both commission maps fit inside these rules.

What the law doesn't require

Most of that rulebook is addressed to "the Commission". Read plainly, it doesn't bind the Legislature or a committee of MLAs. Government Motion 37 gave the advisory panel the Act's population rules and its list of factors, nearly word for word: the ±25% band, the s.15(2) exception for up to four remote divisions, and effective representation first. It left out the hearings: the review was to be expedited, with no requirement to hold public hearings (Votes and Proceedings, April 21, 2026).

  • The Legislature doesn't have to adopt a commission's map. A report becomes law only if MLAs pass a bill.
  • I found no provision that requires a committee of MLAs to hold hearings, be independent, publish a draft or explain its choices. Government Motion 37 says the review is expedited and does not require public hearings.
  • Recommendation 5, which the government cited, binds no one.
  • Elections Alberta says it needs at least a year and a half to prepare new ridings. That's a planning need. No law makes it a deadline.
  • Courts don't review a law while it's being made. A challenge can only come after a boundaries law passes.

What happened in 1992

In 1992 Alberta's commission could not agree. Its final report was five separate reports, one from each commissioner. In July 1992 the Assembly passed Motion 24 and handed the work to a Select Special Committee of MLAs chaired by Bob Bogle. The Opposition refused to appoint members, so four Progressive Conservative MLAs drew the map. Alberta voted on it in the 1993 election.

In 1994 the Court of Appeal let that map stand. The 2026 commission's account says the court found it did not rise to the level of Charter condemnation, but called for “a gradual and steady” change (final report, Appendix D, p. 282). Secondary accounts add that the court criticised how the map was made: the committee's members couldn't explain several of its boundaries, one of the smallest districts was Bogle's own, and a proper review was needed before the Legislature's term ran out. I haven't read the judgment itself. In 1995 the Legislature amended the Act and changed how commissions are appointed. Alberta used that process from then until 2026.

What this means in November

The new map, once enacted, can still be challenged in court. It faces the same test as any map: effective representation, with every population gap justified. Reasons matter. Secondary accounts say Alberta's Court of Appeal criticised the last committee-drawn map for not giving them. I'm scoring the map and the process. Whether it's constitutional is for a court to say.

How the committee got the job #

Before the committee took over, the commission did its work in public. It held hearings in sixteen communities. It received 1,345 written submissions. And after many of them (the minority report counts at least 56), it reversed its plan to remove Lesser Slave Lake.

Four weeks after the split, Government Motion 37 (introduced April 16) created the Select Special Committee on Electoral Boundaries, with six MLAs:

  • four UCP: Brandon Lunty (chair), de Jonge, Sawyer and Wiebe;
  • two NDP: Ganley and Gray.

The committee oversees an Independent Advisory Panel, which drafts the 91-seat proposal. In June the committee appointed four panel members: the Hon. Monte Solberg and Darwin Durnie, nominated by the Premier, and Dr. Gerard Kennedy and Brent Robinson, nominated by the Opposition.

Alberta's acting chief justice declined the committee's request to help find a judge to chair the panel, citing "the irregularity of this process". The Alberta branch of the Canadian Bar Association declined to take part, and so did the Law Society of Alberta. Retired Court of Appeal justice Brian O'Ferrall was later named chair. The panel advises. The committee's MLAs review its report and send it to the Assembly, where MLAs decide. The panel reported to the committee on October 22. At the committee's October 28 meeting, its UCP members voted 3–2 to end the review of the panel's report before NDP motions on it were considered, and voted down 3–2 an NDP motion to disclose what the panel and its consultants were paid (committee transcript, pp. CEB-73 and CEB-74). The committee also defeated an NDP motion to publish whom the panel consulted; a UCP member cited privileged legal advice and the panel's independence (p. CEB-75). NDP members said the panel took no public submissions. The committee held no further meetings, and had to send the panel's report to the Assembly by November 2.

In May, Duane Bratt, Professor of Political Science at Mount Royal University, described the process to me as without precedent in Canada. He's right. 1992 isn't the same thing.

In 1992 the commission couldn't agree. Five commissioners filed five reports, and none had a majority behind it. In 2026 a majority signed one, and under the Act a majority report is the commission's report. Government Motion 37 set it aside anyway. That is the part with no precedent.

On the floor, the government presented its route as following the commission: "We are adopting the recommendations from the report" (Government House Leader, Hansard, April 16, 2026). The opposition called it "retaliation against the independent Electoral [Boundaries Commission]." The report itself doesn't fully support the government's claim. The majority did say two more seats would have given more effective representation (final report, p. 58), and Recommendation 5 itself says its change “would need to be studied by an all-party Select Special Committee or other equivalent Legislative Committee” (p. 66). But the majority asked the Legislature to adopt its 89-seat map. The one recommendation that proposed 91 seats now came from the chair alone.

Every step was lawful. But this time the MLAs who oversee the map will also run in it. And nothing stops a future government, of any party, from citing this cycle as precedent.

Recommendation 5: what the government cited

To justify handing the job to a committee, the government cited one recommendation in the commission's report. So that recommendation is the first thing to check when the panel's map comes out. The chair, Justice Dallas Miller, wrote Recommendation 5 alone, in an addendum to the majority report. He was "reasonably certain" that with 91 seats no division outside Edmonton and Calgary would be lost, and added: "My majority colleagues do not agree with me on this point. That is why I am alone in making this recommendation" (final report, p. 66). He also stated its purpose: it "is formulated for the express purpose of dissuading the Legislature from accepting the minority report."

It's a fallback, and a specific one. If the Legislature wouldn't accept the majority's choice to remove two divisions outside Edmonton and Calgary, the chair asked it to amend the Electoral Divisions Act from 89 seats to 91, restore those two divisions, and keep "the rest of the province as we propose" (the majority map) "to the extent possible." Restoring the two divisions should:

  • (a) Leave Airdrie and the south alone. No change to any division in Airdrie, or with any land south of Airdrie's southern city limit, except Drumheller-Stettler. On the majority map that is 41 divisions: both Airdrie seats, all 28 Calgary seats, and the 11 divisions around and south of Calgary.
  • (b) Leave north Edmonton alone. No change to any Edmonton division north of the North Saskatchewan River: 11 divisions on the majority map.
  • (c) Put south Edmonton back. Every Edmonton division south of the river returns to the commission's October 2025 interim map, the draft it published for comment before the final split. The committee may add Nisku to Edmonton-South East if it wants Leduc and Beaumont back in one division. That is 10 divisions.
  • (d) Consider a Clearwater seat. Consider restoring a division made mostly of Clearwater County and western Mountain View County, with s.15(2) status. This is the only restored seat the recommendation places. It names no place for the second.

Together, the conditions rule out most of the ground the commission split over. Airdrie, Calgary, Cochrane and Chestermere, home to the four-way Airdrie split and the Calgary-edge hybrids, all fall under condition (a). Red Deer and St. Albert are covered only by the softer clause, "to the extent possible." A map that reopens Airdrie or Calgary may still be lawful. But it isn't the map Recommendation 5 describes. Government Motion 37 itself lets the panel apply the recommendation "subject to any modifications the independent advisory panel considers necessary." The map explorer outlines the area under each of the four conditions, and each one's pin says whether Recommendation 5 changes it or leaves it alone.

How we know #

How the neutral maps were drawn

A computer drew 1,010,000 Alberta maps from the official Elections Alberta files, each with 87 districts, the 2019 map's count, all inside the population band and in one piece. The method, ReCom, is the standard one in this field. It merges two neighbouring districts, splits the result at random into two new legal ones, and repeats. The computer never sees a vote. That is what makes these maps neutral. Where a real map's numbers fall among the million shows how unusual it is. Neutral maps drawn at 89 districts, the commission maps' size, show the same lean (those results are still preliminary).

Some of my commitments are filed on the Open Science Framework, the first on May 6, 2026. The random seeds come from a public randomness beacon. I fixed them in the code on April 27, before the official map files arrived; the record of that is a commit in my own repository, not an OSF registration. The Methods page labels each finding pre-registered or exploratory.

In Alberta, neutral is not 50/50

Rural UCP voters win their ridings by wide margins, about 70-30 on average, and those margins waste a lot of extra votes. Urban NDP voters win theirs by closer margins, about 60-40. So when the vote splits dead even, the median neutral map gives the UCP only 44.8% of seats. That is the baseline both maps are measured against.

What "1 in 568,000" means

The chance that a neutral process produces the minority map's combined profile across the four fairness measures is about 1 in 568,000 (p ≈ 1.76×10⁻⁶ after a Bonferroni correction). I looked in two places, so I doubled the raw figure. No neutral map matched the profile, so the figure comes from a statistical model, not a count: it is an estimate beyond the edge of every map drawn, not a bound. A more cautious version of the model gives about 1 in 450,000.

That figure isn't the probability that the map is innocent. Treating it that way is the prosecutor's fallacy. A map this rare can still come from unusual but honest judgment. My claim is only that drawing that follows the rules and never looks at votes almost never produces this profile.

Checking with a second method

I swapped the simulation's engine for a structurally different one, a spanning-forest sampler registered in advance at OSF (he53s) and run on 2026-07-10. The minority's percentile on every metric stayed within a point of the main run. That smaller run can't measure a shift that fine (its margin is about two points), so the honest reading is no change it could detect. A separate implementation, the R redist package, drew 5,000 maps and found none reaching the minority's value. On that smaller run the majority map sits just above the 95th percentile; on the main run, at the 78th.

Limits of the simulation

The simulation enforces population and contiguity, keeping each district in a single piece, but not every rule the commission weighed (s.15(2), community of interest, municipal anchoring). It's a strong outside check, not a list of every map a lawful commission might draw. The structural tests describe how the two commission maps differ; the simulation is what says how unusual the minority map is. The tied-vote test has a limit too: it isolates what a map contributes on its own. A big enough swing in a real election can still outweigh any map's lean.

What didn't hold up

Some tests came back against my findings. They stay on the page.

  • The anchoring test came back neutral. The minority map follows municipal lines 72% of the time and the majority 80%. The 2019 map, drawn by the last commission, scores 75% on the same measure.
  • The efficiency gap missed. The minority came in at +4.0%, the 94.5th percentile. The pre-registered line was the 95th (about +4.1%), so I report it as a miss.
  • The mechanism test couldn't be run. I planned to test whether the flagged shapes produce the seat advantage. But on the 5,000-map run the test was planned for, no neutral map reaches the minority's seat count, so there is nothing to compare. At the lower cutoff I first used, only 3 maps qualify, and they are slightly less compact than the rest: too few to show anything. The shapes and the seat numbers point at the same map, but the link between them is untested.
  • The minority passed one test. On neighbour-drain, which looks for packed districts next to thin-margin ones, it shows 1 coupled signal, against the majority's 2 and the 2019 map's 5. The test is exploratory and the pass is weak: with the districts' labels shuffled at random, the same result turns up about 91% of the time. Its other prediction, that both maps would look ordinary, failed: all three maps score unusually low.
  • Two statistics don't hold up. The Swing-Zone Allocation Test falls from p = 0.0024 to p ≈ 0.19 once corrected for neighbouring areas voting alike, so it is exploratory only. Combining it with the ensemble test, as Fisher's method does, assumes the two are independent. They aren't, so I don't use that combined figure.

Recommendation 5. Test X1 scores the panel's map against the commission chair's four conditions, one at a time:

  • (a), (b) and (c) pass if the map leaves those divisions as described.
  • (d) asks only that a Clearwater seat be considered, so it's scored on evidenced consideration. It passes if the map contains the division, or if the committee's or the panel's published report names the option and gives a reason, tied to the Act's factors, for adopting or declining it. With no other public record of how the map was made, those reports are the only record that counts. Silence or boilerplate fails.

X1 passes only if all four do. Condition (c) needs the commission's interim map, which isn't in the project's files; if I can't get it when scoring begins, I'll report (c) as not scored. I'll also report a strict reading, which fails (d) unless the seat exists. Recommendation 5 binds no one in law. What X1 tests is whether the map delivers what the government cited.

The map. Where does it land on every measure on this page, against the same million-map distribution and under the pre-registered thresholds (on all ballots, Amendment 12 set two of those lines afresh)?

What each outcome means. If the panel's map repeats the minority map's pattern on the partisan measures, drawn by different people, under different constraints, on a different timeline, the partisan finding is replicated. The structural tests add little, because they match the minority's pattern on 93.5% of neutral 91-seat maps. If it lands inside the neutral bands, the finding stays an isolated observation about one map that was never enacted, and I'll say so here. Either way, the arithmetic is public. You can check it yourself.

November: the panel's map, checked #

Added November 7, 2026. The official shapefile of the panel's map has not been posted. Every result in this section uses the audit's reconstruction of the map from the panel's report, so all of it is provisional. The reconstruction failed its first two calibration checks: 49.0% and then 13.8% of voting areas landed in the wrong district, against a 10% limit. Amendment 10 withdrew the registered limit of one more try, and the third try passed, but only as a reproducibility run on pages already seen while the method was built, not as a fresh test of its accuracy. The scoring reruns on the official file as soon as it is posted.

The scorecard, run again (November 4). The tests written down in advance were run on the panel's map (findings/a10_stepc/README.md). Seven amendments to them were written November 2–7: Amendments 7–10 before any of the panel's map was scored, and 11–13 after results had been seen (findings/pre_registration_amendment_log.md).

  • Structural half: 2 of 5 flags. A match needs 3. Not replicated.
  • Partisan half: 1 of 4 flags. Seats at a tied vote sit at the 98.8th percentile; the efficiency gap (+1.45%, 45th percentile), mean-median and declination sit inside the normal range. A match needs 2. Not present.
  • The recommendation the government cited: Recommendation 5 asked for no change to the 41 divisions from Airdrie south, apart from one. The panel changed 10 of them, so on the audit's registered test the map does not follow it.

So, on this provisional reading, the panel's map does not carry the minority map's signature on either half. Per the commitment above, this site says so.

The ballots behind the tests changed (November 7). Until now the votes behind every partisan test were 2023 election-day ballots. Those leave out about half of 2023's ballots (advance, mobile and special ballots, and Vote Anywhere), which Elections Alberta reports only by division. On the 2019 map, election-day ballots alone elect 57 UCP and 30 NDP members; the real 2023 result was 49 and 38. The audit spread the missing ballots over each division's voting areas in proportion to the election-day votes each area cast, so each division's totals match the official result by design; where the votes sit inside a division is an estimate. It then registered a change after the all-ballots results, the commission minority's included, had been seen: every vote-based test now uses all ballots, and election-day results are kept beside them (Amendment 12, findings/pre_registration_amendment_log.md).

  • The panel's map gets the same counts either way: 2 of 5 and 1 of 4.
  • The commission minority's map would move: its efficiency gap on all ballots (+2.55%, 98.8th percentile) is above the new line (+1.39%). Its partisan half would read 4 of 4 instead of 3 of 4. The amendment does not reopen the registered Phase 1 result in Finding 7, but the reader should know which way the new rule points.
  • Rescoring finished (November 8): every comparison set has been rerun on all ballots. Two early chains did not reproduce exactly and were dropped, as Amendment 13 requires; it too was written after results had been seen. The reading is the same: the only flag is the city-versus-rest test in the narrowest set, as before.

What the newer checks found. Each of these but the count of city-limit crossings had its plan written down before it was computed. None is registered, and most were planned after related results were known; the ledger records that (findings/test_ledger/README.md).

  • What the panel's changes do to seats. Against the panel minority's alternative (its ¶167 map, which undoes the changes south of Airdrie), the panel's map gives the NDP 1 fewer seat on all ballots and 2 fewer on election-day ballots. Every seat that differs is too close to call on the reconstruction. The sign is the same in the 2015, 2019 and 2023 elections and over most of a 10-point swing either way (findings/iap_para167/, findings/six_signals/).
  • Against neutral maps, the panel's map sits near the middle (UCP seats at the 43rd percentile; efficiency gap at the 48th). On those measures the ¶167 map sits nearer the edge. On seats at a tied vote the panel's map sits higher, at the 90th to 97th percentile against neutral maps with its own 91 seats, and there it is the panel's map, not the ¶167 map, that is nearer the edge.
  • Lethbridge. Neutral redraws of the region that respect city limits split Lethbridge two ways in 51% of plans, three ways in 48%, and four ways in 0.9%; the panel's map splits it four ways. The panel's map puts 45% of the city's NDP voters in seats the NDP loses by 10 points or more, and the competitive second city seat is gone. Against those redraws that sits at the 93rd percentile, just inside the line fixed in advance (findings/hybrid_regions/). How much those redraws respect city limits matters. Past commission maps imply a city-boundary weight between 0.52 and 0.66 (the redraws above use 0.5). At 0.52 the panel's map is at the 93rd percentile; at 0.66, the weight that matches the 2026 commission majority's own map, it is at the 97th, beyond the line (findings/city_weight/). Those weights were measured on redraws of the whole province and are assumed to mean the same in a redraw of one region; that has not been checked.
  • Where moved voters went. Of the NDP votes the panel's changes move, 14,199 go from a seat the NDP wins or nearly wins into one it loses by 10 points or more, and 3,152 go the other way. Most of it, about 10,000, comes from Lethbridge, and about 3,700 from Edmonton-Beaumont into Leduc-Beaumont; the Banff change adds little. The panel did not change NDP-held or competitive seats more often than others; it changed them less often (findings/local_targeting/). Against neutral redraws of the south and central region, the panel's layout there is ordinary and the commission majority's is the unusual one, partly because of how the redraws are built, so much of this flow is a move away from an unusual starting point.
  • City limits in past maps. Here a division counts as crossing a city limit when 5% to 95% of its people live in the city; one that reaches past the limit but holds almost only city residents is not counted. At Calgary's limit, the 2010 and 2017 maps and the commission majority's map have none, and the 2003 map had three. The panel's map has two (the Springbank divisions); the commission minority's had six. At Edmonton's limit, the 2003, 2010 and 2017 maps had none; the commission majority's map and the panel's each have two, and the minority's three (findings/city_weight/big_city_lines.json).
  • Rural voters. In Lethbridge County, the number of rural residents placed in divisions that are mostly city goes from 0 on the commission majority's map to 8,138 on the panel's. Rocky View County goes from 4 pieces to 6. On the check's own measure, one of the panel's own changes overrides a rural preference on 2023 votes: Edmonton-Nisku, which holds about 7,000 rural residents (findings/rural_voice/).
  • Which population the comparisons use. The neutral maps balance 2021 census population; the real maps were drawn on 2024 estimates. Calgary and Edmonton grew faster, and on 2024 figures they hold about two seats' worth more people (51.4 against 49.5). On each map's own published figures, city divisions average 9 to 10 points more people than the rest, compared with what the 2021 figures show (the panel's map: +13.4% against +4.3%). That is a limit on every city comparison in this report. A rerun of the neutral maps on 2024 figures is proposed (findings/population_basis/).
  • Every changed region. Each group of divisions the panel changed was redrawn without reference to party, at each map's own number of seats. The panel's layouts sit inside the normal range in every region, on every measure. The commission majority's layout of the south and central region is the unusual one: it packs more NDP votes into wide wins (Lethbridge-West and Canmore-Banff) than any of the redraws (50,000, drawn each of two ways), and puts fewer in lopsided losses. Part of that is built in. The redraws keep every district within 25% of the region's average and have no exception for remote divisions, so none can hold a district as small as the majority's Canmore-Banff or the panel's Rocky Mountain House-Sundre. Even Lethbridge-West on its own packs more NDP votes than 95% of the redraws do across the whole region, both ways. The panel's changes lower that packing (findings/changed_districts/).
  • All of it together. The newer checks made 132 percentile comparisons (and 11 other tests). Maps drawn without reference to party would put about 13 of them beyond the 5th or 95th percentile. 12 are there: 8 for the panel's map and the ¶167 map, and 4 for the commission majority's layout of the south. None holds up after correcting for the number of tests.

What would change my mind #

Each load-bearing finding has a written condition that would retract it. Most were set on April 24, 2026; later findings got theirs when they were added. If one is met, I'll publish the retraction. The project's written rule is within 48 hours of the condition becoming known.

  • Someone shows the trade-off was forced. If anyone shows that no legal Alberta map can satisfy the minority's own stated reasons for Airdrie, Cochrane, Nolan Hill and Rocky Mountain House–Banff Park while following municipal lines about as often as the majority's, I'll retract the finding that those boundaries can't be explained by their stated reasons. A map that does both would strengthen the finding instead. The challenge is open at Issue #14.
  • A document shows the reasons came first. If an internal commission document, dated before the minority's final choices, shows those choices were driven by community submissions I haven't seen, I'll retract the inference that they were drafting decisions.
  • The neighbour-drain pass turns out to be an artefact. If the continuous version of that score falls in the extreme upper tail (p < 0.05) of random permutations, I'll withdraw the minority's pass.
  • An election contradicts the simulation. Neither commission map will be used in 2027, so this can't be tested on them. If the 2027 election, on whatever map is enacted, gives a seat split that contradicts the projection from 2023 votes on that map, I'll revisit the simulation's percentile findings against the actual results.
  • A court distinguishes Lalande. In Quebec (AG) v. Lalande, 2026 SCC 13, the Supreme Court struck down Quebec's law pausing its commission, and Quebec then legislated its own map. If a court reviewing Alberta's route finds it constitutionally different, for example because handing a finished commission's work to an MLA committee isn't the same as freezing a commission mid-process, I'll retract the argument on the norms page that the two belong in the same class.

The November map can also weaken the overall conclusion, as described above. I correct mistakes in public. I don't delete them. Every correction is listed in the academic report's Documented Corrections section.

The author's view #

Everything above is the audit. This section is my opinion. Delete it and no number on this page changes.

Two facts are hard to explain innocently. The commissioners the government nominated drew a map that neutral map-drawing almost never produces: fewer than a hundred in a million draws lean that far. And when the commission split instead of endorsing that map, the government moved the job to a committee its own MLAs control.

Each fact, on its own, has an innocent explanation. A commissioner can be eccentric in good faith, and a legislature can lose patience with a split commission. Put the two facts in order, though, and the innocent explanations get harder to hold.

What worries me is that no law was broken. I checked, and that checking is most of what this audit is. The Act makes the commission's report advisory. It sets no notice period, and requires no draft, hearings or consultation before the Legislature acts. The protection was a convention, not a law, and a convention binds only a government that chooses to follow it. In 1995, after its 1992 committee, Alberta changed how commissions are appointed.

The committee that replaced the commission has a government majority and a government chair. It has an advisory panel, chaired by a retired judge who was appointed after the province's acting chief justice declined to help find one. The panel only advises. The MLAs vote, and they will run in the districts they approve. The map won't have a public hearing before it's final.

This government has set a convention aside before. Its first bill, as written, would have let cabinet rewrite laws without the Legislature, and that power was removed only after public outcry. Its own ethics commissioner found the Premier breached the conflicts law by pressing her attorney general about a criminal case. It passed a law letting cabinet repeal local bylaws and trigger the removal of elected councillors. When a court paused one of its laws in June 2025, it invoked the notwithstanding clause that December. Apart from the ethics breach, each of those moves was lawful, and each set aside a convention to get past an independent check.

I also worry about what the map buys. Replay the 2023 election-day vote and the minority map gives the UCP sixty seats of eighty-nine. Sixty seats would protect a government from floor-crossings and leave fewer opposition members to keep up scrutiny in committee. I'd worry about that in anyone's hands.

I could be wrong, and I've set out how you'd know. The panel's map went to the Assembly on November 2, and the rules that decide its verdict were fixed in June, before it was made public. If it lands inside the neutral range, my suspicion has taken real damage, and this site will say so within 72 hours of its release. I committed to that before I knew the answer.

— Will Conner

Fixes for next time #

Two problems make every audit of Alberta's maps harder than it needs to be. First, about half of all votes now come in before election day, and Elections Alberta reports them by division rather than by voting area. So roughly 810,000 NDP and UCP votes cast in 2023 can't be pinned to the voting area where they were cast. Second, commissions draw from census data that's already years old by the time a map takes effect. Both problems can be fixed. The proposals page explains how.
