Alberta Electoral Boundary Audit

Fixes for next time

Plain-language companion · what would make the next map easier to check

This audit ran into two data problems. Neither one is about the commission's work. Both come from how Alberta's electoral system is set up, and both can be fixed.

About half of Alberta's votes are now cast before election day, at advance polls, at mobile polls and by special ballot. Elections Alberta reports these votes only as totals for each riding, not for each voting area, the small areas a riding is made of. So about 810,000 NDP and UCP votes cast in 2023 cannot be tied to a neighbourhood on a map. They are counted. They just cannot be placed. Every advance voter is checked against a voters list before getting a ballot, and that list ties each voter to a voting area. To report advance votes by voting area, the count would sort advance ballots by area, joining small areas together to keep each vote secret. That changes how votes are counted, not how people vote.

This matters most to people who check a map. Commissions are right to draw from population, not votes, and the census already tells them where people live. But turnout by area would show which communities a map's ridings really reach. As far as the public record shows, commissioners see the same published results as everyone else.

In at least one community in northern Alberta, the gap is total. In the northern part of the Lesser Slave Lake riding lies a voting area of 4,832 km², more than four-fifths the size of Prince Edward Island. It was home to 844 people at the 2021 census. In 2023, not one vote there was cast at an election-day poll. Every vote went into the riding-wide totals for advance, mobile and special ballots. Those votes count in the riding's total, but they cannot be placed on a map. In the published results, the community does not appear at all.

When the commission first planned to remove the Lesser Slave Lake riding and merge it into a larger one, it had no vote data by place for those communities. In the end it kept the riding. That came after at least 56 public submissions, by the minority report’s count, said the region is one community of interest. Some, such as submission EBC-2025-2-0028, wrote about the riding's small northern Indigenous communities. The commission kept the riding under s. 15(2) of the Electoral Boundaries Commission Act, the exception for remote ridings set out below. The communities got there. But the published results could not show how many of their people voted.

SECTION 15(2) EBCA

Section 15(2) of Alberta's Electoral Boundaries Commission Act lets a commission protect a small riding, one with more than 25% fewer people than the provincial average. The riding must meet at least three of five tests: (a) an area over 20,000 km², or a surveyed area over 15,000 km²; (b) more than 150 km from the Legislature Building to the riding's nearest boundary, by the most direct highway route; (c) no town with more than 8,000 people; (d) an Indian reserve or a Métis settlement in the riding; and (e) part of the riding's boundary running along Alberta's border. Up to four ridings may be protected this way, down to 50% below average. The protection is not automatic. The commission must judge whether the tests are met, and it may choose not to use it.

The commission's first plan removed the riding because of its population. With about 27,000 people, Lesser Slave Lake had about half the provincial average: right at the legal floor. That looks like a plain use of the rules. It is not. The law lets a commission choose here, and Lesser Slave Lake meets three of the five tests, the number the Act asks for. Its area is 69,566 km² (test a: over 20,000 km²). No town in it has more than 8,000 people (test c). And it contains Indian reserves and Métis settlements (test d). Those communities share a northern geography and a common interest in an MLA who answers mainly to northern Alberta. Test b, more than 150 km from the Legislature to the riding's nearest boundary by highway, is not met on my road model. The model gives 134 km for the riding as both final maps draw it.

The first plan merged Lesser Slave Lake with Peace River into one riding called Mackenzie. That would have folded those communities' shared voice into the largest riding in the province. The communities had not changed. The lines around them had. Together, the three tests it meets describe a large, thinly settled riding, home to communities whose stake in representation does not show in raw population counts. The commission's final report counted four tests met. On my road model it is three, which is still enough. The small population did not force the riding's removal. Section 15(2) let the commission keep it once it added people. The final report added Big Lakes County, Swan Hills and part of Woodlands County, bringing the riding to 45% below average. The commission's own words for its first plan: "we erred."

There is another side to this story. All five commissioners agreed to the first plan, which removed Lesser Slave Lake. Both final maps then protected it under s. 15(2). The minority also used s. 15(2) for Rocky Mountain House–Banff Park, which it stretched into the empty part of Banff National Park. The commission's majority, its own chair among them, called it "a bad faith effort" in the official final report. My check undercuts their charge: the riding qualifies under s. 15(2) even without the park land. Still, on the minority's map, an exception meant for remote places where people live was stretched over empty parkland. The 91-seat map that followed was drawn by an advisory panel, overseen by a committee of MLAs. Section 15(2) binds the commission. As I read Government Motion 37, it gave the panel the same exception. No law required public hearings on the panel's map, and none were held.

The other problem is timing. Alberta should wait for the 2026 census before drawing the next map. Canada counts its people every five years. The 2026 census was taken in May 2026, and its first results are due in February 2027. The commission whose maps this audit checks had to start from the 2021 census, brought up to July 2024 with a provincial estimate. That count was already four years old when the maps were drawn. Alberta grows fast, and not evenly. On the audit's population estimates, the 2019 riding of Airdrie-East grew by about 30% between the 2021 census and mid-2025, while Athabasca-Barrhead-Westlock grew by about 6%. So a map drawn from old counts is out of date from the day it is first used. A simple change to the Electoral Boundaries Commission Act could fix that. It could require that a new commission be appointed only after Statistics Canada releases the newest small-area data from the latest census. The result would be maps that show where Albertans live now, not where they lived years before.

The committee worked under the existing law, which sets no rule on census timing. It could not delay past its November 2, 2026 deadline on its own. This proposal is for a future change to the Electoral Boundaries Commission Act, not for the current process. The tension is real. The 91-seat map was drawn from a census that was five years old when the map was delivered. Naming that limit is more useful than pretending it is not there.

Neither data problem is a finding about the commission's maps. Both are about a system that makes checking a map harder than it needs to be. I offer them as practical suggestions, not conclusions. Both can be fixed, and fixing them would give every future commission, and every future audit, firmer ground.
