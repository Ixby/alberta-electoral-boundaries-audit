that is not correct. In Clause C.(e)(ii)(B), the Legislature was simply directing the panel with respect to a
particular proposal it wished to see in the panel's proposals. The advisory panel was given more than one
task. One was to review and make proposals. Another was to divide the Province into 91 proposed electoral
divisions. Yet another was to incorporate the Chairman's recommendations in its proposals, subject to any
modifications the independent advisory panel considered necessary. And finally, the panel was to report to the
Legislature its proposals based on its review.

But for Clause C. (e)(ii)(B), the panel might not have incorporated any part of the Chairman's Recommendation
5. And so, Clause C.(e)(ii)(B) was clearly not superfluous. The Legislature, in Clause C.(e)(ii)(B), was simply
indicating that one proposal it wished to see was a proposal to restore the two deleted electoral divisions. How
that was proposed to be accomplished was to be incorporated into the panel's proposals.

Administrative law principles about ensuring the delegated power are consistent with the grant of authority has
no application here. No legislative or other authority was delegated. The advisory panel was simply asked for
advice, which it is now giving.

That two of our panel members would rely on principles of delegated authority is revealing. It suggests a
mistaken view of the electoral boundary determination process. Electoral boundaries commissions in this
Province are not delegated any legislative or other authority or power. Their function, as set out in Section 3 of
the Act, is simply to review the existing electoral boundaries" and make proposals. The panel's mandate was
identical; the same wording as is in the Act is in Government Motion 37. The Commission's proposals may or
may not be approved. The advisory panel's proposals may or may not be approved. But it is the Legislature
which does the approving or rejecting. It is the Legislature which has not only the ultimate authority to
determine electoral boundaries, but also ultimate responsibility for their fairness. If it were otherwise, in the
extreme event of a commission recommending patently unfair electoral boundaries, the Legislature would be
powerless to correct it.

Likewise, the doctrine of collateral attack has no application. Contrary to what the minority asserts, no
issues have been litigated before or decided by the Commission. The Commission has no authority to decide
anything. Electoral boundaries commissions do not determine electoral boundaries. A Commission's only
authority is to make proposals to the Legislature as to the area, boundaries and names of the Province's
electoral divisions. And those proposals may be accepted, rejected or approved with alterations. In other
words, those proposals are subject to review by the Legislature. The advisory panel has likewise been asked to
assist the Legislature in that task, but only to the extent of making proposals which the Legislature can accept,
reject or approve with alterations.

Nor does the concept of collateral attack apply to the facts of this case because, as the minority is aware, the
proposals of the Commission have in fact been accorded a great deal of deference by the panel in formulating
its proposals.

Because it is called a "commission" and is chaired by a judge does not change the fundamental nature of an
electoral boundaries commission. It is not a quasi-judicial tribunal. Its function is limited to simply making
proposals, to advising the Legislature. It is simply an advisory body. The 2025-2026 Electoral Boundaries
Commission, and much less its Chairman acting alone, had no statutory or other authority to "impose"
conditions on its or his recommendations or to dictate how its proposals were to be implemented.

We deal with the Chairman's recommendations elsewhere in this report. They were not non-partisan. As the
minority opinion correctly points out, they had partisan implications. They were made for the express purpose
of persuading the Legislature to uphold the Chairman's view of the electoral map of Alberta.

Nor were the Chairman's recommendations binding. They were suggestions presented for consideration,
couched in terms of a recommendation. They were merely terms of a proposed compromise. They were made
by the Chairman to persuade the Legislature to approve the majority's proposals. And the Legislature may yet be
persuaded to approve the majority's proposals. But the Chairman's Recommendation 5 was no more and no less
than a proposed compromise, offered for consideration and designed to persuade the Legislature to approve the
Commission's majority proposals and to dissuade it from approving the Commission's minority proposals.
