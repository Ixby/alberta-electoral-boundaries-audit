Lethbridge already has population to spare. And if it grows any more, its population will have to be shared
with electoral divisions outside of Lethbridge because it will have too much population for two electoral
divisions and not enough population for three electoral divisions. Lethbridge is almost at that point right
now. A similar but smaller problem in Medicine Hat led to the creation of Brooks-Medicine Hat and Cypress-
Medicine Hat.

Lethbridge is not an isolated urban municipality in the midst of a void. It is southern Alberta's principal
service, education, health, justice, transportation and agri-food centre. Its government-funded institutions,
its hospitals, its courts and its universities, are built for a regional population far beyond the city's
boundaries. The Highway 3, 4 and 23 agri-food economy, irrigation network, processing plants, research
institutions, logistics chains and municipal partnerships link Lethbridge directly to Coaldale, Taber, Warner,
Cardston, the Little Bow country and the southwestern Foothills. The panel is recommending electoral
boundaries that reflect those lived relationships rather than treat the city limits as an impermeable wall.

The proposed redistribution balances two legitimate objectives. First, Lethbridge has grown large and diverse
enough to contain a distinct inner-city community of interest that warrants separate representation and its
own electoral division. Second, residents elsewhere in the city and in surrounding rural communities share
services, employment, infrastructure and economic interests that are best represented through measured
urban-rural electoral divisions. This is not hybridization for an improper purpose. It is a model grounded in
the desire to achieve effective representation.

The proposed reconfiguration is neither extreme nor unprecedented. There are numerous examples
throughout the Province where population, geography, community history, community interests,
transportation and practical representation have justified departures from purely municipal or urban-
rural lines. Lethbridge serves a regional population much larger than its municipal population. Chinook
Regional Hospital provides specialized acute, cancer, intensive, neonatal and ambulatory services for all of
southwestern Alberta. The University of Lethbridge and Lethbridge Polytechnic draw students and support
research from across the south. Courts, provincial offices, entertainment facilities, professional services,
retail and transportation infrastructure were expressly intended to serve surrounding municipalities and the
Blood Tribe. For many rural residents, Lethbridge is their city in every practical sense. Lethbridge is not there
solely for its resident population. It is also there for the people of southern Alberta.

The economic evidence is equally strong. Canada's Premier Food Corridor is a formal regional partnership
extending along Highway 3 and linking the City of Lethbridge, Lethbridge County, Coaldale, Taber and the
Municipal District of Taber and related communities. It exists because the agri-food supply chain already
functions regionally: irrigation, primary production, research, food processing, labour, finance, rail and
highway logistics are mutually dependent. A municipal boundary model isolates Lethbridge from these
communities of interest and obscures the very relationships which section 14 of the Electoral Boundaries
Commission Act requires the Committee to consider.

The minority completely loses credibility when it argues a lack of a clear and obvious shared community of
interest in Lethbridge and southern Alberta. The assertion that agriculture is the sixth largest employer "by
industry classification" begs the question what "industries" employ more. No reference or explanation is
given for the assertion. It may be that government or even health care services are bigger employers. It may
be that educational institutions, schools, universities, technical training institutes employ more people. It
may even be that retail, commercial and hospitality services employ more people. But these employers and
their employees are only needed if there is a population creating wealth and the need for their services.

Agriculture and agriculture-related industries account for nearly 20% of GDP in the Lethbridge region.
Roughly 4,000 people in Lethbridge alone work directly for local food processing plants. And that is only a
portion of the sector. Farmers and ranchers and farm workers are typically self-employed and perhaps are
not counted as an "industry classification" employee.
