## The organizing idea

The report stops arguing a case and starts processing a scene. Readers came
looking for a smoking gun. There isn't one, and the report says so in the
first hundred words — not as a hedge but as the thesis. What the audit found
is **casings**: pieces of evidence, each real, each measured, each of which
would matter somewhere — in a US courtroom, in a peer-reviewed journal, under
a different statute — and none of which, alone or together, can legally prove
the thing everyone intuits. The report walks the reader through every casing,
one at a time, asking the same five questions every time, in the same order,
under the same headers. The repetition IS the argument. The frustration is
not stated; it accumulates. The order speaks the case.

**The discipline enforces the no-verdict rule structurally.** Beat 2 states
the inference the reader wants to draw. Beat 5 takes it away. The report
never has to declare a gerrymander because the form makes the reader feel
exactly where the proof gap sits — fourteen times.

## The five-beat litany (fixed headers, verbatim, every casing)

1. **What we found** — the evidence, stated plainly, with the number.
2. **What it could show** — the inference a reasonable person wants to draw
   from it. Stated fairly, at full strength. Never strawmanned.
3. **What the experts say** — how this signal is treated in the redistricting
   literature and in jurisdictions that do litigate it (US courts, academic
   metrics). Why serious people treat it as evidence.
4. **What Canadian practice expects** — the norm: what commissions in Canada
   actually do, and what this map/process did instead.
5. **Why it isn't a smoking gun** — the specific legal or epistemic reason
   this evidence cannot prove the case in Alberta. **Each casing carries ONE
   legal lesson**, so by the end the reader has absorbed the entire /law page
   through repetition instead of a lecture.

Refrain, closing every casing: **A casing, not a gun.**
Honesty variant for retracted/failed items: **Not even a casing. We logged it
anyway.**

## Structure of the rewritten report

- **Cold open:** the scorecard as a light teaser. One figure, minimal words:
  "Two maps, drawn in the same room, from the same rules and data. Five
  structural tests, four fairness measures. One map is not like the other.
  By the end of this report you will know exactly what this picture can — and
  cannot — prove." Then the smoking-gun/casings frame, then the litany begins.
- **Act I — What the map does** (casings 1–5: visible geometry, no votes needed)
- **Act II — What the simulation sees** (casings 6–10: the 1,010,000-map lens)
- **Act III — What people did** (casings 11–14: conduct and process)
- **The scorecard, revisited** — the synthesis, run through the same five
  beats one last time, keyed to November 2, 2026.
- **The author's view** — the severable opinion section (separate draft,
  author-approved before integration).
- Corrections box, retraction conditions, methods/law links: all retained.

## ACT I — WHAT THE MAP DOES

### Casing 1 — A city of 85,805 with zero seats

- **What we found:** The minority map splits Airdrie — larger than Red Deer;
  one council, one tax bill, one school division — across four divisions
  (Calgary-Airdrie, Calgary-Foothills-Airdrie West, Olds-Three Hills-Didsbury,
  Airdrie East), each primarily identified with a different surrounding
  jurisdiction. Airdrie residents are a minority in all four. The majority
  map splits it two ways; the 2022 federal redistribution also went two ways.
- **What it could show:** the textbook definition of *cracking* — splitting a
  community thinly so it decides nothing. If you wanted to dilute a fast-
  growing, politically contested city, this is what it would look like.
- **What the experts say:** community splitting is a primary red flag in the
  US literature; the audit's own pre-registered cracking test (academic
  §5.3.2) fires on every criterion here, and on none for the majority's split.
- **What Canadian practice expects:** commissions treat municipal integrity
  as a core community-of-interest anchor; a two-way split of a city this size
  is normal, a four-way split leaving residents a minority everywhere is not.
- **Why it isn't a smoking gun:** the EBCA permits splitting municipalities.
  Community of interest is one non-binding factor among several, and the
  Saskatchewan Reference tells courts to defer to how boundary bodies weigh
  those factors. No Canadian court has ever struck a map for splitting a
  city. *(Legal lesson: the statute's factors are discretionary, and courts
  defer.)*
- **A casing, not a gun.**

### Casing 2 — The three boundaries the chair called out

- **What we found:** the commission's own chair flagged the minority map's
  Rocky Mountain House–Banff Park extension through uninhabited national
  park, the Nolan Hill–Cochrane lasso corridor, and the Olds reach into north
  Airdrie. He called the park extension "a bad faith effort" to claim a
  s.15(2) population protection written for remote communities where people
  actually live. The audit's blind geometric tests — which owe the chair
  nothing — independently confirmed three of his four geometric flags. The
  majority map received zero flags from the same chair (noting he signed it,
  so his silence there is expected; the audit's independent confirmation is
  what gives the flags weight).
- **What it could show:** an insider — the person legally responsible for the
  process — looked at these lines and publicly alleged the protection was
  being gamed. That is as close to direct evidence as this record gets.
- **What the experts say:** bizarre-shape analysis is where gerrymandering
  detection started; corridors and uninhabited extensions are the canonical
  signatures. (Fairness beat kept: the Banff placement has a defensible
  community-of-interest logic — the report's existing two-sided treatment of
  the townsite/park question carries over intact.)
- **What Canadian practice expects:** commissioners disagree in reports all
  the time — but a chair accusing colleagues of bad faith in the official
  final report is, in the cycles this audit reviewed, unprecedented.
- **Why it isn't a smoking gun:** the chair's words are opinion inside a
  report that has no legal force. "Bad faith" in a commission report is an
  accusation, not a finding; no tribunal ever adjudicated it, and under the
  EBCA both reports were advisory the moment they were filed. *(Legal
  lesson: commission reports bind no one — the legislature holds the pen.)*
- **A casing, not a gun.**

### Casing 3 — One zone of Calgary, drawn a quarter too big

- **What we found:** in NE/central Calgary ("Zone A"), the minority's
  divisions average 11.5% above provincial-average population, versus 2.8%
  on the majority — same zone, same commission, same constraints. Thirteen
  of the zone's seventeen districts went NDP in 2023.
- **What it could show:** *packing* — oversizing districts where your
  opponent's voters live so each of their ballots weighs less.
- **What the experts say:** packing/cracking is the mechanism behind every
  US gerrymandering case; the audit's pre-registered packing signature test
  (academic §5.3.1) fires here.
- **What Canadian practice expects:** deviations inside the band are normal,
  but commissions justify them with community-of-interest reasons — and the
  minority's published rationales for this zone are among those that fail
  independent check (see Casing 11).
- **Why it isn't a smoking gun:** every one of those districts sits inside
  the EBCA's ±25% band, and Canadian law has no cause of action for partisan
  *effect*. Section 3 of the Charter protects effective representation of
  citizens, not proportional translation of party votes into seats. *(Legal
  lesson: inside the band, size asymmetry is lawful discretion; partisan
  effect is not a recognized harm.)*
- **A casing, not a gun.**

### Casing 4 — The map that's worse at the one thing it had to be good at

- **What we found:** the minority's population spread (MAD 4,707 on the
  commission's own tables) is 48% wider than the majority's (3,180). On the
  audit's official-shapefile substrate its spread sits at the 99th percentile
  of 1,010,000 neutral maps — only about 1 in 100 neutral draws is worse.
- **What it could show:** the map sacrificed the statute's central value —
  vote equality — to achieve its shapes. Neutral processes don't pay this
  price; something else was being bought.
- **What the experts say:** population equality is the first-order constraint
  in every redistricting formalism; a plan in the 99th percentile of spread
  is paying for something.
- **What Canadian practice expects:** commissions treat the band as a limit,
  not a budget; the drift toward tighter equality has been the norm since
  Carter.
- **Why it isn't a smoking gun:** the ±25% band is the *only* numeric line
  in the statute, and no district crosses it. The law does not rank maps
  inside the band — 99th percentile of spread and 50th percentile of spread
  are legally identical. *(Legal lesson: the statute has exactly one number,
  and this map complies with it.)*
- **A casing, not a gun.**

### Casing 5 — The casing that dissolved when we picked it up

- **What we found:** early analysis showed the minority anchoring to
  municipal boundaries only 15% of the time — 4.9× below the Canadian norm.
  On official Elections Alberta shapefiles the figure died: majority 80%,
  minority 72%, both inside the 70–85% norm (Quebec 78%, Ontario 82%, BC 71%).
  The retraction is permanent and displayed. What survives is narrower: the
  minority's *departures* from civic lines concentrate exactly at the
  chair-flagged urban-edge zones where urban-rural pairing decides seats.
- **What it could show:** (as originally believed) systematic abandonment of
  recognizable civic geography.
- **What the experts say:** this is what an artifact looks like — the
  provisional map tracings manufactured a signal the real shapefiles erased.
- **What Canadian practice expects:** nothing — there is no anchoring
  requirement anywhere in Canadian law or practice guidance; the norm is
  descriptive.
- **Why it isn't a smoking gun:** it isn't even evidence. It's the audit's
  own error, kept on display because a report that shows you its dead casings
  is the only kind whose live ones you should trust. *(Epistemic lesson: the
  null can win, and when it does, we say so.)*
- **Not even a casing. We logged it anyway.**

## ACT II — WHAT THE SIMULATION SEES

*(Act II opens with the one-paragraph plain-language explanation of the
ensemble: 1,010,000 maps drawn by a neutral procedure on the same official
geography, same legal constraints, no vote data consulted — the "what would
maps look like if nobody was trying" machine. Link to /methods.)*

### Casing 6 — Fewer than 100 maps in 1,010,000

- **What we found:** hold the province at a perfect 50/50 vote and ask each
  map how many seats it hands the UCP. Neutral median: 44.8%. Majority map:
  46.1% (78th percentile — normal). Minority map: 51.7% — the 99.99th
  percentile; 66 of 1,010,000 neutral maps reach it. On mean-median it sits
  at p99.97, on declination p98.79. On actual recent voting patterns the
  minority geometry yields 60 of 89 seats.
- **What it could show:** neutral processes essentially never produce this
  map. It is the kind of map you have to aim at.
- **What the experts say:** ensemble outlier analysis is the state of the
  art (the method US courts found persuasive in state cases); three of four
  pre-registered metrics in the extreme tail, all four directionally aligned,
  is a strong outlier profile.
- **What Canadian practice expects:** no Canadian commission has ever been
  required to test its map against an ensemble — this is the audit importing
  a standard the process itself never had to meet.
- **Why it isn't a smoking gun:** no Canadian court has ever admitted
  ensemble evidence, and in the US the Supreme Court held (Rucho, 2019) that
  even good statistical evidence of partisan gerrymandering is not for
  federal courts to police. A percentile is a description, not a threshold
  crossed. *(Legal lesson: the best evidence type in the field has no legal
  home in Canada.)*
- **A casing, not a gun.**

### Casing 7 — The test that missed

- **What we found:** the fourth metric, efficiency gap, came in at +4.0% —
  the 94th percentile. The audit's pre-registered outlier line is the 95th.
  It missed. Directionally UCP-favoured, but below the line, and reported
  that way everywhere.
- **What it could show:** taken alone, almost nothing — which is the point
  of including it.
- **What the experts say:** the EG's proposed US threshold (~7%) has been
  adopted by no court; near-misses are the natural behaviour of honest
  batteries — four-for-four should make a reader more suspicious of the
  auditor, not the map.
- **What Canadian practice expects:** n/a — no Canadian body uses EG at all.
- **Why it isn't a smoking gun:** it isn't even claimed as one. This casing
  is in the pile to show the pile was counted honestly. *(Epistemic lesson:
  pre-registration cuts both ways — the same locked line that gives the hits
  their force makes this a miss, and it stays a miss.)*
- **A casing, not a gun — and this one we don't even fire.**

### Casing 8 — One in 568,000, and what that number is not

- **What we found:** the joint probability that a neutral process produces
  the minority's combined four-metric profile is bounded at p ≤ 1.76×10⁻⁶ —
  about one in 568,000, Bonferroni-corrected, dependence-robust. The report
  used to say one in fifteen million; that figure combined two channels that
  were not independent, and was retired. The correction is displayed.
- **What it could show:** the reader wants this to be "the probability the
  map is innocent."
- **What the experts say:** it is the probability of the *map's numbers*
  under a *neutral drawing process* — a well-defined, defensible statistical
  statement, and the audit's strongest single number.
- **What Canadian practice expects:** no Canadian legal standard consumes a
  number like this; there is no threshold it could cross.
- **Why it isn't a smoking gun:** P(evidence | innocence) is not
  P(innocence | evidence) — treating one as the other is the prosecutor's
  fallacy, and this report refuses it. A one-in-568,000 map can still be the
  product of eccentric-but-sincere judgment; the number cannot see motive.
  *(Legal-epistemic lesson: no probability of a map is a probability of
  intent.)*
- **A casing, not a gun.**

### Casing 9 — We tried to shoot our own result down

- **What we found:** the tail position survives swapping the sampler's
  mathematical machinery (Forest-ReCom robustness check, OSF he53s: all four
  metrics shift less than one percentile point — pre-registered verdict
  ROBUST). It survives an independent software implementation. And one of
  our own claims *failed* its falsification test: the audit tested whether
  the flagged geometry (the corridors, the park extension) is the specific
  mechanism producing the seat advantage — it is not, and the report says so.
- **What it could show:** the outlier is a property of the map, not of the
  audit's tooling.
- **What the experts say:** robustness across samplers and implementations is
  exactly what distinguishes a finding from an artifact.
- **What Canadian practice expects:** nothing requires any of this; the audit
  imposed it on itself.
- **Why it isn't a smoking gun:** ruling out artifacts is not ruling in
  design. Surviving every attack we could mount still only establishes that
  the map is genuinely extreme — never *why*. *(Epistemic lesson:
  robustness strengthens the description, not the accusation.)*
- **A casing, not a gun.**

### Casing 10 — The signals we retired

- **What we found:** two of the audit's own instruments died under better
  nulls. The Swing-Zone Allocation Test looked significant (p = 0.0024)
  until spatial clustering among neighbouring voting areas was properly
  accounted for — corrected p ≈ 0.19, not significant, retired to
  exploratory. The Fisher combination (source of the old one-in-fifteen-
  million) assumed independence its channels didn't have — retired. Both
  retirements are permanent and displayed.
- **What it could show:** (as originally computed) that the specific
  boundary choices, not just the overall map, were partisan-loaded.
- **What the experts say:** spatial autocorrelation is the classic way
  redistricting statistics fool their makers; finding it in your own work
  and correcting it is the method working.
- **What Canadian practice expects:** n/a.
- **Why it isn't a smoking gun:** these were our casings, not theirs.
  *(Epistemic lesson, second time, on purpose: the null can win. A report
  that never loses an argument with itself isn't checking.)*
- **Not even casings anymore. We logged them anyway.**

## ACT III — WHAT PEOPLE DID

### Casing 11 — Five of six reasons don't survive checking

- **What we found:** of the minority commissioners' published rationales for
  their contested redraws, five of six fail independent verification against
  public data — Cochrane's "fluid movement" into Calgary says nothing about
  the Nolan Hill neighbourhood the corridor reaches; Chestermere shares no
  school division or transit with the Calgary district it's sliced into;
  Sylvan Lake and Red Deer are in different school divisions; Airdrie's real
  growth never required a four-way split. In each case the underlying
  community tie is real and a boundary honouring it *could* have been drawn —
  the particular line actually drawn is not the one the evidence points to.
  (A seventh claimed failure was itself retracted when its source couldn't
  be located — the discipline applies to us too.)
- **What it could show:** rationales that don't survive checking look like
  rationalizations — reasons found after the line, not before.
- **What the experts say:** pretext analysis — comparing stated justification
  to what the evidence supports — is exactly how US courts smoke out intent
  in racial-gerrymandering cases.
- **What Canadian practice expects:** commissions publish reasons precisely
  so the public can weigh them; reasons that fail on public data defeat the
  purpose of publishing them.
- **Why it isn't a smoking gun:** no Canadian law requires a commissioner's
  rationale to survive independent check, and a failed rationale legally
  establishes sloppiness just as well as pretext. Courts reviewing boundary
  decisions ask whether the outcome denies effective representation — not
  whether the file's reasoning was good. *(Legal lesson: bad reasons for a
  lawful line are still a lawful line.)*
- **A casing, not a gun.**

### Casing 12 — The commission that couldn't sign one map

- **What we found:** the commission split — two final reports, incompatible
  maps, filed the same day (March 23, 2026), the first split in the Canadian
  cycles this audit reviewed. The government-nominated commissioners wrote
  the minority report; the chair's side produced the majority. The audit's
  tests cut against the chair too where the data went that way (his
  "no public support" claim was overbroad; the audit said so) — the
  instruments have no client.
- **What it could show:** the split itself is the anomaly — a process
  designed to converge on one answer produced two, and the outlier map came
  from the government's nominees.
- **What the experts say:** who appointed whom is standard evidence in
  capture analysis; symmetric application of the tests is what makes the
  asymmetric result informative.
- **What Canadian practice expects:** consensus reports are the norm;
  the machinery assumes one map arrives.
- **Why it isn't a smoking gun:** nomination is not instruction. The audit
  found no evidence of coordination between the government and its nominees,
  and none is inferable from appointment alone — commissioners of every
  provenance have gone their own way. *(Epistemic lesson: provenance raises
  the question; it cannot answer it.)*
- **A casing, not a gun.**

### Casing 13 — When the process might have answered, it was replaced

- **What we found:** three weeks after the split reports arrived, Motion 19
  (April 16, 2026) set both aside and handed the drawing to a Special Select
  Committee of five MLAs — three UCP, two NDP, chaired by UCP MLA Brandon
  Lunty — with a 91-seat mandate and a report due November 2, 2026. The
  committee is not required to hold public hearings and will publish no
  draft for the public to contest. The commission it replaced held hearings
  in sixteen communities, received 1,147 written submissions, and reversed
  its interim proposal after 80+ of them.
- **What it could show:** when the independent route failed to deliver a
  usable answer, the government took the pen — and removed every mechanism
  by which the public could check what it writes.
- **What the experts say:** civil-liberties observers (CCLA) publicly raised
  the politicization concern; the design forfeits every transparency feature
  the field considers protective.
- **What Canadian practice expects:** this is the deepest norm in Canadian
  redistribution — politicians do not draw their own districts. An MLA
  committee drafting the map mid-cycle is without precedent in the cycles
  this audit reviewed (Bratt).
- **Why it isn't a smoking gun:** every step is lawful. The EBCA makes the
  commission's report advisory, sets no minimum notice or comment period,
  and imposes no hearing requirement on what follows. Parliamentary
  sovereignty means the legislature may lawfully do exactly this. *(Legal
  lesson: the process that could have created accountability is optional,
  and opting out is legal.)*
- **A casing, not a gun.**

### Casing 14 — The sixty-year norm, set aside in an afternoon

- **What we found:** Canada spent six decades building independent boundary
  commissions precisely so that voters choose their representatives and not
  the reverse (Courtney; the norm's history is in the monograph's §2).
  Motion 19 suspends that architecture for this cycle. Nothing prevents the
  next government, of any party, from citing it as precedent.
- **What it could show:** the injury isn't one map — it's the demonstration
  that the guardrail was a convention, and conventions only bind governments
  that consent to be bound.
- **What the experts say:** the comparative literature treats independent
  commissions as the single most protective institutional feature against
  gerrymandering; removing the institution matters more than any one
  boundary.
- **What Canadian practice expects:** every province and the federal process
  use arm's-length commissions. That is the standard this cycle departed
  from.
- **Why it isn't a smoking gun:** constitutional conventions are politically
  binding but legally unenforceable — a court will name a convention and
  still decline to enforce it. The norm's violation is real, documented,
  and judicially irrelevant. *(Legal lesson, the last one: norms are not
  law, and the gap between them is where this entire episode lives.)*
- **A casing, not a gun.**

## THE SCORECARD, REVISITED — the synthesis

Return to the figure from the cold open — now the reader can read it. Then
run the litany one final time, on the instrument itself:

- **What we found:** fourteen casings, no gun — and one scorecard. It is the
  best instrument the author could build to correlate all of this into
  something that can be stated, checked, and falsified: five pre-registered
  structural tests (the minority crosses every discriminating one; the
  majority crosses none; the fifth is retracted and shown), four partisan-
  fairness metrics against 1,010,000 neutral maps (three in the extreme
  tail, one reported miss).
- **What it could show:** the closest thing to a gerrymandering *signal*
  that can exist under Alberta law and Canadian norms — a disciplined way of
  saying "this map is the kind of map that aiming produces."
- **What the experts say:** composite outlier batteries are how the field
  reads maps when intent is unobservable; pre-registration is what keeps
  them honest.
- **What Canadian practice expects:** nothing — no Canadian institution
  scores maps this way, which is exactly why the audit had to build one.
- **Why it isn't a smoking gun:** its thresholds are the audit's own
  calibrations, not legal lines; Alberta is one province with one split
  commission — n = 1; and no accumulation of casings becomes a gun, because
  the gap between "extreme" and "deliberate" is not statistical. It is the
  gap the law chose to leave open.
- **A casing, not a gun. But it is a casing that will fire again.**

**The November hinge (grounded):** the Special Select Committee chaired by
Brandon Lunty, MLA for Leduc-Beaumont, reports by **November 2, 2026**. The
same pre-registered tests (OSF qsgy8: hearings held? advisers named? does
the map honour the recommendation it claims to implement? where does it land
on every metric above?) fire on that map within 72 hours of release,
whatever direction the results point — the commitment was registered before
the map existed. If the committee's map lands inside the neutral bands, this
scorecard will be the instrument that says so.

**The playbook section (author-directed, 2026-07-10).** Before the November
hinge, the report states — in the audit's neutral voice — what it would look
like to *run the play again*: the full signature, spelled out as a testable
profile (process criteria: no hearings, no draft, advisers unnamed or ToR
unpublished, Recommendation-5 conditions unmet; outcome criteria: tail
position on the registered metrics, structural casings recurring). That
profile IS the pre-registration; the report's framing is: "We cannot prove a
gerrymander — the word has no legal home here. We can say, in advance and in
public, exactly what the signature looks like, and then say whether the
November map carries it. Pass or fail. The test is already registered." The
registration's own name for this is **the Lunty test** (november spec: "the
confirmatory counterpart" — if the committee's map replicates the minority's
two-lane signature under different members and constraints, the structural
finding earns confirmatory status; if not, the minority verdict stands as an
isolated exploratory observation). Findings voice uses "the Lunty test" /
"the registered signature" only. Any personalized coinage for the maneuver
is the author's, and lives exclusively in the severable Author's View.

## "Know more" appendices (author-directed, 2026-07-10)

Every casing carries an inline **Know more →** link, and the report ends
with an Appendices index collecting all fourteen. Each appendix page owns
exactly one concept and walks a fixed pedagogical arc: **"this is
confusing" → "now I understand."**

The arc, per page:
1. **Name the confusion.** Open with the honest reader reaction ("A
   percentile of a simulation? What does that even mean?").
2. **Build the concept once, concretely.** One toy example on Alberta-shaped
   facts (a 3-district mini-map, a 10-map mini-ensemble) — never abstract
   notation first.
3. **Return to the casing.** Re-read the casing's actual number with the new
   concept ("now re-read: 66 of 1,010,000 — you can see what that claims,
   and what it doesn't").
4. **Close with the limit.** One sentence on what the concept cannot tell
   you — the appendix teaches the tool *and* its edge, mirroring beat 5.
5. **Back to Casing N** link.

Concept map (appendix letter = casing number):
- A (Airdrie) — community of interest; what "cracking" means mechanically
- B (chair flags) — shape analysis; what s.15(2) protection is for
- C (Calgary zone) — packing, with a toy 3-district example of vote weight
- D (population spread) — MAD; why the ±25% band exists; band vs budget
- E (anchoring retraction) — data artifacts; provisional vs official geometry
- F (ensemble tail) — what a neutral ensemble is (the "what-if machine");
  reading a percentile
- G (EG miss) — the efficiency gap in one worked example; why thresholds
  are calibrations
- H (joint bound) — P(evidence|innocence) vs P(innocence|evidence); the
  prosecutor's fallacy in plain language
- I (robustness) — what a sampler is; why swapping it matters; falsification
- J (retired signals) — spatial autocorrelation ("neighbours vote alike");
  why combining tests can double-count
- K (rationales) — how to check a stated reason against public data
- L (commission split) — how commissioners are appointed; what nomination
  does and does not imply
- M (Motion 19) — the EBCA process end-to-end; what "advisory" means;
  parliamentary sovereignty
- N (norms) — conventions vs law; how norm erosion works (backsliding)

Implementation: `/explainers/<slug>` routes (prerendered, i18n keys,
unnumbered — appendices are lettered, not numbered, so they never disturb
the section chain). Inline link at the foot of each casing's litany block;
Appendices index section before References. FK grade 11 holds; each page
one concept, no cross-dependencies — a reader can land on any appendix cold
from a shared link.
