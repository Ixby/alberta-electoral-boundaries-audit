72. Third, providing the Panel 4.5 months from the appointment of the panelists (compared to the nearly 12
     months in which the ABEBC had a clear mandate to draw all 89 boundaries for the province) indicates
     that the legislature conceived of the Panel's mandate as narrower than the ABEBC. The legislature must
     have known that such a task would be difficult without extraordinary support. That cautions against a
     broad interpretation of the Panel's mandate, which would render inadequate the supports Motion 37
     granted.

73. Fourth, a narrow interpretation of the Panel's mandate mitigates the legislative abrogation of procedural
     fairness that occurred through Motion 37. The ABEBC heard from hundreds of Albertans orally, and
     received over 1,000 written submissions. The Panel did none of that due to time constraints. Of course,
     procedural fairness considerations from Baker v Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration),
     [1999] 2 SCR 817 can be overridden by the legislature--and indeed they have been by Motion 37's limiting
     the Panel holding any public hearings. But departures from common law duties of fairness must be
     interpreted strictly: see Knight v Indian Head School Division No 19, [1990] 1 SCR 653. This should
     caution against making drastic changes to the ABEBC Majority Report maps. Otherwise, this legislative
     abrogation of common law procedural fairness will be greater than necessary.

74. Fifth, this narrow interpretation of the Panel mandate minimizes common law problems regarding
     collateral attack. The IAP Majority implies that this Panel is a "do-over" of the ABEBC's determinations.
     We disagree. An independent commission has already rejected the arguments that the changes to
     Lethbridge and Calgary are necessary to achieve effective representation. It offends basic principles of
     procedural law to be re-raising these arguments afresh to a new body. Re-raising these arguments is
     contrary to efficiency and fairness, rationales that permeate the common law doctrine of res judicata: see
     Janet Walker, et al, Civil Litigation, 2d ed (Toronto: Irwin Law/University of Toronto Press, 2026) at 71-
     73. Relitigating these decisions unnecessarily increases this Panel's work and constitutes a poor use of
     public resources: see the decision of Justices Cromwell and Karakatsanis in Penner v Niagara (Regional
     Police Services Board), 2013 SCC 19 at para 46.

75. Unnecessarily revisiting these findings further restricts the public from participating after the public was
     able to participate in the ABEBC process, and improperly gives dissatisfied advocates of greater
     hybridization in these cities a second "bite at the cherry": see Danyluk v Ainsworth Technologies Inc,
     2001 SCC 44 ["Danyluk"] at para 18. This offends basic principles of fair play: one doesn't get a "do-over"
     if one is unhappy with a commission's recommendations by instead opting into a less independent, less
     consultative process. This Panel cannot act as an unofficial appellate body of the ABEBC's decisions and
     readjudicate them. Parties disappointed with the decisions of an administrative process are not allowed to
     try to make those arguments again before a subsequent body: Danyluk.

76. If the ABEBC's determinations declining to further hybridize Calgary and Lethbridge were truly
     unreasonable, the legislature could reject parts of the ABEBC Majority Report at its own constitutional
     risk. It has not yet clearly done so. Alternatively, they could be judicially reviewed in the Court of King's
     Bench, which is the proper procedure when electoral boundaries commissions' decisions are unreasonable:
     Fédération Acadienne.
