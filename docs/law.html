Alberta Electoral Boundary Audit

What the Law Says About an Electoral Map

A plain-language guide · Not legal advice

An electoral map divides the province into ridings. Each riding elects one MLA. This audit measures maps. Courts judge them. Those are two different jobs. This page sets out what Canadian law asks of a map, as I understand it, and where my evidence fits. The audit itself takes no position on any legal conclusion. It reports what the data show, and it stops there.

Why the audit never calls a map a gerrymander

On this site, "gerrymandering" has its everyday meaning: drawing lines to give a party an advantage. As I understand Canadian law, the word has no legal definition. No law forbids "gerrymandering" by name, and no court test uses the word. So a court cannot find a map guilty of it. That is one reason the audit never calls any map a gerrymander, whatever the numbers show.

Most of the audit's statistical tools come from American court fights. One is the efficiency gap, which compares the votes each party wastes. Another is a large set of computer-drawn maps to compare a real map against. In the United States, partisan gerrymandering is at least a named legal claim. But in Rucho v. Common Cause, 588 U.S. 684 (2019), the US Supreme Court closed the federal courts to it. It held that federal judges have no clear, workable standard for judging such claims. Some US state courts still use the same measures. The best-known case is League of Women Voters v. Commonwealth, 178 A.3d 737 (Pa. 2018).

Canada took a different path. Instead of a gerrymandering test, Canadian law asks two questions a court can answer. Does the map give voters effective representation under section 3 of the Charter? And did the commission follow the law that governs it? My findings are evidence a court could weigh on those two questions. They do not answer either one. Statistics also cannot read minds. The lines can show what a map does, never what the people who drew it intended.

The Charter test: effective representation

Section 3 of the Charter of Rights and Freedoms gives every citizen the right to vote. The leading case on maps is Reference re Provincial Electoral Boundaries (Saskatchewan), [1991] 2 SCR 158. This site calls it the Saskatchewan Reference. In it, the Supreme Court of Canada held that when ridings are drawn, section 3 protects effective representation. It does not demand that every riding hold exactly the same number of people.

Justice McLachlin wrote for the majority of the Court. Roughly equal voting power comes first, she explained. But it can give way to other things: geography, community history, shared local interests, and the voice of minority groups. The Court set no fixed limit on how far a riding may stray from the average. A riding may be bigger or smaller than average if those other reasons justify it.

No government can switch this right off. The notwithstanding clause lets a legislature set some Charter rights aside for a time. It does not apply to section 3.

As I read the case law, two things follow. Both are reasons not to read too much into my numbers:

  • A lean toward one party is not enough, on its own, to make a map unconstitutional. In the cases reviewed in the audit's academic report, a lean had to come with something more. That could be gaps in riding population that geography or community do not justify. Or it could be evidence that those reasons were used as cover for a party's gain, not for their real purpose. That is why the audit also tests how a map's lines are drawn: how much riding sizes vary, how cities are split, and the boundaries the commission's majority flagged.
  • Courts defer to the people who draw the map. They give legislatures and commissions a lot of room. A court asks whether reasonable people, applying the right principles, could have drawn these lines. It does not look for the best possible map. The closest case on community ties is Raîche v. Canada (Attorney General), 2004 FC 679. There the Federal Court found no breach of section 3. It did find that the federal commission had broken the federal boundaries law's duty to respect communities of interest. It set aside the commission's decision for the ridings in dispute and delayed its ruling by a year, and Parliament made the fix. That was a lower-court ruling on a federal law. An Alberta court may find it persuasive, but it does not have to follow it.

How a map is made can matter too

Courts have also looked at how a map gets made. In 2017 Nova Scotia's Court of Appeal found that the province's map broke section 3. The government had told its independent commission to change course, so the final map did not reflect the commission's own view. The court added that a legislature may still draw a map itself.

To protect ridings in the Gaspésie, a Quebec law paused the province's commission. It passed unanimously. That did not save it. In 2026 the Supreme Court of Canada struck it down (Quebec (AG) v. Lalande, 2026 SCC 13, decided 7 to 2, reasons May 1). Quebec could have protected those ridings in a narrower way. On June 12, 2026, Quebec's National Assembly passed its own 127-seat map.

Where does the audit fit? I compared each real map with 1,010,000 maps a computer drew from the same voting areas, without ever seeing a vote. A court could weigh those comparisons when it asks about effective representation. So could my other measurements: how population varies from riding to riding, and where lines leave town and city limits. No score, and no mix of scores, decides the legal test. When I report that several measures point the same way, that describes a pattern in the data. It is not a legal conclusion.

Alberta's law: the Electoral Boundaries Commission Act

Alberta's commissions work under the Electoral Boundaries Commission Act, RSA 2000, c. E-3. This page calls it the Act. The Act calls a riding an electoral division.

The rules in brief

The Act sets these rules for the commission:

  • Who sits. Cabinet chooses the chair from a list of eligible roles that includes judges. The Speaker appoints four members who are not MLAs: two nominated by the Premier and two by the Opposition.
  • Hearings. It must hold public hearings before its draft map, and again after.
  • Deadlines. It has seven months to produce a draft map, then five more for its final report.
  • Seats. 91. The Act said 89 when the commission drew its maps. Bill 32, introduced April 23, 2026, raised the number to 91.
  • Population. Every riding has to sit within 25% of the average. Up to four remote ridings may go as low as 50% below average, if the commission finds that at least three of five tests are met. Both rules are explained below.
  • Factors. The commission must consider effective representation. It may also weigh growth, communities of interest, geography and other factors.

Both commission maps fit inside these rules. Two of the rules need a closer look: the population band, and the exception for remote ridings.

The population band. As a general rule, each riding's population must be within 25% of the provincial average, above or below. Both of the commission's 2026 maps meet that rule. Apart from the three ridings each map protects under the remote-riding exception, every riding sits inside the band. The furthest above average, on the commission's minority map, is 24.06% over: within a point of the limit, but not past it. Inside that wide band, the commission has a lot of freedom. My population findings describe choices made inside the legal band. One is that riding sizes on the minority map are spread 48% wider than on the majority map, on the commission's own tables. Another is the crowding of north-east and central Calgary. On 2021 census counts, the average gap from the ideal size comes out almost the same on all three maps. Whether a map is legal and whether it is unusual are different questions.

The remote-riding exception, section 15(2). Up to four ridings may have as many as 50% fewer people than average. The commission must be of the opinion that at least three of these five tests are met:

  • an area over 20,000 km² (or a surveyed area over 15,000 km²);
  • more than 150 km from the Legislature Building to the riding's nearest boundary, by the most direct highway route;
  • no town in the riding with more than 8,000 people;
  • an Indian reserve or a Métis settlement in the riding;
  • part of the riding's boundary runs along Alberta's border.

The exception is a choice, not a duty. Meeting the tests makes a riding eligible. It never forces the commission to use the exception. Each of the commission's 2026 maps uses it for three ridings. I checked all six against the tests in the Act and found every one eligible. So the dispute is not about eligibility. It is about how one riding was drawn. The minority's Rocky Mountain House–Banff Park riding meets one test, the provincial-border one, only because it runs through national-park land where no one lives. The commission's own final report (March 23, 2026, p. 10) records what the majority commissioners said about that choice:

"They propose to retain an electoral division of 'Rocky Mountain House-Banff Park' by artificially extending its boundary to the province's western border with British Columbia (taking part of Banff National Park, where no one lives), which is a bad faith effort to ensure it can be protected under s. 15(2) of the Act."

Those words, "bad faith" among them, belong to the three majority commissioners, the chair among them. They wrote them in an official report that the Act requires. They are not the audit's words. My own check found that the riding qualifies for the exception even without the park land. Quoting the report records what it says. It does not record what any commissioner intended.

What a commission report can and cannot do. Under the Act, a commission's report is not law by itself. The lines change only when the Legislative Assembly passes a separate bill, an Electoral Divisions Act. In Quebec a commission's map takes effect without the National Assembly's final approval. In Alberta, as in most provinces, it takes a bill, so the governing party decides whether any commission map becomes law. The Act's rules on hearings, deadlines and the 25% band are addressed to the commission. As I read it, nothing in the Act governs what the Assembly does instead. It sets no minimum notice and no period for public comment.

What the law does not require

On April 21, 2026, Government Motion 37 set both commission maps aside. It handed the work to a committee of six MLAs, four of them from the governing party, with a report due by November 2, 2026. The committee then appointed an advisory panel to draw the map. The motion gave the panel the Act's population rules and its list of factors, nearly word for word: the 25% band, the exception for up to four remote ridings, and effective representation first. It left out the hearings. The review was to be expedited, which means done quickly, with no requirement to hold public hearings (Votes and Proceedings, April 21, 2026). As for the Act, most of its rules are addressed to "the Commission". Read plainly, they do not bind the Legislature or a committee of MLAs.

  • I found no provision that requires a committee of MLAs to hold hearings, be independent, publish a draft or explain its choices.
  • Recommendation 5, the one recommendation the government cited, binds no one.
  • Elections Alberta says it needs at least a year and a half to prepare for new ridings. That is a planning need. No law makes it a deadline.

Does the size of a majority matter? On 2023 election-day ballots, the commission's minority map gives the UCP 60 of 89 seats, a two-thirds majority. On all 2023 ballots, the registered count gives 57, which is not. The older rule for placing early ballots, kept as a check, gives 55. (Where early ballots belong inside the new ridings is an estimate.) In Alberta, two-thirds unlocks no special power. The Assembly decides every question by a simple majority of the votes cast. A simple majority can already pass laws and budgets. It can put a time limit on debate, change the rules of the House, and set every committee's membership, which the rules keep in proportion to seats. Conventions, the unwritten rules of politics, need no supermajority either: a simple majority set aside the practice of leaving the map to an independent commission. A majority that size would give a government a cushion. A few MLAs crossing the floor could not threaten it. And a smaller opposition would have fewer members to keep up the work on committees.

Alberta handed the map to MLAs once before, in 1992

In 1992 Alberta's commission could not agree. Its final report was five separate reports, one from each commissioner. In July 1992 the Assembly passed Motion 24 and handed the work to a Select Special Committee of MLAs chaired by Bob Bogle. The Opposition refused to name members, so four Progressive Conservative MLAs drew the map. Alberta voted on it in the 1993 election. The 2026 commission did not deadlock. A majority signed its report, and the report was set aside anyway.

In 1994 the Court of Appeal let the 1992 map stand. I have not read the judgment itself, so this account of it comes from others. The 2026 commission's report says the court found that the map did not rise to the level of Charter condemnation, but called for “a gradual and steady” change (final report, Appendix D, p. 282). Other published accounts add that the court criticised how the map was made. The committee's members could not explain several of its boundaries. One of the smallest ridings was Bogle's own. And a proper review was needed before the Legislature's term ran out. In 1995 the Legislature amended the Act and changed how commissions are appointed. Alberta used that process from then until 2026.

If the new map is challenged

Courts do not review a law while it is being made. Once a new map becomes law, someone can challenge it in court. It would face the same test as any map: effective representation, with every population gap justified. As I read the law, Canada has no rule against partisan advantage as such. The question is whether voters get effective representation. The commission's minority map shows how far apart the two can be. None of its choices breaks the Act's number rules, and the Act allows splitting cities. The commission's majority argued that the map would likely fail the Charter test. No court has ruled on that.

The full legal analysis is in the academic report, sections 2 and 5.9, in the audit repository. It includes the review of case law, the writing on how courts defer to commissions, and a comparison with how other provinces make their maps.

The audit's standing rule

Every version of this audit follows the same rule. It may report what the tests show. It may say how unusual a map is next to maps a computer drew with no aim. It does not call any map a gerrymander. It does not guess at intent from the shape of the lines. And it does not rule on whether a map meets the Constitution or the Act. Those questions belong to courts, commissions and readers, with the audit's evidence in front of them.
